# Pantas Level 4 lesson audit, packet L4-4464

## Your job

You audit one lesson of the Pantas exam review app against its lesson standards.
You report findings. You change nothing.

- This packet holds everything you need: the rules, the standards text, the
  lesson, and the lessons it depends on.
- Judge the lesson only from this packet. Do not open any other file, packet,
  issue, or pull request in any repository, apart from the relay instructions
  your assignment names. Another reader is checking the same lesson separately,
  and the audit depends on the two of you not seeing each other's work.
- Every verdict is yours. Do not ask the user anything.
- You finish by handing in one form. Your chat reply is not read by anyone.

## How to judge

Work through the rules in the table below, one at a time, against the lesson
between the `LESSON START` and `LESSON END` markers.

1. **Read the lesson in full first**, then the prerequisite lessons.
2. **Give every rule in the form a verdict:** `pass`, `fail`, or
   `not_applicable`. Rule L11 is the one exception: use `needs_source_check` if
   the lesson contains Filipino text, or `not_applicable` if it does not. You
   cannot confirm Filipino spelling against the official source from here.
3. **A fail needs evidence.** Each finding has a `quote` copied exactly from the
   lesson (at least 8 characters, from between the markers, no paraphrase and no
   ellipsis) and a `problem` saying what breaks the rule and why. When something
   is missing, quote the place where it was needed.
4. **A pass on a Blocker or Major rule needs proof.** In `checked`, list what
   you checked in this lesson, concretely. For L17, name each formula an example
   uses and where Core Concept introduces it. For B02, give your own answer to
   each worked example. "Looks fine" is not proof.
5. **`not_applicable` needs a `note`** saying why the rule cannot apply here.
6. **Solve before you read.** For B02, work each example from its question
   alone, then compare with the lesson's solution.
7. **Explanation reuse (L25, L26, L27).** A term, concept, or condition counts
   as explained if this lesson explains it, if a prerequisite lesson below
   teaches it and this lesson gives a short reminder or reference, or if this
   lesson clearly points to its own later section and gives enough meaning to
   follow until then. A mere earlier mention is not an explanation.
8. **Learning goal (L01).** The goal is in the Level 3 record below. Judge
   whether the lesson serves it.
9. **Script candidates.** If the form includes B07, B08, B09, B10, L13, or L16,
   a script flagged lines for you under "Script candidates". Decide which
   flagged lines truly break the rule. Subject-matter uses do not: "marks" as
   punctuation, a playing card, the stability of an atom.
10. **Judge the rule as written.** Do not invent stricter rules, and do not
    excuse a clear breach. If you are unsure, say so in the `problem` or the
    `checked` text and choose the verdict the evidence supports best.

### Illustration review

Separately from the rules, judge the lesson's illustrations. Add one entry to
`illustrations` for each case:

- `missing`: a figure would improve understanding and none is there.
- `insufficient`: a placeholder or figure exists but does not do the job.
- `unneeded_existing`: an existing figure or placeholder is decorative.

For each entry give `position_quote` (exact lesson text at the spot),
`description` (one line an artist could draw from), `why` (how it helps the
student), `priority` (`high`, `medium`, `low`), `method` (`code` for a figure
that can be drawn in code, `library` for a public-domain or open-licensed image,
`table_or_formula` when a table or formula would serve better than an image),
and `kind` (`exact_exam_figure` when the figure is what an exam question shows,
`concept` otherwise). Do not recommend decoration. An empty list is a valid
answer.

### Accuracy concerns

If you notice a fact or an answer that looks wrong, also add it to
`accuracy_concerns` with an exact `quote` and what looks wrong. An empty list is
a valid answer.

## Standards text: lesson style guide

# Lesson Style Guide

Distilled from Aedwon's hand-reviewed lessons (see
`content/lessons/mathematics/`). Every generated lesson must follow this.
When in doubt, imitate `arithmetic-number-sense/order-of-operations.md`.

Facts are sourced per [source-policy.md](source-policy.md). Before submitting,
check the lesson against [content-checklist.md](content-checklist.md); a lesson
passes only with no blocker and no major finding.

## Learning goal

Every lesson has a learning goal: one or two sentences on what a student can do
after it and which exam requirement that serves. Record it in the audit records
as [source-policy.md](source-policy.md) describes, not in the frontmatter.
Everything in the lesson should serve the goal.

## Teaching completeness

- Explain necessary terms, symbols, and units before students need their
  meanings to understand the teaching or use a method.
- Explain what each concept means and how it works. A name or formula alone
  is not an explanation.
- State the relevant conditions, limitations, and exceptions of a rule,
  formula, or shortcut so students know when it applies.

For these three requirements, reuse explanations actually taught in an
identified prerequisite lesson with a short reminder or reference rather than
repeating the full explanation. If the current lesson develops an explanation
later, clearly point to that section and give any meaning needed to follow
the intervening teaching. A prior mention alone does not count as teaching.
If neither source supplies the explanation, it is a teaching gap. The current
lesson must still teach its own learning goal; prerequisite references cannot
replace that teaching. Record prerequisite evidence in the audit records.

## Voice

- Very simple English a Grade 5 student can read. Short sentences. No
  filler, no long introductions. Every sentence teaches something.
  Filipino-language lessons follow the Filipino section below.
- Explain exam verbs in one line the first time they appear:
  `"Evaluate" means find the final number answer.`
- Friendly-tutor analogies are welcome in Core Concept (building a house,
  twins holding hands), one per lesson at most.
- Example questions use authentic exam wording and difficulty. Solutions
  return to simple English.
- In-lesson quizzes follow the quiz rules: realistic target-exam language and
  plausible wrong choices based on reasoning errors or misconceptions. The Grade 5
  benchmark governs the teaching prose, not these questions.
- Mention CETs when it motivates: why the topic is tested, what the trick
  usually is. Vary how you say it — do not open every trap with
  "Test-makers often…".

## Filipino-language lessons

Lessons that teach Filipino grammar, vocabulary, or reading (Aedwon,
4 October 2026):

- Write the teaching prose in simple Filipino, held to the same Grade 5 standard
  as English lessons: short sentences, common words, one idea per sentence.
- Give a hard grammar term a short English gloss the first time it appears:
  `**pang-abay** (adverb)`. After that, use the Filipino term alone.
- Example sentences, examples, and in-lesson quiz items are in Filipino.
- Spelling and usage follow the Komisyon sa Wikang Filipino, including the
  Ortograpiyang Pambansa. Source contested rules (nang and ng, din and rin,
  affixes) per [source-policy.md](source-policy.md).
- No Taglish in teaching prose. Use an English word only when it is the common
  term students and the exam use.
- There is no numeric readability score. English formulas misjudge Filipino
  text, and Filipino-specific models are still research tools. Readability is
  judged by the rules above and by a reader check at the student's level.

## Emphasis and de-AI rules

These keep lessons from reading as machine-written. Apply everywhere.

- **Never use ALL-CAPS for emphasis** (`the WHOLE paragraph`, `build
  PROTEINS`). Use **bold** for a key term the first time it is defined,
  and *italics* rarely for light stress. Capitalize only real acronyms
  (DNA, ATP, GCF) and proper nouns.
- **Target words in a question are bold**, and the prompt says "the word
  in bold" (never "the underlined word" — markdown cannot underline).
- **No em dashes.** Use a comma, colon, or separate sentence.
- **Avoid stock AI phrasing:** "it's important to note", "delve",
  "crucial", "plays a vital role", "furthermore/moreover", and the
  "not just X but Y" construction.
- **Vary sentence openings and rhythm.** Do not start three sentences in a
  row the same way; mix short and medium sentences instead of a uniform
  staccato.
- **Vary the summary.** The frontmatter `summary` should describe the
  lesson plainly; do not begin every lesson with "This lesson teaches
  you…".

## Section structure

```
## Core Concept
## Mnemonics & Memory Aids     ← OPTIONAL, see below
## Examples
## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts
## Traps to Watch For
```

- Exactly these titles ("Core Concept", not "The Core Concept";
  "Examples", not "Worked Examples").
- Mnemonics & Memory Aids is included ONLY when a genuinely known or
  natural mnemonic exists (PEMDAS sentences, SOH-CAH-TOA). If a mnemonic
  would sound forced or invented-for-the-sake-of-it, DROP the whole
  section. Do not fabricate cringe acronyms.
- An extra topical `##` section is allowed when the topic really needs it
  (e.g. "When to List Multiples" in divisibility rules) — sparingly.
- Sub-topics from the outline's required sections become `###` headings
  inside the appropriate section (usually Core Concept or Examples).

### Per-subject section fit

The five sections above were derived from Mathematics. The app renders
section titles generically, so other subjects may adapt them:

- **Mathematics:** keep the five sections. Every formula used in a
  solution must first appear in **Core Concept** — never introduce a
  formula for the first time inside an example.
- **Science (conceptual: biology, earth science):** "Speed Tricks &
  Shortcuts" may be titled **"Exam Strategies"** when the content is
  test strategy, not calculation. A **"Key Terms"** section is allowed
  for term-dense lessons (taxonomy, cell biology, genetics).
- **Science (quantitative: physics, parts of chemistry):** keep "Speed
  Tricks & Shortcuts"; the formula-in-Core-Concept rule applies here too.
- **Language Proficiency:** "Speed Tricks & Shortcuts" may be titled
  **"Common Confusions"** or **"Usage Notes"**.
- **Reading Comprehension:** "Speed Tricks & Shortcuts" may be titled
  **"Reading Strategies"**.
- **Reasoning:** use a reduced layout — **Core Concept → Examples →
  Traps to Watch For** — and drop Speed Tricks/Mnemonics, which rarely
  apply. Every example is figure-driven (see Illustration placeholders).

## Examples section

- Each example: `### #N: Short Descriptive Title` (e.g.
  `### #2: Nested Grouping Symbols`).
- Cover every distinct exam-relevant scenario, variation, and edge case
  within the lesson's learning goal, with one worked example per distinct
  case. Changing only the numbers does not create a new scenario.
- Order examples from basic to authentic target-exam difficulty.
  Let coverage determine the number of examples; avoid near-duplicates.
- Worked-example questions follow the question guide's
  [Problem information and expected knowledge](question-style-guide.md#problem-information-and-expected-knowledge)
  rule: supply necessary problem-specific givens, without supplying formulas,
  definitions, or methods students are expected to know or select. This does
  not restrict explanations in Core Concept or the worked solution.
- Body: question sentence with inline math, then `**Solution:**` on its
  own line, then numbered steps (`1.`, `2.` markdown list), with each
  key computation as a `$$ ... $$` display line between/after steps.
- Keep each display equation narrow enough for a phone screen.

## Traps section

- Each trap: `### #N: Short Title`, then a blockquote one-line warning,
  then a short paragraph explaining the trick and the wrong answer the
  test-makers plant.

## Math notation

- Use `\dfrac` for top-level fractions. Use `\frac` only inside another fraction.
- Every number in prose is wrapped in inline math, even isolated ones:
  `the $2$ in $3^2$`, `The correct answer is $25$.` Same for operators
  referenced in prose: `plus ($+$)`.
- Inline math `$...$`; display math `$$...$$` on its own line.
- Never `\( \)`, `\[ \]`, or code fences for math. `\cancel{}` is allowed.

## Formatting rules

- Allowed: `##`/`###` headings, paragraphs (1–3 short sentences), bold for
  key terms, italics sparingly, blockquotes, bullet lists (sparingly) and
  numbered lists (solution steps), inline/display LaTeX.
- NEVER: tables, links, code blocks, or horizontal rules.
- Follow the assigned workstream's current illustration contract and supported
  image format. Do not infer that images are unavailable from this older guide.
  Placeholders below are authoring notes, not completed illustrations.

## Illustration placeholders

Wherever a figure, diagram, or picture would genuinely help learning
(geometry figures, graphs, cell diagrams, figure-series patterns, gear
setups), insert an HTML comment at the exact spot the image belongs:

```
<!-- ILLUSTRATION: labeled right triangle with legs 3 and 4, hypotenuse x -->
```

- One line, specific enough that an artist could draw it without reading
  the whole lesson.
- Invisible in the app and the preview; greppable later with
  `grep -rn "ILLUSTRATION:" content/lessons`.
- Put a placeholder in **every example whose question depends on a figure**,
  not just one per lesson. A figure-based example needs its own placeholder
  in its own block.
- Usually the surrounding text must still teach without the image (describe
  the figure in words) — the illustration upgrades the lesson.
- **Load-bearing exception:** in Reasoning (figure series, grouping,
  matrices, paper folding, mental rotation, gears, pulleys, levers) and in
  Reading Comprehension's visual-media lesson, the figure *is* the
  question. There the placeholder is required and the prose cannot fully
  substitute for the image; still describe enough that the example is not
  a blank.

## Standards text: expected knowledge in example questions (rule L05)

## Problem information and expected knowledge

Provide the problem-specific facts, values, conditions, and referenced
material needed for a determinate answer. Include necessary units and state
rounding or approximation requirements when they affect the answer. A
referenced passage, figure, table, or marked word must be available with the
question when it is shown to the student.

Do not supply formulas, definitions, or solution methods that students are
expected to know or select. Use taught content and source-backed exam
requirements to identify that expected knowledge. Include a formula only when
the target exam supplies it or recalling it is outside the skill being tested.
Record the reason for that exception in the audit or authoring records.

For example, a simple-interest question supplies the principal, annual rate,
duration, and interest type. It does not supply the simple-interest formula
when recalling or selecting that formula is part of the tested skill.

This distinction applies to question stems, including worked-example stems
inside lessons. It does not prevent lesson teaching or answer solutions from
explaining formulas and methods. During the coverage audit, Level 4 checks
worked-example stems; Level 5 checks in-lesson quizzes and the question bank.


## Standards text: voice and math rules

## Voice, for in-app content and interface text

These rules govern the app. External marketing and website copy have separate
direction from Aedwon, who personally writes and approves website copy. Do not
apply these rules to that copy by default. See `WORKFLOW.md`.

- App interface text uses simple English and short sentences.
- **Lesson teaching content:** plain, simple English that a Grade 5 student can
  understand.
- **Quiz questions and answer choices:** realistic language for the target exam.
  This applies to Mock, normal quizzes, and in-lesson quizzes. Do not apply the
  lesson's Grade 5 language rule to those questions. Wrong choices must be
  plausible and reflect meaningful reasoning errors or misconceptions.
- **Filipino-language content** (Aedwon, 4 October 2026): lessons,
  explanations, and wrong-choice notes that teach or test Filipino use simple
  Filipino at the same Grade 5 standard instead of English. Quiz stems and
  choices use realistic exam Filipino. Rules:
  `docs/content/lesson-style-guide.md` and `docs/content/question-style-guide.md`.
- **Quiz answer explanations:** plain English a Grade 5 student can understand,
  including Mock, normal quizzes, and in-lesson quizzes. Explain the reasoning
  clearly without reducing the subject's accuracy. The explanation (shown to
  Free and Pro) says only why the correct answer is correct, with the worked
  solution when solving is involved. It never discusses wrong choices.
- **Wrong-choice notes (Pro):** every wrong choice carries its own note in the same
  plain English: the mistake it catches, why it looks right, and one check to avoid
  it next time. Rules: `docs/content/question-style-guide.md`.
- **No em dashes.** Use a comma or a colon.
- **American spelling.** practice, color, behavior, analyze, gray, center, organize.
  Never practise, colour, behaviour, analyse, grey, centre, organise. This holds in
  specs and prose as well as UI strings.
- Scores are **"points"**, never "marks". `mark` already means the answer dot, the
  flag pennant and the outcome mark on a reviewed question.
- **"Questions"**, never "cards".

## Core Concept

**Parallel lines** run in the same direction forever and never meet, like train rails. We write $l \parallel m$.

**Perpendicular lines** cross at exactly $90^\circ$. We write $l \perp m$.

A **transversal** is a line that cuts across two or more lines. When a transversal cuts two parallel lines, it creates eight angles with beautiful rules.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "transversal", "labels": ["1", "2", "3", "4", "5", "6", "7", "8"], "semanticsLabel": "Two parallel lines cut by a transversal with angles 1 through 4 at top intersection and 5 through 8 at bottom intersection."} -->

The equal pairs:

- **Corresponding angles** sit in the same corner position at each crossing (like both "top left"). They are equal.
- **Alternate interior angles** sit between the parallel lines on opposite sides of the transversal, forming a letter Z. They are equal.
- **Alternate exterior angles** sit outside the parallel lines on opposite sides. They are equal.
- **Vertical angles** at each crossing are equal, as always.

The supplementary pair:

- **Same-side interior angles** (also called co-interior) sit between the parallel lines on the same side of the transversal, forming a letter C. They add up to $180^\circ$.

Here is the shortcut view: once you know one of the eight angles, you know all eight. Every angle is either equal to it or supplementary to it.

These rules also work in reverse. If a transversal makes equal corresponding angles, the two lines must be parallel. Test-makers use this to ask "which lines are parallel?"

## Examples

### #1: One Angle Unlocks All Eight

Lines $l$ and $m$ are parallel, cut by a transversal. One angle at the top crossing measures $65^\circ$. Find the measures of its linear pair neighbor, its corresponding angle, and its same-side interior angle.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "transversal", "labels": ["Line l", "Line m", "65°", "Linear pair: 115°", "Corresponding: 65°", "Same-side int: 115°"], "semanticsLabel": "Parallel lines l and m cut by a transversal with top angle 65 degrees and its linear pair, corresponding, and same-side interior angles."} -->


**Solution:**
Every angle in the figure is either $65^\circ$ or its supplement.

1. The supplement of $65^\circ$ is what completes a straight line.

$$180 - 65 = 115$$

2. The linear pair neighbor is $115^\circ$.
3. The corresponding angle at the bottom crossing is equal: $65^\circ$.
4. The same-side interior angle must add with $65^\circ$ to make $180^\circ$, so it is $115^\circ$.

### #2: Corresponding Angles with Algebra

Lines $p$ and $q$ are parallel. A transversal makes corresponding angles of $(2x + 30)^\circ$ and $(3x - 10)^\circ$. Find the measure of the angles.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "transversal", "labels": ["Line p", "Line q", "Corresponding: (2x + 30)°", "Corresponding: (3x - 10)°"], "semanticsLabel": "Parallel lines p and q cut by a transversal with corresponding angles labeled 2x plus 30 degrees and 3x minus 10 degrees."} -->


**Solution:**
Corresponding angles are equal, so set the expressions equal.

1. Write the equation.

$$2x + 30 = 3x - 10$$

2. Subtract $2x$ from both sides and add $10$ to both sides.

$$40 = x$$

3. Substitute back: $2(40) + 30 = 110$.
4. Check the other expression: $3(40) - 10 = 110$. Each angle is $110^\circ$.

### #3: Same-Side Interior Angles

Lines $a$ and $b$ are parallel. Two same-side interior angles measure $(3x + 20)^\circ$ and $(2x - 15)^\circ$. Find both angles.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "transversal", "labels": ["Line a", "Line b", "(3x + 20)°", "(2x - 15)°", "Sum = 180°"], "semanticsLabel": "Parallel lines a and b with same-side interior angles labeled 3x plus 20 degrees and 2x minus 15 degrees summing to 180 degrees."} -->


**Solution:**
Same-side interior angles are supplementary, so they add to $180^\circ$.

1. Write the sum equation.

$$(3x + 20) + (2x - 15) = 180$$

2. Combine like terms.

$$5x + 5 = 180$$

3. Subtract $5$, then divide by $5$.

$$5x = 175$$

$$x = 35$$

4. The angles are $3(35) + 20 = 125^\circ$ and $2(35) - 15 = 55^\circ$.
5. Check: $125 + 55 = 180$. Correct.

### #4: Which Rule Applies?

Lines $l$ and $m$ are parallel. An alternate interior angle pair measures $(4x)^\circ$ and $(3x + 18)^\circ$. Solve for $x$.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "transversal", "labels": ["Line l", "Line m", "Alt interior: (4x)°", "Alt interior: (3x + 18)°"], "semanticsLabel": "Parallel lines l and m cut by a transversal with equal alternate interior angles labeled 4x degrees and 3x plus 18 degrees."} -->


**Solution:**
First decide: equal pair or supplementary pair? Alternate interior angles form the letter Z and are equal.

1. Set the expressions equal.

$$4x = 3x + 18$$

2. Subtract $3x$ from both sides.

$$x = 18$$

3. Each angle is $4(18) = 72^\circ$. Check: $3(18) + 18 = 72$. Correct.

### #5: The Zigzag Between Parallel Lines

Lines $l$ and $m$ are parallel. Point $P$ sits between them. A segment from line $l$ to $P$ makes a $30^\circ$ angle with $l$, and a segment from $P$ to line $m$ makes a $40^\circ$ angle with $m$. Find the angle at $P$ between the two segments.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "parallel-zigzag", "labels": ["Line l", "Line m", "Point P", "30°", "40°", "Angle P = 30° + 40° = 70°"], "semanticsLabel": "Zigzag between parallel lines l and m with segments meeting at point P forming angles of 30 and 40 degrees with a parallel helper line."} -->


**Solution:**
This is a classic CET figure. The trick: draw a helper line through $P$ parallel to both lines.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "parallel-zigzag", "labels": ["Top Line", "Bottom Line", "Point P", "Helper Line through P"], "semanticsLabel": "Two parallel lines with point P between them and a dashed helper line parallel to both splitting the vertex angle."} -->

1. The helper line splits the angle at $P$ into two smaller angles.
2. The upper part and the $30^\circ$ angle are alternate interior angles (a letter Z with line $l$). So the upper part is $30^\circ$.
3. The lower part and the $40^\circ$ angle are alternate interior angles with line $m$. So the lower part is $40^\circ$.
4. Add the two parts.

$$30 + 40 = 70$$

5. The angle at $P$ is $70^\circ$. Remember the pattern: the bent angle equals the sum of the two given angles.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Every angle-pair question has only two possible relationships: equal or supplementary. Trace the shape with your eyes. A letter Z or F means equal ($Z$ for alternate interior, $F$ for corresponding). A letter C or U means supplementary (same-side interior).

Once you find one angle, do not re-derive the rest. All eight angles are just two numbers: $x$ and $180 - x$, alternating around each crossing.

For the zigzag figure (Example $5$), skip the helper-line work on exam day: the middle angle is simply the sum of the two outer angles. If they give the middle angle and one outer angle, subtract.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: The Lines Might Not Be Parallel

> The angle rules only work when the lines are parallel.

If the problem never says the lines are parallel (in words or with matching arrow marks in the figure), you cannot use corresponding or alternate angle rules. Test-makers draw lines that look parallel and hope you assume. Check the given information first.

### #2: Equal or Supplementary?

> Same-side interior angles are not equal. They add to $180^\circ$.

The most common wrong equation is setting same-side interior angles equal to each other. If both expressions sit between the parallel lines on the same side of the transversal (the letter C), write a sum equation, not an equal equation.

### #3: Corresponding vs. Alternate Confusion

> Corresponding angles are on the same side of the transversal; alternate angles are on opposite sides.

Both pairs are equal when lines are parallel, so for computation the mix-up may not hurt. But CETs also ask you to name the relationship, and "corresponding" vs. "alternate interior" is exactly the kind of one-point gift you should not miss.
