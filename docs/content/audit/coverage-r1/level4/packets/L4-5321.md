# Pantas Level 4 lesson audit, packet L4-5321

## Your job

You audit one lesson of the Pantas exam review app against its lesson standards.
You report findings. You change nothing.

- This packet holds everything you need: the rules, the standards text, the
  lesson, and the lessons it depends on.
- Judge the lesson only from this packet. Do not open any other file, packet,
  issue, or pull request in any repository, apart from the relay instructions
  your assignment names. Another reader is checking the same lesson separately,
  and the audit depends on the two of you not seeing each other's work.
- Every verdict is yours. Do not ask the user anything.
- You finish by handing in one form. Your chat reply is not read by anyone.

## How to judge

Work through the rules in the table below, one at a time, against the lesson
between the `LESSON START` and `LESSON END` markers.

1. **Read the lesson in full first**, then the prerequisite lessons.
2. **Give every rule in the form a verdict:** `pass`, `fail`, or
   `not_applicable`. Rule L11 is the one exception: use `needs_source_check` if
   the lesson contains Filipino text, or `not_applicable` if it does not. You
   cannot confirm Filipino spelling against the official source from here.
3. **A fail needs evidence.** Each finding has a `quote` copied exactly from the
   lesson (at least 8 characters, from between the markers, no paraphrase and no
   ellipsis) and a `problem` saying what breaks the rule and why. When something
   is missing, quote the place where it was needed.
4. **A pass on a Blocker or Major rule needs proof.** In `checked`, list what
   you checked in this lesson, concretely. For L17, name each formula an example
   uses and where Core Concept introduces it. For B02, give your own answer to
   each worked example. "Looks fine" is not proof.
5. **`not_applicable` needs a `note`** saying why the rule cannot apply here.
6. **Solve before you read.** For B02, work each example from its question
   alone, then compare with the lesson's solution.
7. **Explanation reuse (L25, L26, L27).** A term, concept, or condition counts
   as explained if this lesson explains it, if a prerequisite lesson below
   teaches it and this lesson gives a short reminder or reference, or if this
   lesson clearly points to its own later section and gives enough meaning to
   follow until then. A mere earlier mention is not an explanation.
8. **Learning goal (L01).** The goal is in the Level 3 record below. Judge
   whether the lesson serves it.
9. **Script candidates.** If the form includes B07, B08, B09, B10, L13, or L16,
   a script flagged lines for you under "Script candidates". Decide which
   flagged lines truly break the rule. Subject-matter uses do not: "marks" as
   punctuation, a playing card, the stability of an atom.
10. **Judge the rule as written.** Do not invent stricter rules, and do not
    excuse a clear breach. If you are unsure, say so in the `problem` or the
    `checked` text and choose the verdict the evidence supports best.

### Illustration review

Separately from the rules, judge the lesson's illustrations. Add one entry to
`illustrations` for each case:

- `missing`: a figure would improve understanding and none is there.
- `insufficient`: a placeholder or figure exists but does not do the job.
- `unneeded_existing`: an existing figure or placeholder is decorative.

For each entry give `position_quote` (exact lesson text at the spot),
`description` (one line an artist could draw from), `why` (how it helps the
student), `priority` (`high`, `medium`, `low`), `method` (`code` for a figure
that can be drawn in code, `library` for a public-domain or open-licensed image,
`table_or_formula` when a table or formula would serve better than an image),
and `kind` (`exact_exam_figure` when the figure is what an exam question shows,
`concept` otherwise). Do not recommend decoration. An empty list is a valid
answer.

### Accuracy concerns

If you notice a fact or an answer that looks wrong, also add it to
`accuracy_concerns` with an exact `quote` and what looks wrong. An empty list is
a valid answer.

- Every `problem` and every accuracy `concern` is at least 20 characters.
- Every `note` and every `checked` item that counts as proof is at least 15

- Empty values are `[]` and `""`, never `null`.
- A rule that passes or is not applicable has no findings.
- Do not reuse one `checked` text across several rules. Say what you checked for
  each rule.

Make no other relay request. Do not edit, create, or delete any other file.

## Standards text: lesson style guide

# Lesson Style Guide

Distilled from Aedwon's hand-reviewed lessons (see
`content/lessons/mathematics/`). Every generated lesson must follow this.
When in doubt, imitate `arithmetic-number-sense/order-of-operations.md`.

Facts are sourced per [source-policy.md](source-policy.md). Before submitting,
check the lesson against [content-checklist.md](content-checklist.md); a lesson
passes only with no blocker and no major finding.

## Learning goal

Every lesson has a learning goal: one or two sentences on what a student can do
after it and which exam requirement that serves. Record it in the audit records
as [source-policy.md](source-policy.md) describes, not in the frontmatter.
Everything in the lesson should serve the goal.

## Teaching completeness

- Explain necessary terms, symbols, and units before students need their
  meanings to understand the teaching or use a method.
- Explain what each concept means and how it works. A name or formula alone
  is not an explanation.
- State the relevant conditions, limitations, and exceptions of a rule,
  formula, or shortcut so students know when it applies.

For these three requirements, reuse explanations actually taught in an
identified prerequisite lesson with a short reminder or reference rather than
repeating the full explanation. If the current lesson develops an explanation
later, clearly point to that section and give any meaning needed to follow
the intervening teaching. A prior mention alone does not count as teaching.
If neither source supplies the explanation, it is a teaching gap. The current
lesson must still teach its own learning goal; prerequisite references cannot
replace that teaching. Record prerequisite evidence in the audit records.

## Voice

- Very simple English a Grade 5 student can read. Short sentences. No
  filler, no long introductions. Every sentence teaches something.
  Filipino-language lessons follow the Filipino section below.
- Explain exam verbs in one line the first time they appear:
  `"Evaluate" means find the final number answer.`
- Friendly-tutor analogies are welcome in Core Concept (building a house,
  twins holding hands), one per lesson at most.
- Example questions use authentic exam wording and difficulty. Solutions
  return to simple English.
- In-lesson quizzes follow the quiz rules: realistic target-exam language and
  plausible wrong choices based on reasoning errors or misconceptions. The Grade 5
  benchmark governs the teaching prose, not these questions.
- Mention CETs when it motivates: why the topic is tested, what the trick
  usually is. Vary how you say it — do not open every trap with
  "Test-makers often…".

## Filipino-language lessons

Lessons that teach Filipino grammar, vocabulary, or reading (Aedwon,
4 October 2026):

- Write the teaching prose in simple Filipino, held to the same Grade 5 standard
  as English lessons: short sentences, common words, one idea per sentence.
- Give a hard grammar term a short English gloss the first time it appears:
  `**pang-abay** (adverb)`. After that, use the Filipino term alone.
- Example sentences, examples, and in-lesson quiz items are in Filipino.
- Spelling and usage follow the Komisyon sa Wikang Filipino, including the
  Ortograpiyang Pambansa. Source contested rules (nang and ng, din and rin,
  affixes) per [source-policy.md](source-policy.md).
- No Taglish in teaching prose. Use an English word only when it is the common
  term students and the exam use.
- There is no numeric readability score. English formulas misjudge Filipino
  text, and Filipino-specific models are still research tools. Readability is
  judged by the rules above and by a reader check at the student's level.

## Emphasis and de-AI rules

These keep lessons from reading as machine-written. Apply everywhere.

- **Never use ALL-CAPS for emphasis** (`the WHOLE paragraph`, `build
  PROTEINS`). Use **bold** for a key term the first time it is defined,
  and *italics* rarely for light stress. Capitalize only real acronyms
  (DNA, ATP, GCF) and proper nouns.
- **Target words in a question are bold**, and the prompt says "the word
  in bold" (never "the underlined word" — markdown cannot underline).
- **No em dashes.** Use a comma, colon, or separate sentence.
- **Avoid stock AI phrasing:** "it's important to note", "delve",
  "crucial", "plays a vital role", "furthermore/moreover", and the
  "not just X but Y" construction.
- **Vary sentence openings and rhythm.** Do not start three sentences in a
  row the same way; mix short and medium sentences instead of a uniform
  staccato.
- **Vary the summary.** The frontmatter `summary` should describe the
  lesson plainly; do not begin every lesson with "This lesson teaches
  you…".

## Section structure

```
## Core Concept
## Mnemonics & Memory Aids     ← OPTIONAL, see below
## Examples
## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts
## Traps to Watch For
```

- Exactly these titles ("Core Concept", not "The Core Concept";
  "Examples", not "Worked Examples").
- Mnemonics & Memory Aids is included ONLY when a genuinely known or
  natural mnemonic exists (PEMDAS sentences, SOH-CAH-TOA). If a mnemonic
  would sound forced or invented-for-the-sake-of-it, DROP the whole
  section. Do not fabricate cringe acronyms.
- An extra topical `##` section is allowed when the topic really needs it
  (e.g. "When to List Multiples" in divisibility rules) — sparingly.
- Sub-topics from the outline's required sections become `###` headings
  inside the appropriate section (usually Core Concept or Examples).

### Per-subject section fit

The five sections above were derived from Mathematics. The app renders
section titles generically, so other subjects may adapt them:

- **Mathematics:** keep the five sections. Every formula used in a
  solution must first appear in **Core Concept** — never introduce a
  formula for the first time inside an example.
- **Science (conceptual: biology, earth science):** "Speed Tricks &
  Shortcuts" may be titled **"Exam Strategies"** when the content is
  test strategy, not calculation. A **"Key Terms"** section is allowed
  for term-dense lessons (taxonomy, cell biology, genetics).
- **Science (quantitative: physics, parts of chemistry):** keep "Speed
  Tricks & Shortcuts"; the formula-in-Core-Concept rule applies here too.
- **Language Proficiency:** "Speed Tricks & Shortcuts" may be titled
  **"Common Confusions"** or **"Usage Notes"**.
- **Reading Comprehension:** "Speed Tricks & Shortcuts" may be titled
  **"Reading Strategies"**.
- **Reasoning:** use a reduced layout — **Core Concept → Examples →
  Traps to Watch For** — and drop Speed Tricks/Mnemonics, which rarely
  apply. Every example is figure-driven (see Illustration placeholders).

## Examples section

- Each example: `### #N: Short Descriptive Title` (e.g.
  `### #2: Nested Grouping Symbols`).
- Cover every distinct exam-relevant scenario, variation, and edge case
  within the lesson's learning goal, with one worked example per distinct
  case. Changing only the numbers does not create a new scenario.
- Order examples from basic to authentic target-exam difficulty.
  Let coverage determine the number of examples; avoid near-duplicates.
- Worked-example questions follow the question guide's
  [Problem information and expected knowledge](question-style-guide.md#problem-information-and-expected-knowledge)
  rule: supply necessary problem-specific givens, without supplying formulas,
  definitions, or methods students are expected to know or select. This does
  not restrict explanations in Core Concept or the worked solution.
- Body: question sentence with inline math, then `**Solution:**` on its
  own line, then numbered steps (`1.`, `2.` markdown list), with each
  key computation as a `$$ ... $$` display line between/after steps.
- Keep each display equation narrow enough for a phone screen.

## Traps section

- Each trap: `### #N: Short Title`, then a blockquote one-line warning,
  then a short paragraph explaining the trick and the wrong answer the
  test-makers plant.

## Math notation

- Use `\dfrac` for top-level fractions. Use `\frac` only inside another fraction.
- Every number in prose is wrapped in inline math, even isolated ones:
  `the $2$ in $3^2$`, `The correct answer is $25$.` Same for operators
  referenced in prose: `plus ($+$)`.
- Inline math `$...$`; display math `$$...$$` on its own line.
- Never `\( \)`, `\[ \]`, or code fences for math. `\cancel{}` is allowed.

## Formatting rules

- Allowed: `##`/`###` headings, paragraphs (1–3 short sentences), bold for
  key terms, italics sparingly, blockquotes, bullet lists (sparingly) and
  numbered lists (solution steps), inline/display LaTeX.
- NEVER: tables, links, code blocks, or horizontal rules.
- Follow the assigned workstream's current illustration contract and supported
  image format. Do not infer that images are unavailable from this older guide.
  Placeholders below are authoring notes, not completed illustrations.

## Illustration placeholders

Wherever a figure, diagram, or picture would genuinely help learning
(geometry figures, graphs, cell diagrams, figure-series patterns, gear
setups), insert an HTML comment at the exact spot the image belongs:

```
<!-- ILLUSTRATION: labeled right triangle with legs 3 and 4, hypotenuse x -->
```

- One line, specific enough that an artist could draw it without reading
  the whole lesson.
- Invisible in the app and the preview; greppable later with
  `grep -rn "ILLUSTRATION:" content/lessons`.
- Put a placeholder in **every example whose question depends on a figure**,
  not just one per lesson. A figure-based example needs its own placeholder
  in its own block.
- Usually the surrounding text must still teach without the image (describe
  the figure in words) — the illustration upgrades the lesson.
- **Load-bearing exception:** in Reasoning (figure series, grouping,
  matrices, paper folding, mental rotation, gears, pulleys, levers) and in
  Reading Comprehension's visual-media lesson, the figure *is* the
  question. There the placeholder is required and the prose cannot fully
  substitute for the image; still describe enough that the example is not
  a blank.

## Standards text: expected knowledge in example questions (rule L05)

## Problem information and expected knowledge

Provide the problem-specific facts, values, conditions, and referenced
material needed for a determinate answer. Include necessary units and state
rounding or approximation requirements when they affect the answer. A
referenced passage, figure, table, or marked word must be available with the
question when it is shown to the student.

Do not supply formulas, definitions, or solution methods that students are
expected to know or select. Use taught content and source-backed exam
requirements to identify that expected knowledge. Include a formula only when
the target exam supplies it or recalling it is outside the skill being tested.
Record the reason for that exception in the audit or authoring records.

For example, a simple-interest question supplies the principal, annual rate,
duration, and interest type. It does not supply the simple-interest formula
when recalling or selecting that formula is part of the tested skill.

This distinction applies to question stems, including worked-example stems
inside lessons. It does not prevent lesson teaching or answer solutions from
explaining formulas and methods. During the coverage audit, Level 4 checks
worked-example stems; Level 5 checks in-lesson quizzes and the question bank.


## Standards text: voice and math rules

## Voice, for in-app content and interface text

These rules govern the app. External marketing and website copy have separate
direction from Aedwon, who personally writes and approves website copy. Do not
apply these rules to that copy by default. See `WORKFLOW.md`.

- App interface text uses simple English and short sentences.
- **Lesson teaching content:** plain, simple English that a Grade 5 student can
  understand.
- **Quiz questions and answer choices:** realistic language for the target exam.
  This applies to Mock, normal quizzes, and in-lesson quizzes. Do not apply the
  lesson's Grade 5 language rule to those questions. Wrong choices must be
  plausible and reflect meaningful reasoning errors or misconceptions.
- **Filipino-language content** (Aedwon, 4 October 2026): lessons,
  explanations, and wrong-choice notes that teach or test Filipino use simple
  Filipino at the same Grade 5 standard instead of English. Quiz stems and
  choices use realistic exam Filipino. Rules:
  `docs/content/lesson-style-guide.md` and `docs/content/question-style-guide.md`.
- **Quiz answer explanations:** plain English a Grade 5 student can understand,
  including Mock, normal quizzes, and in-lesson quizzes. Explain the reasoning
  clearly without reducing the subject's accuracy. The explanation (shown to
  Free and Pro) says only why the correct answer is correct, with the worked
  solution when solving is involved. It never discusses wrong choices.
- **Wrong-choice notes (Pro):** every wrong choice carries its own note in the same
  plain English: the mistake it catches, why it looks right, and one check to avoid
  it next time. Rules: `docs/content/question-style-guide.md`.
- **No em dashes.** Use a comma or a colon.
- **American spelling.** practice, color, behavior, analyze, gray, center, organize.
  Never practise, colour, behaviour, analyse, grey, centre, organise. This holds in
  specs and prose as well as UI strings.
- Scores are **"points"**, never "marks". `mark` already means the answer dot, the
  flag pennant and the outcome mark on a reviewed question.
- **"Questions"**, never "cards".
- No article on archetype names: "Night-Owl Sprinter", not "The Night-Owl Sprinter".
- The words FSRS, stability, retention, retrievability and algorithm never appear in
  a user-facing string.

### Maths in content

## Core Concept

A **wave** is a traveling disturbance or oscillation that transfers energy and momentum. In a mechanical wave, the material of the medium generally oscillates around equilibrium instead of traveling permanently with the disturbance.

### Wave Properties

**Wavelength** ($\lambda$): the distance between adjacent points in the same phase, such as crest to crest for a sinusoidal transverse wave.

**Frequency** ($f$): the number of cycles per second, measured in **hertz** ($Hz$).

**Amplitude**: the maximum size of the disturbance relative to equilibrium. For many linear waves, the transported energy or power grows with the **square** of amplitude, so doubling amplitude can have a much larger effect than simply doubling energy.

**Wave speed** ($v$): for a periodic wave,

$$v=f\lambda$$

The wave speed is set by the medium and physical conditions for mechanical waves, and by the electromagnetic properties of the medium for light. If the wave enters a new medium and its speed changes while the source frequency remains fixed, its wavelength changes accordingly.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "concept-diagram", "type": "wave", "labels": {"wavelength": "Wavelength (λ)", "amplitude": "Amplitude (A)", "direction": "Wave travel direction"}, "semanticsLabel": "A transverse wave diagram with labeled wavelength between crests, amplitude from rest line, and direction arrow."} -->

### Mechanical vs. Electromagnetic Waves

A **mechanical wave** is a disturbance that propagates through matter. Sound in air, seismic waves, waves on strings, and water-surface waves are mechanical waves.

An **electromagnetic wave** is a propagating disturbance of electric and magnetic fields. It does **not** require a material medium and can travel through vacuum. Radio waves, visible light, X-rays, and gamma rays are electromagnetic waves.

For mechanical waves, classification as **transverse** or **longitudinal** refers to the direction of the medium's disturbance compared with the propagation direction. In a transverse wave, the disturbance is perpendicular to the direction of travel; in a longitudinal wave, it is parallel. Sound in fluids is longitudinal. Waves in solids can include both longitudinal and transverse modes. Electromagnetic waves are transverse in the sense that their electric and magnetic fields oscillate perpendicular to the direction of propagation—not because particles of a medium move sideways.

### Sound

Sound is a mechanical pressure disturbance. Its speed depends on the medium and its state, including temperature. In dry air near $20°C$, a commonly used value is about $343\text{ m/s}$, not one universal speed for all conditions.

For a sinusoidal sound wave, **frequency** is closely related to perceived pitch, while intensity depends on transported power per area. Perceived loudness is more complicated than amplitude alone and also depends on frequency and human hearing.

### The Doppler Effect

The **Doppler effect** is a change in observed frequency caused by relative motion between a source and observer. For sound, motion of the source and motion of the observer affect the observed frequency in different ways because sound propagates relative to its medium.

Approach generally raises the observed frequency; separation lowers it. The speed of sound relative to the surrounding medium is set mainly by the medium's properties, but an observer moving through the medium measures wavefronts arriving at a different rate.

## Examples

### #1: Calculating Wave Speed

A wave has a frequency of $5\text{ Hz}$ and a wavelength of $2\text{ m}$. Find its speed.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "concept-diagram", "type": "wave", "labels": {"wavelength": "2 m", "frequency": "5 Hz"}, "semanticsLabel": "A transverse wave diagram with a 2 m wavelength and frequency of 5 Hz."} -->

**Solution:**

$$v=f\lambda=(5)(2)=10\text{ m/s}$$

### #2: Finding Wavelength from Speed and Frequency

A sound wave travels at $340\text{ m/s}$ with a frequency of $170\text{ Hz}$. Find its wavelength.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "concept-diagram", "type": "wave", "labels": {"wavelength": "?", "speed": "340 m/s", "frequency": "170 Hz"}, "semanticsLabel": "A sound wave diagram with wavelength marked as a question mark, speed 340 m/s, and frequency 170 Hz."} -->

**Solution:**

$$\lambda=\frac{v}{f}=\frac{340}{170}=2\text{ m}$$

### #3: Mechanical or Electromagnetic?

Classify each wave: (a) sound traveling through air; (b) sunlight traveling through interplanetary vacuum.

**Solution:**

1. Sound requires matter through which the pressure disturbance propagates: **mechanical**.
2. Light is an oscillation of electromagnetic fields and can propagate through vacuum: **electromagnetic**.

### #4: Applying the Doppler Effect

An ambulance siren sounds higher in pitch while approaching a stationary observer and lower after receding. Explain why.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind":"physics-energy-diagram","type":"doppler-ambulance","illustrationKey":"science/physics/waves-and-sound#4","source":"moving ambulance","ahead":{"wavelength":"shorter","pitch":"higher"},"behind":{"wavelength":"longer","pitch":"lower"}} -->

**Solution:**

1. A moving source emits successive wavefronts from different positions.
2. In front of the approaching source, the wavefront spacing in the air is reduced, so a stationary observer encounters them more frequently.
3. Behind the receding source, the wavefront spacing is increased, so the observer encounters them less frequently.
4. The observed frequency therefore rises during approach and falls during recession.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Use $v=f\lambda$ only after identifying which speed, frequency, and wavelength belong to the same wave in the same medium.

For mechanical versus electromagnetic waves, ask whether a material medium is required. Sound requires matter; electromagnetic radiation does not.

For Doppler questions, first identify **who is moving relative to the medium**: source, observer, or both. “Approaching means higher observed frequency” is a reliable direction check, but the quantitative formula depends on the motion setup.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Saying a Wave Carries the Medium Along With It

> In an ordinary mechanical wave, particles of the medium oscillate about equilibrium while the disturbance propagates.

A water wave can move across a surface while individual water parcels mainly move locally rather than traveling with the crest across the entire basin.

### #2: Describing Light as Particles of a Medium Moving Transversely

> Electromagnetic waves are transverse oscillations of electric and magnetic fields.

They do not need material particles to oscillate, which is why light can cross vacuum.

### #3: Assuming Bigger Amplitude Means Proportionally Bigger Energy

> For many linear waves, energy or power is proportional to amplitude squared.

Doubling amplitude can therefore correspond to roughly four times the transported energy or power, depending on the wave system.

### #4: Treating the Speed of Sound as Always $340\text{ m/s}$

> Sound speed depends on the medium and its state.

Values near $340$–$343\text{ m/s}$ are common approximations for room-temperature air, not universal constants.

### #5: Saying the Doppler Effect Makes Sound Travel Faster Through Air

> Source motion changes the spacing of emitted wavefronts; observer motion changes the rate at which wavefronts are encountered.

The propagation speed relative to the medium is governed mainly by the medium, not by whether the source is approaching the listener.

## Core Concept

Trigonometry starts with a right triangle and one chosen acute angle $\theta$. The three sides get names based on where they sit relative to $\theta$:

- **Hypotenuse**: across from the right angle. Always the longest.
- **Opposite**: the side across from $\theta$.
- **Adjacent**: the side next to $\theta$ (not the hypotenuse).

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "right-triangle-trig", "labels": ["θ", "Opposite", "Adjacent", "Hypotenuse", "90°"], "semanticsLabel": "Right triangle with acute angle theta, opposite side, adjacent side, and hypotenuse labeled relative to theta."} -->

The three main ratios:

$$\sin \theta = \dfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}$$

$$\cos \theta = \dfrac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}$$

$$\tan \theta = \dfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}$$

Each has a **reciprocal** partner: cosecant, secant, and cotangent.

$$\csc \theta = \dfrac{1}{\sin \theta} \qquad \sec \theta = \dfrac{1}{\cos \theta} \qquad \cot \theta = \dfrac{1}{\tan \theta}$$

Important: the ratios depend only on the angle, not on the triangle's size. A $3$-$4$-$5$ triangle and a $6$-$8$-$10$ triangle give identical ratios, because they are similar triangles.

The exam values to memorize: $\sin 30^\circ = \dfrac{1}{2}$, $\cos 60^\circ = \dfrac{1}{2}$, $\sin 45^\circ = \cos 45^\circ = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, $\tan 45^\circ = 1$, $\sin 60^\circ = \cos 30^\circ = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, $\tan 30^\circ = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$, and $\tan 60^\circ = \sqrt{3}$.

## Mnemonics & Memory Aids

The famous one:

> SOH-CAH-TOA

**S**ine is **O**pposite over **H**ypotenuse. **C**osine is **A**djacent over **H**ypotenuse. **T**angent is **O**pposite over **A**djacent.

For the reciprocals, match them by avoiding the same first letter: **s**in pairs with **c**sc, **c**os pairs with **s**ec. The leftover pair is tan and cot.

## Examples

### #1: Reading the Ratios from a Triangle

A right triangle has legs $3$ and $4$ and hypotenuse $5$. If $\theta$ is the angle opposite the side of length $3$, find $\sin \theta$, $\cos \theta$, and $\tan \theta$.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "right-triangle-trig", "labels": ["θ", "Opposite = 3", "Adjacent = 4", "Hypotenuse = 5", "90°"], "semanticsLabel": "Right triangle with angle theta opposite side 3, adjacent side 4, and hypotenuse 5."} -->


**Solution:**
Label the sides relative to $\theta$: opposite is $3$, adjacent is $4$, hypotenuse is $5$.

1. Sine is opposite over hypotenuse.

$$\sin \theta = \dfrac{3}{5}$$

2. Cosine is adjacent over hypotenuse.

$$\cos \theta = \dfrac{4}{5}$$

3. Tangent is opposite over adjacent.

$$\tan \theta = \dfrac{3}{4}$$

### #2: One Ratio Gives the Others

If $\sin \theta = \dfrac{5}{13}$ and $\theta$ is acute, find $\cos \theta$ and $\tan \theta$.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "right-triangle-trig", "labels": ["θ", "Opposite = 5", "Hypotenuse = 13", "Adjacent = 12", "90°"], "semanticsLabel": "Right triangle with angle theta, opposite side 5, hypotenuse 13, and adjacent side 12."} -->


**Solution:**
Build the triangle behind the ratio: opposite $5$, hypotenuse $13$. Find the missing adjacent side.

1. Use the Pythagorean theorem.

$$a^2 = 13^2 - 5^2 = 169 - 25 = 144$$

2. Take the square root.

$$a = 12$$

3. Now read off the other ratios.

$$\cos \theta = \dfrac{12}{13} \qquad \tan \theta = \dfrac{5}{12}$$

4. Recognizing the $5$-$12$-$13$ triple skips the squaring entirely.

### #3: Finding a Missing Side

In a right triangle, the hypotenuse is $20$ and one acute angle measures $30^\circ$. Find the side opposite the $30^\circ$ angle.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "right-triangle-trig", "labels": ["30°", "Hypotenuse = 20", "Opposite = ?", "90°"], "semanticsLabel": "Right triangle with 30 degree angle, hypotenuse 20, and opposite side labeled with a question mark."} -->


**Solution:**
We know the hypotenuse and want the opposite: that is sine territory.

1. Set up the ratio.

$$\sin 30^\circ = \dfrac{x}{20}$$

2. Substitute the memorized value $\sin 30^\circ = \dfrac{1}{2}$.

$$\dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{x}{20}$$

3. Multiply both sides by $20$.

$$x = 10$$

4. The opposite side is $10$. Shortcut: in a $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle, the side across $30^\circ$ is always half the hypotenuse.

### #4: The Reciprocal Ratios

Using the triangle from Example $1$ (opposite $3$, adjacent $4$, hypotenuse $5$), find $\csc \theta$, $\sec \theta$, and $\cot \theta$.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "right-triangle-trig", "labels": ["θ", "Opp = 3", "Adj = 4", "Hyp = 5", "Reciprocal Ratios"], "semanticsLabel": "Right triangle with sides 3, 4, and 5 with angle theta for reading reciprocal trigonometric ratios."} -->


**Solution:**
Flip each main ratio upside down.

1. Cosecant flips sine.

$$\csc \theta = \dfrac{5}{3}$$

2. Secant flips cosine.

$$\sec \theta = \dfrac{5}{4}$$

3. Cotangent flips tangent.

$$\cot \theta = \dfrac{4}{3}$$

### #5: Exam-Level Combination

In a right triangle, $\tan \theta = \dfrac{3}{4}$ and the hypotenuse measures $20$. Find the two legs and the value of $\sin \theta$.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "right-triangle-trig", "labels": ["θ", "Opp = 3k = 12", "Adj = 4k = 16", "Hyp = 5k = 20", "90°"], "semanticsLabel": "Right triangle where tangent theta is 3 over 4 with hypotenuse 20 scaled to legs 12 and 16."} -->


**Solution:**
The tangent gives the shape of the triangle; the hypotenuse gives its size.

1. A tangent of $\dfrac{3}{4}$ means opposite and adjacent are in the ratio $3 : 4$. That is the $3$-$4$-$5$ family, so the hypotenuse pattern is $5$ parts.
2. The real hypotenuse is $20$, so each part is $20 \div 5 = 4$.
3. Scale the legs.

$$\text{opposite} = 3 \times 4 = 12 \qquad \text{adjacent} = 4 \times 4 = 16$$

4. Now read the sine.

$$\sin \theta = \dfrac{12}{20} = \dfrac{3}{5}$$

5. Check with Pythagoras: $12^2 + 16^2 = 144 + 256 = 400 = 20^2$. Correct.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Choosing the right ratio is a two-question game: which side do I know, and which side do I want? Pick the ratio whose letters mention exactly those two sides. Know the hypotenuse, want the opposite? The pair O and H lives in SOH, so use sine.

Complementary angles swap sine and cosine: $\sin \theta = \cos(90^\circ - \theta)$. So $\sin 25^\circ = \cos 65^\circ$ with zero computation. "Cosine" literally means "sine of the complement", and CETs test this as a one-liner.

Keep the Pythagorean triples hot: $3$-$4$-$5$, $5$-$12$-$13$, $8$-$15$-$17$. Almost every "find the third side first" ratio problem uses one of them.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Opposite and Adjacent Depend on the Angle

> Switch angles and the labels switch too.

The side that is opposite to one acute angle is adjacent to the other. If a problem computes ratios for both acute angles, relabel the triangle each time. Using one fixed labeling gives you the cofunction's value instead, which is planted in the choices.

### #2: The Hypotenuse Is Never Opposite or Adjacent

> The hypotenuse has one identity: across from the right angle.

Students sometimes call the hypotenuse "adjacent" because it touches $\theta$. It does touch, but it is reserved. The adjacent side is the other side touching $\theta$. Tangent, in particular, never involves the hypotenuse at all.

### #3: Flipping the Wrong Reciprocal

> Cosecant goes with sine, secant goes with cosine.

The names feel backwards: csc starts with c but flips sin, and sec starts with s but flips cos. Use the different-first-letter rule from the mnemonic. Mixing them turns $\csc \theta = \dfrac{5}{3}$ into $\dfrac{5}{4}$, and both appear in the choices.

### #4: Ratios Are Not Lengths

> $\sin \theta = \dfrac{3}{5}$ does not mean the sides are $3$ and $5$.

The sides could be $6$ and $10$, or $30$ and $50$. Only the ratio is fixed. When a problem gives one real length, find the scale factor first (like in Example $5$) before naming any side.

## Core Concept

**Optics** is the branch of physics that studies light and its interaction with matter. This lesson mainly uses **geometric optics**, where light is modeled as rays; that approximation works best when the objects and openings involved are much larger than the light's wavelength.

### Reflection

The **law of reflection** says that the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, with both angles measured from the **normal** (a line perpendicular to the surface at the point of incidence):

$$\theta_i=\theta_r$$

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "concept-diagram", "type": "ray-reflection", "labels": ["Incident ray", "Normal line", "Reflected ray"], "angles": ["Angle of incidence", "Angle of reflection"], "semanticsLabel": "A flat mirror with a normal line showing an incoming incident ray and an outgoing reflected ray at equal angles."} -->

### Refraction

**Refraction** is a change in a light ray's direction when it crosses a boundary where its propagation speed changes. The **index of refraction** is

$$n=\frac{c}{v}$$

where $c$ is the speed of light in vacuum and $v$ is its speed in the material. **Snell's law** relates the incident and refracted angles:

$$n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2$$

When light enters a medium with a higher refractive index, it slows and bends **toward the normal** (except at normal incidence, where it does not change direction). When it enters a lower-index medium, it bends **away from the normal**. The frequency set by the source remains unchanged across the boundary, while the wavelength changes because the speed changes.

If light travels from a higher-index medium to a lower-index one and the incident angle exceeds the **critical angle**, it undergoes **total internal reflection** instead of refracting out.

### Mirrors

For ideal spherical mirrors under the usual paraxial-ray approximation:

- A **concave mirror** is converging for rays parallel and close to its principal axis.
- A **convex mirror** is diverging.

The type and orientation of the image formed by a concave mirror depend on the object's position relative to the focal point. Convex mirrors form upright, reduced, virtual images for real objects in the ordinary setup.

### Lenses

Lenses form images by refraction. For a typical glass or plastic lens **in air**, where the lens has a higher refractive index than its surroundings:

- A **convex lens** (thicker at the center) is converging.
- A **concave lens** (thinner at the center) is diverging.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind":"physics-energy-diagram","type":"lens-comparison","illustrationKey":"science/physics/light-and-optics#2","convexBehavior":"parallel rays bend inward to one focus point","concaveBehavior":"parallel rays spread outward"} -->

This shape rule is not universal for every surrounding medium: changing the relative refractive indices can change a lens's optical power and even reverse whether it converges or diverges.

In vision correction, converging lenses are commonly used for hyperopia (farsightedness), while diverging lenses are commonly used for myopia (nearsightedness).

### The Electromagnetic Spectrum

The **electromagnetic spectrum** includes radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays. In vacuum, all electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed $c$, but they differ in frequency and wavelength:

$$c=f\lambda$$

From longest wavelength to shortest: **radio → microwave → infrared → visible → ultraviolet → X-ray → gamma**. Within visible light, red has the longest wavelength and violet the shortest.

## Examples

### #1: Applying the Law of Reflection

A light ray hits a flat mirror at an angle of incidence of $40°$ measured from the normal. Find the angle of reflection.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "concept-diagram", "type": "ray-reflection", "incidentAngle": 40, "reflectedAngle": 40, "labels": ["40°", "40°"], "semanticsLabel": "A light ray striking a flat mirror at 40 degrees from the normal line and reflecting off at 40 degrees."} -->

**Solution:**

$$\theta_r=\theta_i=40°$$

### #2: Converging or Diverging?

Assume ordinary glass optical elements in air. Classify: (a) a concave mirror; (b) a convex lens; (c) a convex mirror.

**Solution:**

1. Concave mirror: **converging** for paraxial rays.
2. Convex lens in air: **converging**.
3. Convex mirror: **diverging**.

### #3: Ordering the Electromagnetic Spectrum

Arrange X-rays, radio waves, visible light, and gamma rays from longest to shortest wavelength.

**Solution:**

**Radio waves → visible light → X-rays → gamma rays.**

### #4: Explaining Refraction with a Straw in Water

A straw partly submerged in water appears displaced or "bent" when viewed from the side. Explain why.

**Solution:**

1. Light from the underwater part of the straw travels from water into air.
2. Because the refractive indices differ, the light changes speed and direction at the boundary according to Snell's law.
3. Your visual system traces the emerging rays approximately backward along straight lines, so the submerged part appears at a shifted apparent position.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For reflection, measure angles from the **normal**, not from the mirror surface.

For refraction, compare refractive indices: entering higher $n$ bends toward the normal; entering lower $n$ bends away, assuming nonzero incidence angle.

For mirrors and lenses, keep the device types separate. In air: concave mirror converges, convex mirror diverges; convex lens converges, concave lens diverges.

For electromagnetic waves in vacuum, remember that higher frequency means shorter wavelength because $c=f\lambda$.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Measuring Reflection Angles from the Surface

> Incidence and reflection angles are measured from the normal.

A ray $20°$ above a flat mirror surface is $70°$ from the normal, not $20°$.

### #2: Saying Light Always Bends When It Enters a New Medium

> A speed change can occur without a directional bend when the ray hits the boundary along the normal.

Refraction refers to the change associated with entering a different optical medium; the ray's direction changes only when the geometry requires it.

### #3: Treating Lens Shape Alone as Universal

> Whether a lens converges depends on both its shape and its refractive index relative to the surrounding medium.

The familiar "convex converges, concave diverges" rule assumes an ordinary higher-index lens in a lower-index surrounding medium such as air.

### #4: Confusing Reflection and Refraction

> Reflection returns light into the incident medium; refraction transmits light into another medium with a change governed by Snell's law.

At a real boundary, both reflection and refraction can occur at the same time.

### #5: Assuming Visible Light Is Most of the Electromagnetic Spectrum

> Visible light is only a narrow range of electromagnetic wavelengths detectable by human eyes.

Radio, infrared, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma, and other electromagnetic radiation are still forms of light in the broader physics sense.
