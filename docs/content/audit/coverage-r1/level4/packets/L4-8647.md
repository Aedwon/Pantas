# Pantas Level 4 lesson audit, packet L4-8647

## Your job

You audit one lesson of the Pantas exam review app against its lesson standards.
You report findings. You change nothing.

- This packet holds everything you need: the rules, the standards text, the
  lesson, and the lessons it depends on.
- Judge the lesson only from this packet. Do not open any other file, packet,
  issue, or pull request in any repository, apart from the relay instructions
  your assignment names. Another reader is checking the same lesson separately,
  and the audit depends on the two of you not seeing each other's work.
- Every verdict is yours. Do not ask the user anything.
- You finish by handing in one form. Your chat reply is not read by anyone.

## How to judge

Work through the rules in the table below, one at a time, against the lesson
between the `LESSON START` and `LESSON END` markers.

1. **Read the lesson in full first**, then the prerequisite lessons.
2. **Give every rule in the form a verdict:** `pass`, `fail`, or
   `not_applicable`. Rule L11 is the one exception: use `needs_source_check` if
   the lesson contains Filipino text, or `not_applicable` if it does not. You
   cannot confirm Filipino spelling against the official source from here.
3. **A fail needs evidence.** Each finding has a `quote` copied exactly from the
   lesson (at least 8 characters, from between the markers, no paraphrase and no
   ellipsis) and a `problem` saying what breaks the rule and why. When something
   is missing, quote the place where it was needed.
4. **A pass on a Blocker or Major rule needs proof.** In `checked`, list what
   you checked in this lesson, concretely. For L17, name each formula an example
   uses and where Core Concept introduces it. For B02, give your own answer to
   each worked example. "Looks fine" is not proof.
5. **`not_applicable` needs a `note`** saying why the rule cannot apply here.
6. **Solve before you read.** For B02, work each example from its question
   alone, then compare with the lesson's solution.
7. **Explanation reuse (L25, L26, L27).** A term, concept, or condition counts
   as explained if this lesson explains it, if a prerequisite lesson below
   teaches it and this lesson gives a short reminder or reference, or if this
   lesson clearly points to its own later section and gives enough meaning to
   follow until then. A mere earlier mention is not an explanation.
8. **Learning goal (L01).** The goal is in the Level 3 record below. Judge
   whether the lesson serves it.
9. **Script candidates.** If the form includes B07, B08, B09, B10, L13, or L16,
   a script flagged lines for you under "Script candidates". Decide which
   flagged lines truly break the rule. Subject-matter uses do not: "marks" as
   punctuation, a playing card, the stability of an atom.
10. **Judge the rule as written.** Do not invent stricter rules, and do not
    excuse a clear breach. If you are unsure, say so in the `problem` or the
    `checked` text and choose the verdict the evidence supports best.

### Illustration review

Separately from the rules, judge the lesson's illustrations. Add one entry to
`illustrations` for each case:

- `missing`: a figure would improve understanding and none is there.
- `insufficient`: a placeholder or figure exists but does not do the job.
- `unneeded_existing`: an existing figure or placeholder is decorative.

For each entry give `position_quote` (exact lesson text at the spot),
`description` (one line an artist could draw from), `why` (how it helps the
student), `priority` (`high`, `medium`, `low`), `method` (`code` for a figure
that can be drawn in code, `library` for a public-domain or open-licensed image,
`table_or_formula` when a table or formula would serve better than an image),
and `kind` (`exact_exam_figure` when the figure is what an exam question shows,
`concept` otherwise). Do not recommend decoration. An empty list is a valid
answer.

### Accuracy concerns

If you notice a fact or an answer that looks wrong, also add it to
`accuracy_concerns` with an exact `quote` and what looks wrong. An empty list is
a valid answer.

## Standards text: lesson style guide

# Lesson Style Guide

Distilled from Aedwon's hand-reviewed lessons (see
`content/lessons/mathematics/`). Every generated lesson must follow this.
When in doubt, imitate `arithmetic-number-sense/order-of-operations.md`.

Facts are sourced per [source-policy.md](source-policy.md). Before submitting,
check the lesson against [content-checklist.md](content-checklist.md); a lesson
passes only with no blocker and no major finding.

## Learning goal

Every lesson has a learning goal: one or two sentences on what a student can do
after it and which exam requirement that serves. Record it in the audit records
as [source-policy.md](source-policy.md) describes, not in the frontmatter.
Everything in the lesson should serve the goal.

## Teaching completeness

- Explain necessary terms, symbols, and units before students need their
  meanings to understand the teaching or use a method.
- Explain what each concept means and how it works. A name or formula alone
  is not an explanation.
- State the relevant conditions, limitations, and exceptions of a rule,
  formula, or shortcut so students know when it applies.

For these three requirements, reuse explanations actually taught in an
identified prerequisite lesson with a short reminder or reference rather than
repeating the full explanation. If the current lesson develops an explanation
later, clearly point to that section and give any meaning needed to follow
the intervening teaching. A prior mention alone does not count as teaching.
If neither source supplies the explanation, it is a teaching gap. The current
lesson must still teach its own learning goal; prerequisite references cannot
replace that teaching. Record prerequisite evidence in the audit records.

## Voice

- Very simple English a Grade 5 student can read. Short sentences. No
  filler, no long introductions. Every sentence teaches something.
  Filipino-language lessons follow the Filipino section below.
- Explain exam verbs in one line the first time they appear:
  `"Evaluate" means find the final number answer.`
- Friendly-tutor analogies are welcome in Core Concept (building a house,
  twins holding hands), one per lesson at most.
- Example questions use authentic exam wording and difficulty. Solutions
  return to simple English.
- In-lesson quizzes follow the quiz rules: realistic target-exam language and
  plausible wrong choices based on reasoning errors or misconceptions. The Grade 5
  benchmark governs the teaching prose, not these questions.
- Mention CETs when it motivates: why the topic is tested, what the trick
  usually is. Vary how you say it — do not open every trap with
  "Test-makers often…".

## Filipino-language lessons

Lessons that teach Filipino grammar, vocabulary, or reading (Aedwon,
4 October 2026):

- Write the teaching prose in simple Filipino, held to the same Grade 5 standard
  as English lessons: short sentences, common words, one idea per sentence.
- Give a hard grammar term a short English gloss the first time it appears:
  `**pang-abay** (adverb)`. After that, use the Filipino term alone.
- Example sentences, examples, and in-lesson quiz items are in Filipino.
- Spelling and usage follow the Komisyon sa Wikang Filipino, including the
  Ortograpiyang Pambansa. Source contested rules (nang and ng, din and rin,
  affixes) per [source-policy.md](source-policy.md).
- No Taglish in teaching prose. Use an English word only when it is the common
  term students and the exam use.
- There is no numeric readability score. English formulas misjudge Filipino
  text, and Filipino-specific models are still research tools. Readability is
  judged by the rules above and by a reader check at the student's level.

## Emphasis and de-AI rules

These keep lessons from reading as machine-written. Apply everywhere.

- **Never use ALL-CAPS for emphasis** (`the WHOLE paragraph`, `build
  PROTEINS`). Use **bold** for a key term the first time it is defined,
  and *italics* rarely for light stress. Capitalize only real acronyms
  (DNA, ATP, GCF) and proper nouns.
- **Target words in a question are bold**, and the prompt says "the word
  in bold" (never "the underlined word" — markdown cannot underline).
- **No em dashes.** Use a comma, colon, or separate sentence.
- **Avoid stock AI phrasing:** "it's important to note", "delve",
  "crucial", "plays a vital role", "furthermore/moreover", and the
  "not just X but Y" construction.
- **Vary sentence openings and rhythm.** Do not start three sentences in a
  row the same way; mix short and medium sentences instead of a uniform
  staccato.
- **Vary the summary.** The frontmatter `summary` should describe the
  lesson plainly; do not begin every lesson with "This lesson teaches
  you…".

## Section structure

```
## Core Concept
## Mnemonics & Memory Aids     ← OPTIONAL, see below
## Examples
## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts
## Traps to Watch For
```

- Exactly these titles ("Core Concept", not "The Core Concept";
  "Examples", not "Worked Examples").
- Mnemonics & Memory Aids is included ONLY when a genuinely known or
  natural mnemonic exists (PEMDAS sentences, SOH-CAH-TOA). If a mnemonic
  would sound forced or invented-for-the-sake-of-it, DROP the whole
  section. Do not fabricate cringe acronyms.
- An extra topical `##` section is allowed when the topic really needs it
  (e.g. "When to List Multiples" in divisibility rules) — sparingly.
- Sub-topics from the outline's required sections become `###` headings
  inside the appropriate section (usually Core Concept or Examples).

### Per-subject section fit

The five sections above were derived from Mathematics. The app renders
section titles generically, so other subjects may adapt them:

- **Mathematics:** keep the five sections. Every formula used in a
  solution must first appear in **Core Concept** — never introduce a
  formula for the first time inside an example.
- **Science (conceptual: biology, earth science):** "Speed Tricks &
  Shortcuts" may be titled **"Exam Strategies"** when the content is
  test strategy, not calculation. A **"Key Terms"** section is allowed
  for term-dense lessons (taxonomy, cell biology, genetics).
- **Science (quantitative: physics, parts of chemistry):** keep "Speed
  Tricks & Shortcuts"; the formula-in-Core-Concept rule applies here too.
- **Language Proficiency:** "Speed Tricks & Shortcuts" may be titled
  **"Common Confusions"** or **"Usage Notes"**.
- **Reading Comprehension:** "Speed Tricks & Shortcuts" may be titled
  **"Reading Strategies"**.
- **Reasoning:** use a reduced layout — **Core Concept → Examples →
  Traps to Watch For** — and drop Speed Tricks/Mnemonics, which rarely
  apply. Every example is figure-driven (see Illustration placeholders).

## Examples section

- Each example: `### #N: Short Descriptive Title` (e.g.
  `### #2: Nested Grouping Symbols`).
- Cover every distinct exam-relevant scenario, variation, and edge case
  within the lesson's learning goal, with one worked example per distinct
  case. Changing only the numbers does not create a new scenario.
- Order examples from basic to authentic target-exam difficulty.
  Let coverage determine the number of examples; avoid near-duplicates.
- Worked-example questions follow the question guide's
  [Problem information and expected knowledge](question-style-guide.md#problem-information-and-expected-knowledge)
  rule: supply necessary problem-specific givens, without supplying formulas,
  definitions, or methods students are expected to know or select. This does
  not restrict explanations in Core Concept or the worked solution.
- Body: question sentence with inline math, then `**Solution:**` on its
  own line, then numbered steps (`1.`, `2.` markdown list), with each
  key computation as a `$$ ... $$` display line between/after steps.
- Keep each display equation narrow enough for a phone screen.

## Traps section

- Each trap: `### #N: Short Title`, then a blockquote one-line warning,
  then a short paragraph explaining the trick and the wrong answer the
  test-makers plant.

## Math notation

- Use `\dfrac` for top-level fractions. Use `\frac` only inside another fraction.
- Every number in prose is wrapped in inline math, even isolated ones:
  `the $2$ in $3^2$`, `The correct answer is $25$.` Same for operators
  referenced in prose: `plus ($+$)`.
- Inline math `$...$`; display math `$$...$$` on its own line.
- Never `\( \)`, `\[ \]`, or code fences for math. `\cancel{}` is allowed.

## Formatting rules

- Allowed: `##`/`###` headings, paragraphs (1–3 short sentences), bold for
  key terms, italics sparingly, blockquotes, bullet lists (sparingly) and
  numbered lists (solution steps), inline/display LaTeX.
- NEVER: tables, links, code blocks, or horizontal rules.
- Follow the assigned workstream's current illustration contract and supported
  image format. Do not infer that images are unavailable from this older guide.
  Placeholders below are authoring notes, not completed illustrations.

## Illustration placeholders

Wherever a figure, diagram, or picture would genuinely help learning
(geometry figures, graphs, cell diagrams, figure-series patterns, gear
setups), insert an HTML comment at the exact spot the image belongs:

```
<!-- ILLUSTRATION: labeled right triangle with legs 3 and 4, hypotenuse x -->
```

- One line, specific enough that an artist could draw it without reading
  the whole lesson.
- Invisible in the app and the preview; greppable later with
  `grep -rn "ILLUSTRATION:" content/lessons`.
- Put a placeholder in **every example whose question depends on a figure**,
  not just one per lesson. A figure-based example needs its own placeholder
  in its own block.
- Usually the surrounding text must still teach without the image (describe
  the figure in words) — the illustration upgrades the lesson.
- **Load-bearing exception:** in Reasoning (figure series, grouping,
  matrices, paper folding, mental rotation, gears, pulleys, levers) and in
  Reading Comprehension's visual-media lesson, the figure *is* the
  question. There the placeholder is required and the prose cannot fully
  substitute for the image; still describe enough that the example is not
  a blank.

## Standards text: expected knowledge in example questions (rule L05)

## Problem information and expected knowledge

Provide the problem-specific facts, values, conditions, and referenced
material needed for a determinate answer. Include necessary units and state
rounding or approximation requirements when they affect the answer. A
referenced passage, figure, table, or marked word must be available with the
question when it is shown to the student.

Do not supply formulas, definitions, or solution methods that students are
expected to know or select. Use taught content and source-backed exam
requirements to identify that expected knowledge. Include a formula only when
the target exam supplies it or recalling it is outside the skill being tested.
Record the reason for that exception in the audit or authoring records.

For example, a simple-interest question supplies the principal, annual rate,
duration, and interest type. It does not supply the simple-interest formula
when recalling or selecting that formula is part of the tested skill.

This distinction applies to question stems, including worked-example stems
inside lessons. It does not prevent lesson teaching or answer solutions from
explaining formulas and methods. During the coverage audit, Level 4 checks
worked-example stems; Level 5 checks in-lesson quizzes and the question bank.


## Standards text: voice and math rules

## Voice, for in-app content and interface text

These rules govern the app. External marketing and website copy have separate
direction from Aedwon, who personally writes and approves website copy. Do not
apply these rules to that copy by default. See `WORKFLOW.md`.

- App interface text uses simple English and short sentences.
- **Lesson teaching content:** plain, simple English that a Grade 5 student can
  understand.
- **Quiz questions and answer choices:** realistic language for the target exam.
  This applies to Mock, normal quizzes, and in-lesson quizzes. Do not apply the
  lesson's Grade 5 language rule to those questions. Wrong choices must be
  plausible and reflect meaningful reasoning errors or misconceptions.
- **Filipino-language content** (Aedwon, 4 October 2026): lessons,
  explanations, and wrong-choice notes that teach or test Filipino use simple
  Filipino at the same Grade 5 standard instead of English. Quiz stems and
  choices use realistic exam Filipino. Rules:
  `docs/content/lesson-style-guide.md` and `docs/content/question-style-guide.md`.
- **Quiz answer explanations:** plain English a Grade 5 student can understand,
  including Mock, normal quizzes, and in-lesson quizzes. Explain the reasoning
  clearly without reducing the subject's accuracy. The explanation (shown to
  Free and Pro) says only why the correct answer is correct, with the worked
  solution when solving is involved. It never discusses wrong choices.
- **Wrong-choice notes (Pro):** every wrong choice carries its own note in the same
  plain English: the mistake it catches, why it looks right, and one check to avoid

## Core Concept

The coordinate plane puts geometry on a grid. Every point gets an address $(x, y)$: steps right or left, then steps up or down. With coordinates, we can calculate geometric relationships instead of relying only on a drawing.

### Distance and Midpoint Formulas

The **distance** between points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$:

$$d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$$

This comes from the Pythagorean theorem. The horizontal and vertical changes are the legs of a right triangle, and the distance between the points is the hypotenuse.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "coordinate-geometry", "labels": ["(x1, y1)", "(x2, y2)", "dx = x2 - x1", "dy = y2 - y1", "Distance d"], "semanticsLabel": "Grid diagram showing two points joined by a segment forming a right triangle with horizontal and vertical distance legs."} -->

The **midpoint** is the average of the coordinates:

$$M = \left( \dfrac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \dfrac{y_1 + y_2}{2} \right)$$

### Slope of a Line

**Slope** ($m$) measures steepness: rise over run.

$$m = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$$

Read slopes at a glance: uphill left-to-right is positive, downhill is negative, a horizontal line has slope $0$, and a vertical line has undefined slope because its run is $0$.

Two useful line relationships:

- **Parallel nonvertical lines** have equal slopes. Distinct vertical lines are also parallel to one another.
- **Perpendicular nonvertical, nonhorizontal lines** have slopes that are negative reciprocals. For example, a slope of $2$ pairs with $-\dfrac{1}{2}$, and their product is $-1$. A vertical line and a horizontal line are also perpendicular, but the product-of-slopes test does not apply because the vertical slope is undefined.

### Equations of a Line

- **Slope-intercept form**: $y = mx + b$, where $m$ is the slope and $b$ is the $y$-intercept (where the line crosses the $y$-axis).
- **Point-slope form**: $y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)$, useful when you know one point and the slope.
- **Standard form**: $Ax + By = C$.

For nonvertical lines, putting an equation into $y = mx + b$ makes its slope and $y$-intercept easy to read. Vertical lines instead have the form $x = c$.

## Examples

### #1: Distance Between Two Points

Find the distance between $(-3, 4)$ and $(5, -2)$.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "coordinate-geometry", "labels": ["(-3, 4)", "(5, -2)", "run = 8", "rise = 6", "d = ?"], "semanticsLabel": "Coordinate plane with points at negative 3 comma 4 and 5 comma negative 2 forming a right triangle with legs 8 and 6."} -->


**Solution:**
Plug into the distance formula. Watch the signs.

1. Horizontal change.

$$5 - (-3) = 8$$

2. Vertical change.

$$-2 - 4 = -6$$

3. Square both, add, and take the root.

$$d = \sqrt{8^2 + (-6)^2} = \sqrt{64 + 36}$$

4. Simplify.

$$d = \sqrt{100} = 10$$

5. The distance is $10$ units. The squaring removes the sign of each change, and $6$-$8$-$10$ is a Pythagorean triple.

### #2: Midpoint

Find the midpoint of the segment joining $(2, -3)$ and $(-4, 7)$.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "coordinate-geometry", "labels": ["(2, -3)", "(-4, 7)", "Midpoint (-1, 2)"], "semanticsLabel": "Coordinate plane showing segment connecting 2 comma negative 3 and negative 4 comma 7 with midpoint at negative 1 comma 2."} -->


**Solution:**
Average the $x$ values, then average the $y$ values.

1. The $x$-coordinate.

$$\dfrac{2 + (-4)}{2} = \dfrac{-2}{2} = -1$$

2. The $y$-coordinate.

$$\dfrac{-3 + 7}{2} = \dfrac{4}{2} = 2$$

3. The midpoint is $(-1, 2)$.

### #3: Slope, Then the Equation

Find the equation of the line through $(1, 2)$ and $(4, 11)$ in slope-intercept form.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "coordinate-geometry", "labels": ["(1, 2)", "(4, 11)", "run = 3", "rise = 9", "slope m = 3"], "semanticsLabel": "Coordinate plane with points 1 comma 2 and 4 comma 11 showing rise of 9 and run of 3 along the line."} -->


**Solution:**
Slope first, then use one point to find $b$.

1. Compute the slope.

$$m = \dfrac{11 - 2}{4 - 1} = \dfrac{9}{3} = 3$$

2. Start the equation: $y = 3x + b$. Substitute the point $(1, 2)$.

$$2 = 3(1) + b$$

3. Solve for $b$.

$$b = -1$$

4. The equation is $y = 3x - 1$.
5. Check with the other point: $3(4) - 1 = 11$. Correct.

### #4: A Perpendicular Line

Find the equation of the line perpendicular to $y = 2x + 3$ that passes through $(4, 1)$.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "coordinate-geometry", "labels": ["y = 2x + 3", "(4, 1)", "m1 = 2", "m2 = -1/2", "90°"], "semanticsLabel": "Coordinate plane with line y equals 2x plus 3 and perpendicular line through 4 comma 1 intersecting at a right angle."} -->


**Solution:**
The given line is neither vertical nor horizontal, so the perpendicular line has the negative reciprocal slope.

1. The given slope is $2$. Take the reciprocal and switch the sign.

$$m = -\dfrac{1}{2}$$

2. Use point-slope form with $(4, 1)$.

$$y - 1 = -\dfrac{1}{2}(x - 4)$$

3. Distribute.

$$y - 1 = -\dfrac{1}{2}x + 2$$

4. Add $1$ to both sides.

$$y = -\dfrac{1}{2}x + 3$$

5. Quick check: both slopes are defined, and $2 \times \left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\right) = -1$, so the lines are perpendicular.

### #5: Finding a Missing Endpoint

$M(3, -1)$ is the midpoint of segment $AB$. If $A$ is $(7, 4)$, find the coordinates of $B$.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "coordinate-geometry", "labels": ["A(7, 4)", "M(3, -1)", "B(-1, -6)"], "semanticsLabel": "Coordinate plane showing point A at 7 comma 4, midpoint M at 3 comma negative 1, and endpoint B at negative 1 comma negative 6."} -->


**Solution:**
This reverses the midpoint formula. The midpoint is the average, so work backwards.

1. Set up the $x$ equation with $B = (x, y)$.

$$\dfrac{7 + x}{2} = 3$$

2. Multiply by $2$, then subtract $7$.

$$7 + x = 6$$

$$x = -1$$

3. Set up the $y$ equation.

$$\dfrac{4 + y}{2} = -1$$

4. Multiply by $2$, then subtract $4$.

$$4 + y = -2$$

$$y = -6$$

5. Point $B$ is $(-1, -6)$. Check: the averages of $7$ and $-1$, and of $4$ and $-6$, are indeed $3$ and $-1$.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

A faster route to a missing endpoint (Example $5$): the midpoint is the center, so keep moving the same amount again. From $A(7, 4)$ to $M(3, -1)$ you move $-4$ in $x$ and $-5$ in $y$. Move the same again from $M$: $(3 - 4, -1 - 5) = (-1, -6)$. No equations needed.

Before using the distance formula, check if the points share an $x$ or a $y$. Points like $(2, 5)$ and $(2, 9)$ lie on a vertical line; the distance is just $9 - 5 = 4$. No square roots.

To read the slope from standard form $Ax + By = C$ without rearranging, use $m = -\dfrac{A}{B}$ when $B \neq 0$. If $B = 0$, the equation describes a vertical line and its slope is undefined.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Subtracting in Mismatched Order

> In the slope formula, both subtractions must go in the same direction.

Computing $\dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_1 - x_2}$ (top one way, bottom the other) flips the sign of the slope. Pick a "first point" and subtract it in both the top and bottom.

### #2: Reciprocal Without the Sign Switch

> For two nonvertical, nonhorizontal perpendicular lines, flip and negate the slope.

Given slope $\dfrac{2}{3}$, the perpendicular slope is $-\dfrac{3}{2}$. If one line is vertical, however, its perpendicular line is horizontal; do not try to take a reciprocal of an undefined slope.

### #3: Distance Signs Vanish, Coordinates Do Not

> Squaring removes signs in distance, but midpoints keep their signs.

In the distance formula a negative coordinate difference becomes positive after squaring. Midpoint coordinates are averages, so their signs remain important. For $(2, -3)$ and $(-4, 7)$, the midpoint is $(-1, 2)$.

### #4: Zero Slope vs. No Slope

> Horizontal lines have slope $0$. Vertical lines have undefined slope.

These are different things: $0$ is a defined number, while a vertical slope would require division by zero. The line $x = 5$ has undefined slope; the line $y = 5$ has slope $0$.

## Core Concept

Degrees and radians are two languages for measuring the same angle.

You know degrees: a full circle is $360^\circ$, a straight line is $180^\circ$, a right angle is $90^\circ$.

A **radian** measures angles using the circle itself. One radian is the angle you get when the arc is exactly as long as the radius. Wrap the radius along the edge of the circle: the angle it covers is $1$ radian.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "circle-arc", "labels": ["Radius r", "Arc length = r", "Central Angle = 1 rad"], "semanticsLabel": "Circle showing radius r, an arc of length r along the edge, and central angle 1 radian."} -->

The bridge between the two languages:

$$180^\circ = \pi \text{ radians}$$

From this one fact come both conversion rules:

- Degrees to radians: multiply by $\dfrac{\pi}{180}$.
- Radians to degrees: multiply by $\dfrac{180}{\pi}$.

An angle in **standard position** starts from the positive $x$-axis. Counterclockwise is positive, clockwise is negative. Angles that end at the same spot are called **coterminal**; they differ by full turns of $360^\circ$ (or $2\pi$).

Radians also give the cleanest arc length formula. For a central angle $\theta$ in radians and radius $r$:

$$s = r\theta$$

CETs test straight conversions, coterminal angles, and which quadrant an angle lands in. All three are quick points once the conversion is automatic.

## Examples

### #1: Degrees to Radians

Convert $60^\circ$ to radians.

**Solution:**
Multiply by $\dfrac{\pi}{180}$.

1. Set up the product.

$$60 \times \dfrac{\pi}{180}$$

2. Simplify the fraction $\dfrac{60}{180}$ to $\dfrac{1}{3}$.

$$60^\circ = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$$

3. So $60^\circ$ is $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$ radians.

### #2: Radians to Degrees

Convert $\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$ radians to degrees.

**Solution:**
Multiply by $\dfrac{180}{\pi}$. The $\pi$ symbols cancel.

1. Set up the product.

$$\dfrac{3\pi}{4} \times \dfrac{180}{\pi}$$

2. Cancel $\pi$ and divide $180$ by $4$.

$$3 \times 45 = 135$$

3. So $\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$ radians is $135^\circ$.

### #3: Converting Negative and Larger Angles

Convert $150^\circ$ and $-45^\circ$ to radians.

**Solution:**
Same rule; signs just ride along.

1. For $150^\circ$: simplify $\dfrac{150}{180} = \dfrac{5}{6}$.

$$150^\circ = \dfrac{5\pi}{6}$$

2. For $-45^\circ$: simplify $\dfrac{45}{180} = \dfrac{1}{4}$ and keep the minus.

$$-45^\circ = -\dfrac{\pi}{4}$$

3. Quadrant check: $\dfrac{5\pi}{6}$ is a bit less than $\pi$, so it lands in Quadrant II. Reasonable.

### #4: Coterminal Angles

Find the angle between $0^\circ$ and $360^\circ$ that is coterminal with $400^\circ$, and the one coterminal with $-120^\circ$.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "coterminal-angles", "labels": ["400° = 360° + 40°", "-120° = 240°"], "semanticsLabel": "Two coordinate planes showing 400 degrees landing at 40 degrees and negative 120 degrees measured clockwise landing at 240 degrees."} -->


**Solution:**
Add or subtract full turns of $360^\circ$ until you land inside the range.

1. For $400^\circ$, subtract one full turn.

$$400 - 360 = 40$$

2. So $400^\circ$ is coterminal with $40^\circ$.
3. For $-120^\circ$, add one full turn.

$$-120 + 360 = 240$$

4. So $-120^\circ$ is coterminal with $240^\circ$, which lies in Quadrant III.

### #5: Arc Length

A circle has a radius of $10$ cm. Find the length of the arc cut off by a central angle of $\dfrac{\pi}{5}$ radians. Then find the arc for a $72^\circ$ central angle.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "circle-arc", "labels": ["r = 10 cm", "θ = π/5 rad (72°)", "Arc s = rθ = 2π cm"], "semanticsLabel": "Circle of radius 10 cm with highlighted arc for central angle of pi over 5 radians or 72 degrees."} -->


**Solution:**
Use $s = r\theta$, but $\theta$ must be in radians.

1. First arc: the angle is already in radians.

$$s = 10 \times \dfrac{\pi}{5} = 2\pi \text{ cm}$$

2. Second arc: convert $72^\circ$ first. Simplify $\dfrac{72}{180} = \dfrac{2}{5}$.

$$72^\circ = \dfrac{2\pi}{5}$$

3. Now apply the formula.

$$s = 10 \times \dfrac{2\pi}{5} = 4\pi \text{ cm}$$

4. The arcs are $2\pi$ cm and $4\pi$ cm. Notice both angles gave clean answers because radians and $s = r\theta$ fit together perfectly.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Memorize the anchor conversions and build everything from them: $30^\circ = \dfrac{\pi}{6}$, $45^\circ = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$, $60^\circ = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$, $90^\circ = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, $180^\circ = \pi$, $360^\circ = 2\pi$. Then $135^\circ$ is just $3$ copies of $45^\circ$, so it is $\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$. Counting copies beats multiplying fractions.

To place a radian angle in its quadrant without converting: compare it to $\dfrac{\pi}{2} \approx 1.57$, $\pi \approx 3.14$, and $\dfrac{3\pi}{2} \approx 4.71$. An angle like $2$ radians sits between $1.57$ and $3.14$, so it is in Quadrant II.

For coterminal questions, you may need more than one turn: $800^\circ - 360^\circ = 440^\circ$, still too big, so subtract again to get $80^\circ$. Keep going until you land in range.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Multiplying by the Wrong Fraction

> Degrees to radians uses $\dfrac{\pi}{180}$. Radians to degrees uses $\dfrac{180}{\pi}$.

Use the wrong one and the answer explodes or shrinks absurdly. Sanity check: a number without $\pi$ and bigger than $7$ is almost surely degrees; radian answers for common angles are small multiples of $\pi$.

### #2: Degrees Inside $s = r\theta$

> The arc length formula only accepts radians.

Plugging $72^\circ$ straight into $s = r\theta$ gives $720$, a nonsense arc for a circle of radius $10$. Convert to radians first, every time. Test-makers pick angle values that produce believable-looking wrong answers.

### #3: A Radian Is Not a Degree Cousin

> $1$ radian is about $57.3^\circ$, much bigger than $1^\circ$.

When choices mix the two units, remember the scale: $2$ radians is already more than $114^\circ$. If you treat radians as "small degree-like numbers", every estimate you make will be wrong by a factor of about $57$.

## Core Concept

The **unit circle** is a circle of radius $1$ centered at the origin. It extends trigonometric functions beyond acute angles in right triangles: angles such as $225^\circ$ and $-30^\circ$ also have sine and cosine values.

Put an angle $\theta$ in standard position and mark where its terminal side crosses the unit circle. That point has coordinates:

$$(\cos \theta, \sin \theta)$$

Cosine is the $x$-coordinate. Sine is the $y$-coordinate. Where $\cos\theta\neq0$, tangent is their ratio:

$$\tan \theta = \dfrac{\sin \theta}{\cos \theta}$$

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "unit-circle", "labels": ["θ", "P(cos θ, sin θ)", "x = cos θ", "y = sin θ", "r = 1"], "semanticsLabel": "Unit circle with angle theta from positive x-axis and intersection point labeled cos theta comma sin theta."} -->

The exact values for the most commonly used first-quadrant special angles are worth memorizing:

- $\sin 0^\circ = 0$, $\quad \sin 30^\circ = \dfrac{1}{2}$, $\quad \sin 45^\circ = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, $\quad \sin 60^\circ = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, $\quad \sin 90^\circ = 1$.
- Cosine runs the same list backwards: $\cos 0^\circ = 1$ down to $\cos 90^\circ = 0$.
- Tangent: $\tan 0^\circ = 0$, $\tan 30^\circ = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$, $\tan 45^\circ = 1$, $\tan 60^\circ = \sqrt{3}$, and $\tan 90^\circ$ is undefined.

For angles beyond $90^\circ$, use two tools:

**Tool 1, the reference angle**: the positive acute angle between the terminal side and the $x$-axis. For angles between $0^\circ$ and $360^\circ$, the usual formulas are $180^\circ-\theta$ in Quadrant II, $\theta-180^\circ$ in Quadrant III, and $360^\circ-\theta$ in Quadrant IV. In Quadrant I, the angle itself is its reference angle.

**Tool 2, the quadrant signs**: the quadrant determines the signs. Since cosine is $x$ and sine is $y$: Quadrant I has sine and cosine positive; Quadrant II has sine positive and cosine negative; Quadrant III has both negative; Quadrant IV has cosine positive and sine negative. Tangent follows from sine divided by cosine.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "geometry-diagram", "type": "unit-circle-quadrants", "labels": ["Q I: All (+)", "Q II: Sine (+)", "Q III: Tangent (+)", "Q IV: Cosine (+)"], "semanticsLabel": "Unit circle divided into four quadrants showing which trigonometric functions are positive: All, Sine, Tangent, and Cosine."} -->

For the signs of sine, cosine, and tangent in the four quadrants, going counterclockwise from Quadrant I:

> All Students Take Calculus.

**A**ll positive, **S**ine positive, **T**angent positive, **C**osine positive.

For the sine values of $0^\circ, 30^\circ, 45^\circ, 60^\circ, 90^\circ$, use the square-root ladder: $\dfrac{\sqrt{0}}{2}, \dfrac{\sqrt{1}}{2}, \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \dfrac{\sqrt{4}}{2}$. Cosine is the same ladder in reverse.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For special-angle value questions, use the same routine: locate the quadrant, find the reference angle, recall the special-angle magnitude, then apply the sign. For radians, compare with $\dfrac\pi2$, $\pi$, and $\dfrac{3\pi}{2}$.

For the special angles emphasized in this lesson, sine and cosine values come from the set $0$, $\pm\dfrac12$, $\pm\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$, $\pm\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, and $\pm1$. This is a shortcut for these common angles, not a list of all possible sine or cosine values.

In the first quadrant, sine increases from $0$ to $1$ while cosine decreases from $1$ to $0$. This can help distinguish $\sin30^\circ$ from $\sin60^\circ$ if you forget which is larger.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Right Magnitude, Wrong Sign

> The reference angle gives the magnitude; the quadrant gives the sign.

For example, $\cos120^\circ$ has the same magnitude as $\cos60^\circ$ but is negative because $120^\circ$ lies in Quadrant II.

### #2: Swapping Sine and Cosine Coordinates

> The unit-circle point is $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$: cosine first.

Coordinates are ordered $(x,y)$. Cosine supplies the $x$-coordinate and sine supplies the $y$-coordinate.

### #3: The Reference-Angle Formula Depends on the Quadrant

> Measure the positive acute angle from the terminal side to the $x$-axis.

For an angle between $0^\circ$ and $360^\circ$, use the appropriate formula for its quadrant. For angles outside one full turn, first find a coterminal angle in that range.

### #4: Tangent Is Undefined on the $y$-Axis

> $\tan90^\circ$ and $\tan270^\circ$ are undefined.

At those angles cosine is $0$, so $\tan\theta=\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$ would require division by zero. Tangent is $0$ where sine is $0$, such as at $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$.
