**2. Article 10 doctrine: the strongest supporting analogy and its limits.**

***People v. Bulu Chowdury***, **G.R. Nos. 129577–80, February 15, 2000 — closest participation analogy.**

RA 8042 identified principals, accomplices, and accessories as persons criminally liable for illegal recruitment without defining those categories. The Supreme Court supplied the RPC definitions:

> “The Revised Penal Code which supplements the law on illegal recruitment defines who are the principals, accomplices and accessories.”

The Court set out Articles 17–19 and ultimately acquitted Chowdury because knowing and intentional participation was not established. [Decision, Lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2000/feb2000/gr_129577_2000.html).

The analogy to RA 6713 is strong: both statutes expressly use technical categories of participation without independently defining them. **But *Chowdury* applies the definitions; it does not decide whether Article 20’s personal exemption accompanies them.**

***Evangeline Ladonga v. People***, **G.R. No. 141066, February 17, 2005 — BP 22 and conspiracy.**

The Court held that RPC conspiracy doctrine could apply to BP 22, explaining:

> “the general provisions of the RPC which, by their nature, are necessarily applicable, may be applied suppletorily.”

It acquitted Ladonga because the prosecution failed to prove her participation in a conspiracy to issue the checks. **This was not an Article 20 exemption for the issuer’s wife.** [Decision, Lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2005/feb2005/gr_141066_2005.html).

Its significance is that special-law status does not categorically exclude substantive RPC doctrines. Compatibility must be examined provision by provision.

***Benjamin T. Romualdez v. Simeon V. Marcelo***, **G.R. Nos. 165510–33, July 28, 2006, Resolution — supplementation requires a gap.**

In an RA 3019 prosecution, the majority refused to import Article 91’s prescription rule because Act No. 3326 supplied the applicable regime. Article 10, it explained:

> “finds relevance only when the provisions of the special law are silent on a particular matter”

The majority also discussed *Chowdury* as a proper example of supplementation: the special law used participation categories without defining them. [Resolution, Lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2006/jul2006/gr_165510_2006.html).

**This is not an Article 20 ruling under RA 3019.** It frames the interpretive question for RA 6713: does Section 11(c) leave personal exemptions unresolved, or does its liability language already dispose of that issue?

***People v. Martin Simon y Sunga***, **G.R. No. 93028, July 29, 1994 — incompatibility limits supplementation.**

In its discussion of penalties under RA 6425, as amended, the Court stated that supplementary application:

> “cannot be invoked where there is a legal or physical impossibility of, or a prohibition in the special law against, such supplementary application.”

[Decision, Lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/jul1994/gr_93028_1994.html).

*Simon* supplies an important limitation, but **its discussion of special laws adopting RPC penalty terminology does not itself decide the applicability of Article 20**. *Ladonga* and *Chowdury* address substantive participation doctrines beyond penalty computation.

***People v. Marcelino Collado et al.***, **G.R. No. 185719, June 17, 2013 — express exclusion under RA 9165.**

The Court rejected the Court of Appeals’ use of Article 19 to impose accessory liability under Section 14, RA 9165. Relying on Section 98’s restriction on RPC application, it held:

> “particularly Article 19 on Accessories, cannot be applied in determining the degree of participation and criminal liability of Ranada’s co-accused.”

The relevant co-accused were acquitted because possession and conspiracy were unproved. [Decision, Supreme Court E-Library](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55950).

**This expressly refuses Article 19 supplementation, not Article 20.** Its clearest distinguishing feature is RA 9165’s express exclusion. RA 6713 contains no equivalent exclusion.

**3. Other verified cases: useful analogies, not special-law Article 20 holdings.**

***Jackson Padiernos y Quejada, Jackie Roxas y German and Rolando Mesina y Javate v. People***, **G.R. No. 181111, August 17, 2015.**

The defendants had been convicted below as Article 19(2) accessories to illegal possession of lumber under PD 705. They removed the truck after authorities had already discovered the offense. The Supreme Court rejected that accessory characterization because the purpose of preventing discovery was absent. Nevertheless:

> “They are, however, liable for violation of Section 1(b) of P.D. 1829.”

The allegations and evidence established the independent obstruction offense. **There was no kinship or Article 20 issue.** The case therefore cannot be cited as a Supreme Court holding that Article 20 is unavailable under PD 1829. [Decision, Supreme Court E-Library](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61235).

***People v. Datu Akmad “Tato” Ampatuan, Sr.***, **G.R. No. 258710, January 27, 2025.**

The Court upheld an acquittal in the murder prosecution. As an alternative rationale, it explained that even assuming accessory participation, the accused’s relationship as son-in-law attracted the exemption:

> “Article 20 of the Revised Penal Code exempts him from liability.”

This is the Supreme Court’s own alternative reasoning, not merely its reproduction of the trial court’s conclusion. **But it concerns RPC murder, not a special-law offense.** [Decision, Supreme Court E-Library](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69975).

**5. Application to Section 11(c): the competing constructions.**

**The argument for applying Article 20 is an inference from the verified authorities:**

Section 11(c) expressly adopts accessory liability without defining it. Under *Chowdury*, Article 19 can supply the technical meaning. Article 20 then arguably supplies the personal exemption associated with that liability. On this construction, the requirement of equal penal liability determines the consequences for a **nonexempt** accessory; it does not necessarily abolish an exemption. RA 6713 does not expressly address Article 20. [RA 6713, Sections 3 and 11(c)](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/2/6474).

**The counterargument is also interpretive:** Section 11(c) establishes its own liability rule, including equal penal liabilities and participation in conspiracy. Under *Romualdez* and *Simon*, supplementation cannot displace an existing, incompatible special-law rule. No located decision decides whether those words produce that incompatibility.

Two qualifications are essential:

- **Actual participation controls.** Article 20 protects qualifying accessories, not principals or accomplices. Section 11(c)’s unusual combination of conspiracy and accessory terminology requires interpretation; one cannot simply assume that every person charged under it is a technical Article 19 accessory.
- **Article 20’s own limits remain.** The qualifying relationship must fall within its specified coverage, and the Article 19(1) exception excludes accessories who profit themselves or assist the offender to profit from the effects of the crime. RA 6713’s broader definition of relatives does not expand Article 20. [RPC, Articles 19–20](https://lawphil.net/statutes/acts/act1930/act_3815_1930.html).

Accordingly, **a brief can responsibly argue for Article 20’s supplementary application, but should expressly disclose the absence of a located direct holding**. *Chowdury* is the closest supporting precedent; *Ladonga*, *Romualdez*, and *Simon* supply the governing framework. *Collado* and *Padiernos* should be distinguished on their actual statutory and participation grounds.
