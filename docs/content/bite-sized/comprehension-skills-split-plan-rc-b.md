### Part 1: Drawing Inferences from Clues
- New id: `lesson-comprehension-skills-connecting-clues`
- File: `connecting-clues.md`
- One idea: Connect text details to a supported inference.
- Teaching-word estimate: 315.
- Key points:
  1. Connect details to draw an inference.
- Examples: [1, 4]. Traps: [1]. Reading Strategies paragraphs: [1].
- Core Concept material: ## Core Concept  An **inference** is an idea you work out from clues. To **infer** means to connect those clues. The passage does not need to state the answer directly.  A **conclusion** is the idea you reach after considering evidence. Evidence means the facts or details that support an answer.  Use details from the passage and ordinary knowledge needed to understand them. Do not invent an event, motive, or cause to make an answer fit.  A good inference fits the whole passage. It should have stronger support than other possible answers.

### Part 2: Matching Conclusions to Evidence
- New id: `lesson-comprehension-skills-evidence-and-claim-strength`
- File: `evidence-and-claim-strength.md`
- One idea: Keep a conclusion within the evidence.
- Teaching-word estimate: 362.
- Key points:
  1. Match the strength of a claim to its evidence.
- Examples: [2, 3]. Traps: [2, 3]. Reading Strategies paragraphs: [2].
- Core Concept material: ## Core Concept  ### Matching the Strength of the Evidence  *May*, *likely*, and *suggests* leave room for uncertainty. *Must*, *always*, and *certainly* make stronger claims. Choose the strength that the evidence supports.  A possible event is not always a supported inference. A late assignment could have many causes. Without more information, lateness does not prove laziness.  A direct fact may be the only safe conclusion in a question about what is justified. But if a question asks for an inference, look for an idea reached by connecting details.

### Part 3: Finding Necessary Assumptions
- New id: `lesson-comprehension-skills-necessary-assumptions`
- File: `necessary-assumptions.md`
- One idea: Test the unstated link an argument needs.
- Teaching-word estimate: 289.
- Key points:
  1. Identify premises and conclusions.
  2. Test a necessary assumption by imagining its opposite.
- Examples: [5]. Traps: [4]. Reading Strategies paragraphs: [3].
- Core Concept material: ## Core Concept  ### Premises and Necessary Assumptions  A **premise** is a reason or fact used in an argument. The argument's **conclusion** is the claim those reasons are meant to support.  A **necessary assumption** is an unstated idea the argument needs. It connects the given reasons to the conclusion.  To test a proposed assumption, imagine its opposite. Keep the stated facts unchanged. If those facts can no longer support the conclusion, the assumption is needed.  This test concerns the reasoning. A conclusion might still turn out to be true for some other reason. Also, a helpful detail is not always necessary: the argument may still work without it.

### Part 4: Drawing Conclusions from Visual Data
- New id: `lesson-comprehension-skills-visual-data-conclusions`
- File: `visual-data-conclusions.md`
- One idea: Use labels, scales, and notes together.
- Teaching-word estimate: 404.
- Key points:
  1. Read a chart using its labels and scale.
  2. Combine a written limit with visual data.
- Examples: [6, 7]. Traps: [5]. Reading Strategies paragraphs: [4].
- Core Concept material: ## Core Concept  ### Reading Visual Information  Graphs, maps, diagrams, and charts arrange information outside ordinary paragraphs. Read the title and labels first. A **legend** explains symbols, and a **unit** tells what is being counted or measured.  In a bar graph, compare bar lengths using the same scale. A scale tells how much each step or symbol represents. If each square stands for $5$ visitors, count the squares and multiply by $5$.  Use written notes together with visual data. If a note gives a limit, compare the graph's values with that limit. A graph of counts alone does not explain why the counts changed.

## fact-vs-opinion

### Part 1: Distinguishing Checkable Claims and Judgments
- New id: `lesson-comprehension-skills-checkable-claims-and-judgments`
- File: `checkable-claims-and-judgments.md`
- One idea: Classify a checkable claim, judgment, or mixed sentence.
- Teaching-word estimate: 323.
- Key points:
  1. Distinguish factual claims from opinions.
  2. Separate fact and opinion in a mixed sentence.
- Examples: [1, 3]. Traps: none. Reading Strategies paragraphs: [1, 2].
- Core Concept material: ## Core Concept  A **factual claim** is a statement you can check as true or false by using records, measurements, observation, or other evidence. A factual claim can still be wrong. The label tells you what kind of claim it is, not whether it is true.  An **opinion or value judgment** tells what someone thinks, prefers, recommends, or judges. Words such as *best, unfair, beautiful,* and *should* often point to an opinion because they depend on a person's values or standards.  A sentence can contain both kinds. “The hall opened last year and is the best building in town” has a checkable claim about when it opened and an opinion about its quality.  To **classify** a statement means to name the group it belongs to. Here, the groups are factual claim, opinion, and projection.

### Part 3: Distinguishing Projections and Forecasts
- New id: `lesson-comprehension-skills-projections-and-forecasts`
- File: `projections-and-forecasts.md`
- One idea: Separate a prediction from a fact about its publication.
- Teaching-word estimate: 393.
- Key points:
  1. Identify a forecast and its assumptions.
  2. Distinguish a forecast from a fact about that forecast.
- Examples: [4, 6]. Traps: [2, 3]. Reading Strategies paragraphs: [3].
- Core Concept material: ## Core Concept  ### Projections and Forecasts  A **projection or forecast** says what may happen in the future based on information available now. It is not a settled fact about the future.  An **assumption** is something an estimate treats as true while making the forecast. If the assumption changes, the projection may also change.  These three statements are different:  - **Fact about a projection:** “The agency published a projection of $10$ million people by $2030$.” You can check whether the agency published that projection. - **Projection itself:** “The population will reach $10$ million by $2030$.” This says what is expected to happen later. - **Opinion:** “A population of $10$ million would be terrible.” The word “terrible” gives a judgment.
