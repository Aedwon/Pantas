## 01-sidereal-and-solar-day.svg

- File: `content/illustrations/science/earth-science-astronomy/earths-motions/01-sidereal-and-solar-day.svg`
- Marker: top view of Earth at two positions on its orbit, one day apart; a dot on Earth faces a distant star at both positions, labeled "one sidereal day: 23 h 56 min"; at the second position the dot still needs to turn about 1 degree to face the Sun again, labeled "one solar day: 24 h"
- Purpose: In Rotation, show why the solar day (24 h) is a little longer than the sidereal day (23 h 56 min): Earth also moves along its orbit, so after one full turn the Sun is still about 1° ahead. This replaces an older figure that printed a false label ("a few extra degrees"). The true extra turn is about 1°. Never write "a few degrees".
- Layout: Top view, Earth shown as two circles (about 36 across) near the bottom of the frame, left one at about x = 130 and right one at about x = 230, with a small gap. Label them "day 1" and "day 2", with a thin arrow from the left to the right Earth along the orbit path. Each Earth has a dot on its rim. The Sun is a larger circle near the top center. Draw the line from the left Earth to the Sun as solid. At the left Earth, the dot sits on that line, facing the Sun and a distant star in the same direction. Draw a dashed line from the left Earth running up past the Sun direction to a small star symbol near the top, labeled "distant star". At the right Earth, draw a second dashed line parallel to the first (the star is so far away that the direction does not change), with the dot on this dashed line, rim facing the star. Label this dashed line "one sidereal day: 23 h 56 min". From the right Earth also draw a solid line to the Sun. The two lines at the right Earth form a thin wedge. Draw a short curved arrow along the right Earth's rim from the dot to the Sun line, counterclockwise as seen from above, labeled "about 1°". Label the Sun line at the right Earth "one solar day: 24 h". Draw the wedge larger than true so it is visible, and add the small note "angle not to scale" at the bottom.
- Labels (verbatim): "day 1"; "day 2"; "Sun"; "distant star"; "one sidereal day: 23 h 56 min"; "one solar day: 24 h"; "about 1°"; "angle not to scale"
- Focus (accent): The small wedge at the second Earth, with the label "about 1°".
- Must not show: Do not use "a few degrees" or any angle other than "about 1°". Do not show other times, a month of days, or the 4-minute difference. Do not show the axial tilt.
- Suggested height: 480

## 02-june-and-december-solstice-tilt.svg

- File: `content/illustrations/science/earth-science-astronomy/earths-motions/02-june-and-december-solstice-tilt.svg`
- Marker: side view of Earth at the June and December solstices, axis tilted $23.4^\circ$ and pointing the same way both times; parallel sunlight rays shown hitting the leaning-toward hemisphere at a steeper angle than the leaning-away hemisphere; labels "Northern summer, Southern winter" and "Northern winter, Southern summer"
- Purpose: In How Tilt Makes Seasons, show that the axis points the same way at both solstices, so one hemisphere leans toward the Sun and gets steeper (more direct) sunlight, and six months later the other one does.
- Layout: Side view, Sun in the middle of the frame (circle, about 40 across, at about x = 180), Earth at the left (June solstice) and at the right (December solstice), each a circle about 70 across, near x = 62 and x = 298. Seen from the side, both axes lean the same way on the page, toward the upper right: draw each axis as a line through the Earth that extends beyond it, with "N" at the top end and "S" at the bottom end, and a dashed vertical line through the center of each Earth. Show the angle between the dashed vertical and the axis, labeled "23.4°" on each. Because the Sun is to the right of the left Earth and to the left of the right Earth, the north end leans toward the Sun in June and away from it in December. Draw an equator line across each Earth, perpendicular to its axis. Draw parallel sunlight rays (4 or 5 straight arrows, evenly spaced, from the Sun toward each Earth) so they strike the lit side. On the leaning-toward hemisphere the rays meet the surface close to straight on (steeper), and on the leaning-away hemisphere they meet it at a shallow angle, spread over a longer stretch of surface. Shade the half of each Earth facing away from the Sun lightly. Put the label under each Earth, in two lines. Above each Earth write "June solstice" and "December solstice".
- Labels (verbatim): "June solstice"; "December solstice"; "Sun"; "N"; "S"; "23.4°"; "Northern summer, Southern winter"; "Northern winter, Southern summer"
- Focus (accent): The rays on the leaning-toward hemisphere, where they hit steeper.
- Must not show: Do not show equinoxes, Earth-Sun distance, any ray angle in degrees, or a temperature.
- Suggested height: 300

## 03-orion-through-the-year.svg

- File: `content/illustrations/science/earth-science-astronomy/earths-motions/03-orion-through-the-year.svg`
- Marker: top view of Earth's orbit around the Sun with four positions labeled "March", "June", "September", "December"; at each position the night side is shaded and an arrow points to the part of the star background seen at night; Orion marked on the night side in December and on the daytime side in June
- Purpose: In Seasonal Constellations, show that as Earth revolves, the night side faces different parts of the star background, so Orion is seen at night in December and is hidden in daylight in June.
- Layout: Top view. The Sun is a circle at the center (about x = 180, y = 230). The orbit is a circle of radius about 100 around it. Four Earths (circles about 28 across) sit on the orbit: "December" at the top, "June" at the bottom, "March" at the left, "September" at the right. On each Earth, shade the half that faces away from the Sun (the night side) in a darker tint and leave the half facing the Sun light. From the middle of each night side, draw an arrow pointing straight outward from the Sun, about 30 units long, toward the star background outside the orbit. Place a few small star dots near the end of each arrow. Draw Orion once, as a small cluster of 5 or 6 dots joined by thin lines, labeled "Orion", above the top arrow so that it is at the end of the December arrow. Write the note "Sun side: Orion lost in daylight" beside the June Earth, with a thin dashed line from the June Earth's lit half up toward the Sun. Next to the December Earth write "night side: Orion seen". Put each month name beside its Earth, outside the orbit where it fits, not on the arrows. Add "Sun" inside the Sun circle. No arrow is needed for orbit direction.
- Labels (verbatim): "March"; "June"; "September"; "December"; "Sun"; "Orion"; "night side: Orion seen"; "Sun side: Orion lost in daylight"
- Focus (accent): The Orion cluster and the December night-side arrow pointing to it.
- Must not show: Do not show the equinox or solstice dates, the axial tilt, latitude, or other constellations. Do not mark Orion on the night side at June.
- Suggested height: 400

## 04-rotation-and-revolution.svg

- File: `content/illustrations/science/earth-science-astronomy/earths-motions/04-rotation-and-revolution.svg`
- Marker: Earth shown twice: left, a curved arrow around the axis labeled "rotation: day and night"; right, Earth at two points on its orbit around the Sun labeled "revolution: seasons and the year"
- Purpose: In Example #1, place the two motions side by side: rotation (spin on the axis, day and night) and revolution (the trip around the Sun, seasons and the year). The marker follows the example's solution, so the labels may name both effects.
- Layout: Two panels side by side, each about 156 wide, with a gap of 16 between them. Left panel: Earth as a circle about 80 across, with a straight axis line through it from the north end to the south end, extending about 20 beyond the circle at both ends; a curved arrow circles the Earth around the axis (counterclockwise as seen from above the north end, drawn as an arc with an arrowhead around the upper part of the globe). Under it, the label "rotation: day and night" in two lines. Right panel: a small Sun (circle about 30 across) at the center of the panel, a circular orbit of radius about 56 around it, and two small Earths (about 20 across) on the orbit at the left and right ends. Add an arrow along part of the orbit to show the direction of travel. Under it, the label "revolution: seasons and the year" in three lines. Both labels sit on the same baseline.
- Labels (verbatim): "rotation: day and night"; "revolution: seasons and the year"; "Sun"
- Focus (accent): The curved rotation arrow around the axis on the left, and the orbit arrow on the right, drawn in the same accent weight.
- Must not show: Do not show precession, axial tilt angles, dates, or the distance between Earth and Sun.
- Suggested height: 240

## 05-orbit-closest-and-farthest.svg

- File: `content/illustrations/science/earth-science-astronomy/earths-motions/05-orbit-closest-and-farthest.svg`
- Marker: Earth's orbit around the Sun, slightly oval, with Earth in early January labeled "closest to the Sun, Northern winter" and in early July labeled "farthest from the Sun, Northern summer"
- Purpose: In Example #3, show that Earth is nearest the Sun in January, when the north has winter, and farthest in July, when the north has summer, so distance does not cause the seasons.
- Layout: Orbit drawn as a gently oval ellipse, about 270 wide and 220 tall, centered in the frame. The Sun (circle about 36 across) sits inside the ellipse a little to the right of center, close to the right end, so the right end of the orbit is nearer to the Sun than the left end. Draw the ellipse a bit more oval than the true orbit so the difference can be seen. Place Earth (circle about 22 across) on the right end of the ellipse, labeled "early January", and on the left end, labeled "early July". Dashed thin lines from the Sun to each Earth show the distance. Put the first long label at the bottom right, under the right Earth, in two lines: "closest to the Sun," then "Northern winter". Put the second long label at the top left, above the left Earth, in two lines: "farthest from the Sun," then "Northern summer". Use a thin leader line from each label to its Earth.
- Labels (verbatim): "Sun"; "early January"; "early July"; "closest to the Sun, Northern winter"; "farthest from the Sun, Northern summer"
- Focus (accent): The two dashed distance lines, the short one to January and the long one to July.
- Must not show: Do not show distances in kilometers, the axial tilt, other months, or any statement that distance causes the seasons.
- Suggested height: 340

## 07-axis-precession-cone.svg

- File: `content/illustrations/science/earth-science-astronomy/earths-motions/07-axis-precession-cone.svg`
- Marker: Earth with its axis tilted $23.4^\circ$ drawing a slow circle in space, a cone, labeled "one cycle about 26,000 years"; the axis points toward Polaris now and toward Vega later
- Purpose: In Precession (Example #8), show that the axis keeps the same tilt but slowly traces a cone, so the star it points to changes over time. The marker follows the example's solution.
- Layout: Earth as a circle (about 60 across) near the bottom center. Through its center, a dashed vertical line (straight up) and the tilted axis line leaning to the right by 23.4°, extended up about 150 units; label the angle between them "23.4°" with a small arc. Above, draw a large flat oval (a circle seen at a slant, about 200 wide and 60 tall) centered over the dashed vertical line. Draw two solid lines from the Earth's center to the left and right ends of the oval, outlining the cone, and a third (accent) line from the Earth's center to the point on the oval where the axis points now. On the oval, mark that point "Polaris" with a star symbol and the word "now". Mark the point on the opposite side of the oval "Vega" with a star symbol, "later" and "in about 12,000 years" in small text under it. Draw arrowheads on the oval showing the direction of the slow circle. Put the label "one cycle about 26,000 years" inside or just below the oval in two lines if needed.
- Labels (verbatim): "23.4°"; "Polaris"; "now"; "Vega"; "later"; "in about 12,000 years"; "one cycle about 26,000 years"
- Focus (accent): The axis line pointing to Polaris, with the 23.4° angle.
- Must not show: Do not show Thuban, a date for Polaris, the changing tilt angle, or the orbit around the Sun. Do not show the seasons.
- Suggested height: 360
