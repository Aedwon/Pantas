## Core Concept

Figure Grouping teaches how to find a shared rule. Figure Matrices teaches how to check rows and columns. Here, use those skills to compare counts and solve more kinds of figure questions.

A **count relationship** connects two counts in a drawing. The dots might equal the sides, or there might be one fewer dot. Compare the counts within each drawing before comparing drawings.

A **one-of-each rule** means a row or column contains each listed kind exactly once. Shape and stripe direction can follow separate rules.

A **visual analogy** asks you to make the same change to another figure. Find what changes in the first pair. Track shape, fill, and openings separately.

A **solid** has length, width, and depth. Its outside can have flat faces or curved surfaces. A **net** is a flat pattern that folds into the outside of a solid without faces overlapping. Mental Rotation and 3D Visualization teaches cube nets. Here, we will fold a different solid.

## Examples

### #1: Connecting Two Counts

Which drawing breaks the shared count rule?

A shows a triangle with $2$ dots. B shows a pentagon with $4$ dots. C shows an octagon with $7$ dots. D shows a hexagon with $6$ dots. An octagon has $8$ sides; a hexagon has $6$.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: four separate panels labeled A triangle with 2 outside dots, B pentagon with 4 outside dots, C octagon with 7 outside dots, D hexagon with 6 outside dots; no count labels or highlighted answer -->

**Solution:**

1. Compare sides and dots in A: $3$ sides and $2$ dots. There is $1$ fewer dot.
2. B has $5$ sides and $4$ dots. C has $8$ sides and $7$ dots. Both fit that difference.
3. D has $6$ sides and $6$ dots. D breaks the rule of one fewer dot than sides.

Now compare panels of repeated shapes. P contains $4$ triangles. Q contains $6$ pentagons. R contains $8$ octagons. Which panel breaks the shared rule?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: panels P with 4 separate outline triangles, Q with 6 separate outline pentagons, R with 8 separate outline octagons; equal-sized shapes within each panel, no answer labels -->

**Solution:**

1. In P, each triangle has $3$ sides, and there are $4$ triangles. There is $1$ more shape than sides.
2. In Q, each pentagon has $5$ sides, and there are $6$ pentagons. The same difference holds.
3. In R, each octagon has $8$ sides, so the rule would need $9$ octagons. It has $8$. R breaks the rule.

Count the sides of one repeated shape. Do not add the sides of all the shapes together.

### #2: Closed Solid or Open Surface?

Four objects are drawn: A, a closed cone with its circular base attached; B, a sealed rectangular box; C, a sheet folded into a V with no end covers; and D, a ball with no holes. Which differs by whether its surface closes around a space?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: A closed cone including circular base, B sealed rectangular box, C V-folded sheet open at top and both ends, D unbroken sphere; show hidden edges with dotted lines and do not highlight an answer -->

**Solution:**

1. A's curved surface and base close around a space. B's faces do too.
2. C has uncovered gaps above and at its ends. Its folded sheet does not close around a space.
3. D's curved surface is closed. C is the different object.

A dotted edge can show an edge hidden behind a solid. It does not mean the solid has a hole.

### #3: Grids with Several Blanks

A $3 \times 3$ grid must have one triangle, one circle, and one square in each row and each column. Fill its blanks.

Row $1$: triangle, circle, square.

Row $2$: circle, blank, blank.

Row $3$: blank, triangle, blank.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: 3 by 3 grid, row 1 outline triangle circle square, row 2 outline circle blank blank, row 3 blank outline triangle blank; label rows and columns 1 through 3 outside grid; leave all blanks empty -->

**Solution:**

1. Column $1$ already has a triangle and circle. Put a square in row $3$, column $1$.
2. Row $3$ now has a square and triangle. Its last cell needs a circle.
3. Column $3$ now has a square and circle. Its middle cell needs a triangle.
4. Row $2$ now has a circle and triangle. Put a square in its middle cell.
5. Check the completed rows: triangle-circle-square; circle-square-triangle; square-triangle-circle. Each column also has one of each.

Now each cell has a shape on a striped background. The shapes are square, circle, and triangle. The stripe directions are horizontal, vertical, and slanting upward to the right.

Each row and column must use each shape once and each stripe direction once. What fills the blank?

Row $1$: square on horizontal stripes; circle on vertical stripes; triangle on slanting stripes.

Row $2$: triangle on vertical stripes; square on slanting stripes; circle on horizontal stripes.

Row $3$: circle on slanting stripes; triangle on horizontal stripes; blank.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: 3 by 3 grid with outlined central shapes over stripe backgrounds; row 1 square horizontal, circle vertical, triangle rising-right diagonal; row 2 triangle vertical, square rising-right diagonal, circle horizontal; row 3 circle rising-right diagonal, triangle horizontal, blank -->

**Solution:**

1. Check shapes in row $3$. Circle and triangle are present, so the blank needs a square.
2. Check stripes in row $3$. Slanting and horizontal are present, so it needs vertical stripes.
3. Column $3$ confirms both results. It already has triangle and circle, with slanting and horizontal stripes.
4. Fill the blank with a square on vertical stripes.

The square has different backgrounds in different cells. Solve shape and stripes separately before putting them together.

### #4: Continuing Lines Across a Missing Piece

A square hole interrupts a pattern. A horizontal line reaches its left and right edges two-thirds of the way down. A vertical line reaches its top and bottom edges three-quarters of the way across from the left.

Two square pieces are available. A has a horizontal line two-thirds down and a vertical line three-quarters across. B has a horizontal line one-quarter down and a vertical line two-thirds across. Each line runs from edge to edge.

Which piece fits without turning? Can the other piece fit after a turn?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: square hole in larger pattern, horizontal line stubs at 2/3 height on left and right, vertical stubs at 3/4 width on top and bottom; separate square A horizontal at 2/3 height vertical at 3/4 width; separate square B horizontal at 1/4 height vertical at 2/3 width; use equal square sizes, no rotation arrows or fitted answers -->

**Solution:**

1. Match the entry points at the hole's edges. A's horizontal line joins both side stubs. Its vertical line joins the top and bottom stubs. A fits without turning.
2. Imagine turning B a quarter-turn clockwise. Its horizontal line becomes vertical. Being one-quarter below the old top puts it one-quarter left of the new right edge, or three-quarters across.
3. B's vertical line becomes horizontal. Being two-thirds across from the old left puts it two-thirds down from the new top.
4. B also fits after that turn. Check all four joins: left, right, top, and bottom.

A line must meet its neighbor at the same place and continue in the right direction. With curved lines, check that the curve continues smoothly too.

### #5: Transferring a Change in an Analogy

The first pair shows a horizontally striped hexagon containing a black triangle. It changes to a black hexagon containing a vertically striped triangle.

Apply that change to a vertically striped square containing a black oval. What should it become?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: analogy pair horizontal-striped hexagon enclosing black triangle, arrow to black hexagon enclosing vertical-striped triangle; below vertical-striped square enclosing black oval, arrow to question mark; keep inner shapes visibly separate from outer boundaries -->

**Solution:**

1. The outer shape takes the inner shape's black fill.
2. The inner shape takes the outer stripes, turned a quarter-turn. Horizontal stripes become vertical.
3. For the new pair, make the square black. Transfer its vertical stripes to the oval and turn them horizontal.
4. The answer is a black square containing a horizontally striped oval. Neither outline changes shape.

A different analogy shows a plain flat square becoming a cube with no openings. A plain flat circle must become either a sphere or a flat ring. Which fits the same flat-to-solid relationship? A sphere is a ball-shaped solid.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: plain flat square arrow to closed cube; plain flat circle arrow to question mark; choices unbroken sphere and flat circular ring, both unshaded -->

**Solution:**

1. The first pair connects a flat shape to a solid. It does not show paper folding.
2. The sphere is solid. The ring remains flat. Choose the sphere.

Now a square with one central square opening becomes a cube with one square tunnel running straight from front to back. A circle with one central circular opening follows the same rule. Choose a sphere with no tunnel, one round tunnel, or two separate round tunnels.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: flat square frame with one central square opening arrow to cube with one straight square front-to-back tunnel; flat circular ring arrow to question mark; choices sphere without hole, sphere with one circular straight tunnel, sphere with two separate parallel circular tunnels; make openings clear -->

**Solution:**

1. The first pair changes the flat shape to a solid and keeps one opening as one tunnel.
2. Use the circle-to-sphere relationship. Keep its single circular opening as one round tunnel.
3. Choose the sphere with one round tunnel. The other choices lose the opening or add another.

These are rules shown by these pairs. A circle does not have to mean a sphere in a different question.

### #6: Folding a Marked Triangular Prism

The **triangular prism** here has $2$ matching triangular ends joined by $3$ rectangular faces. A face is one flat surface of the solid.

The target has matching equilateral triangular ends, whose sides are equal. One rectangular face has a black dot. The triangles are unmarked. Which pattern folds into it?

A has $3$ equal rectangles side by side in a strip. Each is $4$ centimeters tall and $2$ centimeters wide. Equilateral triangles of side $2$ centimeters attach above and below the middle rectangle. The left rectangle has a dot.

B is the same pattern with a fourth rectangle added at the right. C matches A, but its dot is on the upper triangle.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: target closed triangular prism with unmarked equilateral triangular ends and dot on one rectangular side; nets A three side-by-side 4 cm tall 2 cm wide rectangles with 2 cm equilateral triangles above and below middle rectangle, dot on left rectangle; B same plus fourth rectangle at right; C same as A with dot on upper triangle instead -->

**Solution:**

1. The target needs $3$ rectangles and $2$ triangles. B has an extra rectangle.
2. Fold A's left and right rectangles around the middle one. Their free tall edges meet, making the three-sided tube.
3. Fold the triangles over its opposite ends. Their $2$-centimeter sides fit the short rectangle edges. The prism closes without overlapping faces.
4. A's dot stays on a rectangular face. C puts it on a triangular end, so C fails the marking rule. Choose A.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Choosing by One Count

> Compare the two counts within each figure.

Different dot counts can fit the same side-to-dot rule. Check the relationship.

### #2: Stopping After One Feature

> The answer must satisfy the complete rule.

Check both shape and stripes in a grid. For a net, check the folds and the marked face.

### #3: Fitting Just One Edge

> Check the full boundary of a pattern piece.

One matching line does not prove the piece fits. Continue around its edges after any turn.
