## Core Concept

A **wave** is a traveling disturbance or oscillation that transfers energy and momentum. In a mechanical wave, the material of the medium generally oscillates around equilibrium instead of traveling permanently with the disturbance.

### Wave Properties

**Wavelength** ($\lambda$): the distance between adjacent points in the same phase, such as crest to crest for a sinusoidal transverse wave.

**Frequency** ($f$): the number of cycles per second, measured in **hertz** ($Hz$).

**Amplitude**: the maximum size of the disturbance relative to equilibrium. For many linear waves, the transported energy or power grows with the **square** of amplitude, so doubling amplitude can have a much larger effect than simply doubling energy.

**Wave speed** ($v$): for a periodic wave,

$$v=f\lambda$$

The wave speed is set by the medium and physical conditions for mechanical waves, and by the electromagnetic properties of the medium for light. If the wave enters a new medium and its speed changes while the source frequency remains fixed, its wavelength changes accordingly.

### Mechanical vs. Electromagnetic Waves

A **mechanical wave** is a disturbance that propagates through matter. Sound in air, seismic waves, waves on strings, and water-surface waves are mechanical waves.

An **electromagnetic wave** is a propagating disturbance of electric and magnetic fields. It does **not** require a material medium and can travel through vacuum. Radio waves, visible light, X-rays, and gamma rays are electromagnetic waves.

For mechanical waves, classification as **transverse** or **longitudinal** refers to the direction of the medium's disturbance compared with the propagation direction. In a transverse wave, the disturbance is perpendicular to the direction of travel; in a longitudinal wave, it is parallel. Sound in fluids is longitudinal. Waves in solids can include both longitudinal and transverse modes. Electromagnetic waves are transverse in the sense that their electric and magnetic fields oscillate perpendicular to the direction of propagation—not because particles of a medium move sideways.

### Sound

Sound is a mechanical pressure disturbance. Its speed depends on the medium and its state, including temperature. In dry air near $20°C$, a commonly used value is about $343\text{ m/s}$, not one universal speed for all conditions.

For a sinusoidal sound wave, **frequency** is closely related to perceived pitch, while intensity depends on transported power per area. Perceived loudness is more complicated than amplitude alone and also depends on frequency and human hearing.

### The Doppler Effect

The **Doppler effect** is a change in observed frequency caused by relative motion between a source and observer. For sound, motion of the source and motion of the observer affect the observed frequency in different ways because sound propagates relative to its medium.

Approach generally raises the observed frequency; separation lowers it. The speed of sound relative to the surrounding medium is set mainly by the medium's properties, but an observer moving through the medium measures wavefronts arriving at a different rate.

## Examples

### #1: Calculating Wave Speed

A wave has a frequency of $5\text{ Hz}$ and a wavelength of $2\text{ m}$. Find its speed.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "concept-diagram", "type": "wave", "labels": {"wavelength": "2 m", "frequency": "5 Hz"}, "semanticsLabel": "A transverse wave diagram with a 2 m wavelength and frequency of 5 Hz."} -->

**Solution:**

$$v=f\lambda=(5)(2)=10\text{ m/s}$$

### #2: Finding Wavelength from Speed and Frequency

A sound wave travels at $340\text{ m/s}$ with a frequency of $170\text{ Hz}$. Find its wavelength.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "concept-diagram", "type": "wave", "labels": {"wavelength": "?", "speed": "340 m/s", "frequency": "170 Hz"}, "semanticsLabel": "A sound wave diagram with wavelength marked as a question mark, speed 340 m/s, and frequency 170 Hz."} -->

**Solution:**

$$\lambda=\frac{v}{f}=\frac{340}{170}=2\text{ m}$$

### #3: Mechanical or Electromagnetic?

Classify each wave: (a) sound traveling through air; (b) sunlight traveling through interplanetary vacuum.

**Solution:**

1. Sound requires matter through which the pressure disturbance propagates: **mechanical**.
2. Light is an oscillation of electromagnetic fields and can propagate through vacuum: **electromagnetic**.

### #4: Applying the Doppler Effect

An ambulance siren sounds higher in pitch while approaching a stationary observer and lower after receding. Explain why.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: a moving ambulance with sound wavefronts bunched together (shorter wavelength, higher pitch) ahead of it and spread apart (longer wavelength, lower pitch) behind it -->

**Solution:**

1. A moving source emits successive wavefronts from different positions.
2. In front of the approaching source, the wavefront spacing in the air is reduced, so a stationary observer encounters them more frequently.
3. Behind the receding source, the wavefront spacing is increased, so the observer encounters them less frequently.
4. The observed frequency therefore rises during approach and falls during recession.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Use $v=f\lambda$ only after identifying which speed, frequency, and wavelength belong to the same wave in the same medium.

For mechanical versus electromagnetic waves, ask whether a material medium is required. Sound requires matter; electromagnetic radiation does not.

For Doppler questions, first identify **who is moving relative to the medium**: source, observer, or both. “Approaching means higher observed frequency” is a reliable direction check, but the quantitative formula depends on the motion setup.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Saying a Wave Carries the Medium Along With It

> In an ordinary mechanical wave, particles of the medium oscillate about equilibrium while the disturbance propagates.

A water wave can move across a surface while individual water parcels mainly move locally rather than traveling with the crest across the entire basin.

### #2: Describing Light as Particles of a Medium Moving Transversely

> Electromagnetic waves are transverse oscillations of electric and magnetic fields.

They do not need material particles to oscillate, which is why light can cross vacuum.

### #3: Assuming Bigger Amplitude Means Proportionally Bigger Energy

> For many linear waves, energy or power is proportional to amplitude squared.

Doubling amplitude can therefore correspond to roughly four times the transported energy or power, depending on the wave system.

### #4: Treating the Speed of Sound as Always $340\text{ m/s}$

> Sound speed depends on the medium and its state.

Values near $340$–$343\text{ m/s}$ are common approximations for room-temperature air, not universal constants.

### #5: Saying the Doppler Effect Makes Sound Travel Faster Through Air

> Source motion changes the spacing of emitted wavefronts; observer motion changes the rate at which wavefronts are encountered.

The propagation speed relative to the medium is governed mainly by the medium, not by whether the source is approaching the listener.
