## Core Concept

A **wave** is a disturbance that travels from place to place and carries energy. A **medium** is the material the wave moves through, such as air, water, or a rope. In a simple mechanical wave, each bit of the medium only moves back and forth around its **equilibrium** (rest) position. The disturbance travels on, but the material does not travel with it.

### Wave Properties

An **oscillation** is a repeating back-and-forth motion. One full repeat is one **cycle**. The **period** ($T$) is the time for one cycle.

A **crest** is a high point of the wave. A **trough** is a low point.

**Wavelength** ($\lambda$, in meters): the distance along the wave from one point to the next point that is at the same stage of its cycle. For a sine-shaped wave, this is crest to crest.

**Frequency** ($f$): the number of cycles each second. Its unit is the **hertz** ($\text{Hz}$). One hertz is one cycle per second. Larger units are common: $1\text{ kHz}=1000\text{ Hz}$ and $1\text{ MHz}=1\,000\,000\text{ Hz}$. Period and frequency are linked by $f=\dfrac{1}{T}$.

**Amplitude** ($A$): the largest displacement from equilibrium. A bigger amplitude means a more energetic wave. For waves of the same frequency in the same medium, the power the wave carries is proportional to the **square** of the amplitude. Doubling the amplitude gives about four times the power.

**Wave speed** ($v$) is how fast the pattern travels. In one period the pattern moves one wavelength, so

$$v=f\lambda$$

Check the units: hertz times meters gives meters per second. The speed is set by the medium and its conditions, not by the source. For a rope, a tighter rope gives a faster wave. For sound in air, a warmer day gives a faster wave.

If the source frequency rises, the wavelength shrinks so that $f\lambda$ stays the same. This holds for sound in air and for waves on a rope. School problems keep the speed fixed unless they say otherwise.

When a wave moves into a new medium, the source has not changed, so the frequency stays the same. The speed changes, so the wavelength changes by the same factor.

### Mechanical vs. Electromagnetic Waves

A **mechanical wave** needs a medium. Sound, seismic waves (waves from earthquakes), waves on a string, and water-surface waves are mechanical. Sound cannot travel through empty space.

An **electromagnetic wave** is a traveling disturbance in electric and magnetic fields. It needs no medium and can cross a vacuum (empty space). Radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays are all electromagnetic waves. In a vacuum, all of them travel at the same speed, about $3.00\times10^{8}\text{ m/s}$. This is the speed of light, written $c$.

In ordinary matter, light travels slower than $c$. The speed also differs between the colors of visible light in glass.

The electromagnetic **spectrum** lists these waves in order. Starting from the longest wavelength, the order is: radio, microwave, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma ray. Frequency runs the opposite way, so radio has the lowest frequency and gamma rays the highest. The edges between neighbors blur, and the X-ray and gamma ranges overlap.

Inside visible light, red has the longest wavelength and violet the shortest. Visible wavelengths are roughly $400\text{ nm}$ to $700\text{ nm}$, where $1\text{ nm}=10^{-9}\text{ m}$.

Because $c=f\lambda$ in a vacuum, a wave with a longer wavelength has a lower frequency. The optics lesson covers light in more depth.

### Transverse vs. Longitudinal Waves

These words describe how the disturbance moves compared with the direction the wave travels.

In a **transverse wave**, the disturbance is at right angles to the travel direction. Shake a rope up and down, and the wave runs along the rope.

In a **longitudinal wave**, the disturbance is parallel to the travel direction. Push a spring toy back and forth along its length, and bunched-up coils travel along it. A bunched region is a **compression**. A stretched region is a **rarefaction**.

Sound in air and in other fluids (gases and liquids) is longitudinal. A solid can carry both kinds. Earthquakes show this: the faster P waves are longitudinal, and the slower S waves are transverse. S waves cannot pass through liquids.

Water-surface waves are a mix. Each bit of water moves in a small loop, up and down and also back and forth.

Electromagnetic waves are called transverse because their electric and magnetic fields point at right angles to the travel direction. No matter moves sideways.

### Sound

**Sound** in air is a longitudinal mechanical wave. A vibrating object pushes on the medium, and a pattern of compressions and rarefactions travels outward.

The speed of sound depends on the medium and its temperature. In dry air it is about $331\text{ m/s}$ at $0^\circ\text{C}$ and about $343\text{ m/s}$ at $20^\circ\text{C}$. Sound in water is much faster, about $1480\text{ m/s}$ at $20^\circ\text{C}$. Sound is usually faster in liquids and solids than in gases. This is a general pattern, not a law: some soft solids, such as foam or soft rubber, carry sound more slowly than water does.

**Pitch** is how high or low a sound seems. A higher frequency gives a higher pitch. Most young people hear from about $20\text{ Hz}$ to $20\,000\text{ Hz}$. Frequencies above that are called ultrasound, and those below are called infrasound.

**Intensity** is the power the sound carries through each square meter. At the same frequency, a larger amplitude means a larger intensity. **Loudness** is how strong the sound seems to a listener. It follows intensity but also depends on the pitch and on the listener's ears.

So amplitude is linked to loudness and frequency is linked to pitch. These are two different properties.

## Examples

### #1: Calculating Wave Speed

A wave has a frequency of $5\text{ Hz}$ and a wavelength of $2\text{ m}$. Find its speed.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "concept-diagram", "type": "wave", "labels": {"wavelength": "2 m", "frequency": "5 Hz"}, "semanticsLabel": "A transverse wave diagram with a 2 m wavelength and frequency of 5 Hz."} -->

**Solution:**

$$v=f\lambda=(5)(2)=10\text{ m/s}$$

### #2: Finding Wavelength from Speed and Frequency

A sound wave travels at $340\text{ m/s}$ with a frequency of $170\text{ Hz}$. Find its wavelength.

<!-- FIGURE: {"kind": "concept-diagram", "type": "wave", "labels": {"wavelength": "?", "speed": "340 m/s", "frequency": "170 Hz"}, "semanticsLabel": "A sound wave diagram with wavelength marked as a question mark, speed 340 m/s, and frequency 170 Hz."} -->

**Solution:**

$$\lambda=\dfrac{v}{f}=\dfrac{340}{170}=2\text{ m}$$

### #3: Mechanical or Electromagnetic?

Classify each wave: (a) sound traveling through air; (b) sunlight traveling through interplanetary vacuum; (c) a seismic wave in the Earth.

**Solution:**

1. Sound needs matter for the pressure disturbance to travel through: **mechanical**.
2. Light is an oscillation of electric and magnetic fields and can cross a vacuum: **electromagnetic**.
3. A seismic wave moves through rock, a material medium: **mechanical**.

### #4: Transverse or Longitudinal?

Classify each wave by its disturbance and travel direction: (a) a rope is flicked up and down and a pulse runs along the rope; (b) a speaker pushes the air back and forth along the line to a listener; (c) visible light.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: three small sketches labeled (a) a rope pulse with a hand flicking it up and down, (b) a speaker pushing air toward a listener with compressions along the line, (c) a ray of light -->

**Solution:**

1. In (a), the rope moves up and down while the pulse moves along the rope. The motion is at right angles to travel: **transverse**.
2. In (b), the air moves back and forth along the direction the sound travels: **longitudinal**.
3. In (c), the electric and magnetic fields point at right angles to the travel direction: **transverse**, with no medium moving.

### #5: Wave Entering a New Medium

A sound of frequency $500\text{ Hz}$ travels in air at $343\text{ m/s}$ and then enters water, where the speed is $1480\text{ m/s}$. Find the wavelength in each medium and the frequency in the water.

**Solution:**

1. Find the wavelength in air:

$$\lambda_{\text{air}}=\dfrac{343}{500}\approx0.69\text{ m}$$

2. The source has not changed, so the frequency in the water is still $500\text{ Hz}$.

3. Find the wavelength in water:

$$\lambda_{\text{water}}=\dfrac{1480}{500}\approx2.96\text{ m}$$

The wavelength is about $3.0\text{ m}$ in water, longer than in air because the speed is greater.

### #6: Pitch and Loudness

A speaker plays a note of $440\text{ Hz}$ in air at $20^\circ\text{C}$, where sound travels at $343\text{ m/s}$. Find the wavelength. Then the speaker plays $880\text{ Hz}$ at the same intensity. Then it plays $440\text{ Hz}$ again at twice the amplitude. Describe each change.

**Solution:**

1. Find the first wavelength:

$$\lambda=\dfrac{v}{f}=\dfrac{343}{440}\approx0.78\text{ m}$$

2. At $880\text{ Hz}$ in the same air, the speed is unchanged, so the wavelength is halved, to about $0.39\text{ m}$. The pitch is higher. The intensity was kept the same, so only the pitch changes.

3. At twice the amplitude and the same frequency, the pitch and wavelength stay the same. The intensity is about $4$ times larger, so the sound is louder.

### #7: Order on the Spectrum

Which has the higher frequency, a microwave or ultraviolet light? Which has the longer wavelength, X-rays or infrared?

**Solution:**

1. Frequency rises from radio toward gamma rays. Ultraviolet comes after microwave in that order. So **ultraviolet** has the higher frequency.
2. Wavelength falls in that same direction. Infrared comes before X-rays, so **infrared** has the longer wavelength.

### #8: Radio Wave Wavelength

An FM station broadcasts at $100\text{ MHz}$. Find the wavelength of its signal in air.

**Solution:**

1. Convert the frequency:

$$f=100\text{ MHz}=1.00\times10^{8}\text{ Hz}$$

2. Radio waves are electromagnetic, so they travel at about $c=3.00\times10^{8}\text{ m/s}$ in air or a vacuum:

$$\lambda=\dfrac{c}{f}=\dfrac{3.00\times10^{8}}{1.00\times10^{8}}=3.00\text{ m}$$

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Use $v=f\lambda$ only for the speed, frequency, and wavelength of the same wave in the same medium. Check that the units are hertz, meters, and meters per second.

In one medium with a fixed speed, doubling the frequency halves the wavelength. When a wave crosses into a new medium, keep the frequency and change the wavelength by the same factor as the speed.

To tell mechanical from electromagnetic, ask if the wave needs a material medium. Sound does. Light does not.

For the spectrum, remember one direction: wavelength falls and frequency rises from radio toward gamma rays.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Saying a Wave Carries the Medium Along

> In a simple mechanical wave, the medium moves back and forth around its rest position while the disturbance travels.

A water wave can cross a pond while each bit of water just moves in a small loop. The wave carries energy, and the water mostly stays where it is.

### #2: Thinking Frequency Changes Inside a New Medium

> When a wave enters a new medium, its frequency stays the same while its speed and wavelength change.

The source sets the frequency. The medium sets the speed, so the wavelength has to change to match.

### #3: Thinking Louder Means Higher

> Pitch follows frequency. Loudness mostly follows intensity. At the same frequency, a bigger amplitude gives a bigger intensity.

At the same frequency, a bigger amplitude makes the note louder. It does not make the note higher.

### #4: Treating the Speed of Sound as the Same Everywhere

> Sound speed depends on the medium and its temperature.

A value near $340\text{ m/s}$ is a common approximation for air at room temperature. It is not the speed in water, in steel, or in cold air.
