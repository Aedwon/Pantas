## Core Concept

A **nonconservative force**, such as sliding friction, air drag, or an applied push, pull or motor, can change mechanical energy. Sliding friction and drag turn it into thermal energy, the energy of the random motion of tiny particles, felt as warmth. A push or a motor can add to it.

If the surface that grips an object is at rest, friction that stops slipping does zero work, as under a ball rolling without slipping on the ground. If that surface moves, as a belt or a truck bed does, this friction can do positive work on the object it carries. The work $W_{nc}$ done by the nonconservative forces equals the change in mechanical energy:

$$W_{nc} = \Delta(KE+PE)$$

## Examples

### #1: Friction on a Ramp

A $2.0\ \text{kg}$ block slides from rest down a rough ramp. It starts $1.5\ \text{m}$ above the bottom. At the bottom its speed is $3.0\ \text{m/s}$. Find the work done by friction. Use $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: ramp with a 2.0 kg block at the top, height labeled 1.5 m, speed 3.0 m/s labeled at the bottom -->
**Solution:**
Friction is the only nonconservative force that does work. The ramp's support force is perpendicular to the motion and does none. Choose the bottom as $PE=0$.

1. Initial mechanical energy. The block starts at rest.

$$KE_i + PE_i = 0 + (2.0)(9.8)(1.5) = 29.4\ \text{J}$$

2. Final mechanical energy. The height is zero at the bottom.

$$KE_f + PE_f = \dfrac{1}{2}(2.0)(3.0)^2 + 0 = 9.0\ \text{J}$$

3. Friction's work equals the change in mechanical energy.

$$W_{nc} = 9.0 - 29.4 = -20.4\ \text{J}$$

Friction did $-20.4\ \text{J}$ of work. The $20.4\ \text{J}$ became thermal energy, not nothing.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For a mechanical-energy problem, ask if any force other than gravity does work. This lesson's problems have no springs. Sliding friction, air drag, a push, a pull and a motor all count. If none does, set $KE+PE$ equal at the two states; if one does, include $W_{nc}$.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Saying Energy Is "Lost" to Friction

> Friction on an object sliding over a surface at rest lowers its mechanical energy, but total energy is still conserved.

The energy becomes thermal energy, sound, or other forms. It is not destroyed.
