## Core Concept

**Nuclear chemistry** studies changes in the **nucleus**, the tiny center of an atom that holds its protons and neutrons. In an ordinary chemical reaction, atoms rearrange their electrons and every nucleus stays the same. In a nuclear reaction, the nucleus itself changes, and one element can become another.

### Atoms, Isotopes, and Nuclear Symbols

Two numbers describe a nucleus. The **atomic number** $Z$ is the number of protons. It decides which element the atom is. The **mass number** $A$ is the number of protons plus neutrons.

Atoms of one element with different numbers of neutrons are **isotopes**. A **nuclide** is one specific kind of nucleus, given by its $Z$ and $A$. We write it as $^A_Z X$, where $X$ is the element symbol, or by name and mass number, such as uranium-$238$.

The number of neutrons is $A-Z$. You can review protons and neutrons in the atomic structure lesson.

### Radioactive Decay

Some nuclei are unstable. An unstable nucleus can change by itself and give off radiation. This is **radioactive decay**.

The nucleus before the change is the **parent**. The nucleus after the change is the **daughter**.

Each decay mode below has a known effect on $A$ and $Z$. Learn those effects, because they let you find the daughter.

### Nuclear Equations

A nuclear equation shows the parent on the left and the daughter and the emitted particle on the right. It follows two checks:

- The mass numbers on the left add up to the mass numbers on the right.
- The atomic numbers (charges) on the left add up to the atomic numbers on the right.

Small particles get $A$ and $Z$ values too.

An electron has $A=0$ and $Z=-1$. A positron has $A=0$ and $Z=+1$. A **neutrino** and an **antineutrino** are tiny uncharged particles with $A=0$ and $Z=0$, so they do not change either check. A gamma photon also has $A=0$ and $Z=0$.

### Common Radioactive-Decay Modes

**Alpha ($\alpha$) decay.** The nucleus emits an alpha particle, $^4_2He$, which is $2$ protons and $2$ neutrons.

$$^A_Z X \rightarrow\ ^{A-4}_{Z-2} Y +\ ^4_2 He$$

- Mass number: $A\rightarrow A-4$.
- Atomic number: $Z\rightarrow Z-2$.

**Beta-minus ($\beta^-$) decay.** A neutron in the nucleus changes into a proton. The nucleus emits an electron and an antineutrino. The electron comes from the nucleus, not from the electron cloud around it.

$$^A_Z X \rightarrow\ ^{\ \ A}_{Z+1} Y +\ ^{\ 0}_{-1} e + \bar{\nu}$$

- Mass number unchanged.
- Atomic number: $Z\rightarrow Z+1$.

**Positron ($\beta^+$) emission.** A positron is a particle with the mass of an electron but a positive charge. A proton in the nucleus changes into a neutron. The nucleus emits a positron and a neutrino.

$$^A_Z X \rightarrow\ ^{\ \ A}_{Z-1} Y +\ ^{\ 0}_{+1} e + \nu$$

- Mass number unchanged.
- Atomic number: $Z\rightarrow Z-1$.

**Electron capture.** The nucleus pulls in one of the atom's own inner electrons. A proton changes into a neutron, and a neutrino is emitted.

$$^A_Z X +\ ^{\ 0}_{-1} e \rightarrow\ ^{\ \ A}_{Z-1} Y + \nu$$

- Mass number unchanged.
- Atomic number: $Z\rightarrow Z-1$.

Positron emission and electron capture give the same daughter, but the electron is on the product side in one and on the reactant side in the other. A given nuclide may decay by one, the other, or both.

**Gamma ($\gamma$) emission.** A nucleus in an excited, high-energy state releases a gamma ray, which is a photon of very high energy. The nucleus drops to a lower energy state.

- Mass number unchanged.
- Atomic number unchanged.

The element does not change in gamma emission. Gamma emission often follows another decay, because that decay can leave the daughter excited. An excited nucleus that lasts long enough is marked with an $m$, as in technetium-$99$m.

### How Far Each Radiation Travels

Radiation can knock electrons off the atoms it hits. This is called **ionizing** radiation, and it can damage living cells.

Alpha particles are heavy and stopped by a sheet of paper or by the outer layer of dead skin. Beta particles go farther and are stopped by a thin sheet of aluminum. Gamma rays go much farther. Thick lead or concrete **attenuates** them, which means it reduces their intensity. More shielding means less gamma, but some gamma rays can still get through.

Low penetration does not mean low hazard. An alpha emitter that is inhaled or swallowed sits next to living cells and can damage them. Outside the body, the skin shields most of the alpha particles.

### Fission

**Nuclear fission** splits a heavy nucleus into two smaller nuclei. In reactor examples, uranium-$235$ absorbs a neutron and then splits. Fission releases a large amount of energy and also releases extra neutrons.

$$^{235}_{92}U +\ ^1_0 n \rightarrow\ ^{141}_{56}Ba +\ ^{92}_{36}Kr + 3\ ^1_0 n$$

The released neutrons can strike more uranium-$235$ nuclei. This can start a **chain reaction**, which keeps itself going when enough fuel is present and enough neutrons hit new nuclei. Nuclear power plants control this reaction. The heat from fission boils water, and the steam turns a turbine that makes electricity.

### Fusion

**Nuclear fusion** joins light nuclei into a heavier nucleus. Fusion powers the Sun and other stars.

$$^2_1 H +\ ^3_1 H \rightarrow\ ^4_2 He +\ ^1_0 n$$

Here $^2_1 H$ is **deuterium**, a hydrogen isotope with $1$ proton and $1$ neutron. $^3_1 H$ is **tritium**, a hydrogen isotope with $1$ proton and $2$ neutrons.

Nuclei carry positive charge and push each other away. Fusion needs extremely high temperature and pressure to push them together. The Sun does not join hydrogen into helium in one step. Its net result is that four hydrogen nuclei become one helium-$4$ nucleus, plus other particles and energy, through several steps.

### Why Fission and Fusion Release Energy

The **binding energy** is the energy needed to pull a nucleus apart into separate protons and neutrons. The same amount of energy is released when those particles come together to form the nucleus.

A proton or neutron in a nucleus is called a **nucleon**. A larger binding energy per nucleon means a more tightly held, more stable nucleus.

Binding energy per nucleon is highest near iron-$56$. Splitting a very heavy nucleus gives smaller nuclei with more binding energy per nucleon.

Joining very light nuclei also gives a nucleus with more binding energy per nucleon. In both cases the products are more tightly bound, and the difference is released as energy.

### Comparing Fission and Fusion

The comparison depends on what is compared. Choose a basis first: per reaction, per mole, or per kilogram of fuel.

One fission of uranium-$235$ releases about $200$ MeV (a megaelectronvolt, a unit of energy used for single nuclei). One deuterium-tritium fusion releases about $17.6$ MeV. Per reaction, this fission releases more.

The fusion reaction uses only $5$ nucleons and the fission uses $236$. Per unit mass of fuel, fusion releases more energy. So the answer changes with the basis.

## Examples

### #1: Alpha Decay

Uranium-$238$ ($Z=92$) undergoes alpha decay. Identify the daughter nuclide.

**Solution:**

1. Alpha decay lowers $A$ by $4$ and $Z$ by $2$.
2. Find the daughter's mass number.

$$A'=238-4=234$$

3. Find the daughter's atomic number.

$$Z'=92-2=90$$

4. $Z=90$ is thorium, so the daughter is thorium-$234$.

### #2: Beta-Minus Decay

Carbon-$14$ ($Z=6$) undergoes $\beta^-$ decay. Write the balanced nuclear equation.

**Solution:**

1. $A$ stays $14$.
2. $Z$ rises by $1$ to $7$, which is nitrogen.
3. Add the electron and the antineutrino.

$$^{14}_{6}C \rightarrow\ ^{14}_{7}N +\ ^{\ 0}_{-1}e + \bar{\nu}$$

4. Check mass numbers: $14=14+0$. Check charge: $6=7+(-1)$.

### #3: Finding a Missing Product

A radium-$226$ nucleus ($Z=88$) decays and forms radon-$222$ ($Z=86$). What particle is emitted?

**Solution:**

1. Find the change in mass number.

$$226-222=4$$

2. Find the change in atomic number.

$$88-86=2$$

3. A particle with $A=4$ and $Z=2$ is an alpha particle.

$$^{226}_{88}Ra \rightarrow\ ^{222}_{86}Rn +\ ^4_2He$$

### #4: Positron Emission and Electron Capture

Sodium-$22$ ($Z=11$) decays by positron emission. Beryllium-$7$ ($Z=4$) decays by electron capture. Find each daughter.

**Solution:**

1. In both processes $A$ stays the same and $Z$ drops by $1$.
2. Sodium-$22$: $Z=11-1=10$ is neon, so the daughter is neon-$22$.

$$^{22}_{11}Na \rightarrow\ ^{22}_{10}Ne +\ ^{\ 0}_{+1}e + \nu$$

3. Beryllium-$7$: $Z=4-1=3$ is lithium, so the daughter is lithium-$7$.

$$^{7}_{4}Be +\ ^{\ 0}_{-1}e \rightarrow\ ^{7}_{3}Li + \nu$$

4. The two processes differ in the electron or positron, even though $A$ and $Z$ change in the same way.

### #5: Gamma Emission

An excited nucleus technetium-$99$m ($Z=43$) emits a gamma ray. What is the daughter nuclide?

**Solution:**

1. Gamma emission changes neither $A$ nor $Z$.
2. The daughter has $A=99$ and $Z=43$, so it is technetium-$99$.

$$^{99\text{m}}_{\ \ 43}Tc \rightarrow\ ^{99}_{43}Tc + \gamma$$

3. Only the energy of the nucleus changes. The element stays the same.

### #6: A Decay Series

Uranium-$238$ ($Z=92$) emits an alpha particle. The daughter then emits a $\beta^-$ particle. What is the final nuclide?

**Solution:**

1. After the alpha step, $A=238-4=234$ and $Z=92-2=90$, which is thorium-$234$.
2. The $\beta^-$ step keeps $A=234$ and raises $Z$ to $90+1=91$.
3. $Z=91$ is protactinium, so the final nuclide is protactinium-$234$.

Step 1, alpha decay:

$$^{238}_{92}U \rightarrow\ ^{234}_{90}Th +\ ^4_2He$$

Step 2, $\beta^-$ decay:

$$^{234}_{90}Th \rightarrow\ ^{234}_{91}Pa +\ ^{\ 0}_{-1}e + \bar{\nu}$$

4. Check each step. Step 1: $238=234+4$ and $92=90+2$. Step 2: $234=234+0$ and $90=91+(-1)$.

### #7: Balancing a Fission Equation

In the fission $^{235}_{92}U +\ ^1_0 n \rightarrow\ ^{141}_{56}Ba +\ ^{A}_{Z}Kr + 3\ ^1_0 n$, find $A$ and $Z$ of the krypton.

**Solution:**

1. Mass numbers on the left: $235+1=236$.
2. Mass numbers on the right: $141+A+3(1)=236$.

$$A=236-141-3=92$$

3. Atomic numbers on the left: $92+0=92$.
4. Atomic numbers on the right: $56+Z+0=92$.

$$Z=92-56=36$$

5. The krypton is krypton-$92$ with $Z=36$.

### #8: Fission or Fusion?

Name each process.

(a) A uranium nucleus absorbs a neutron, divides into medium-mass fragments, and releases neutrons.
(b) Deuterium and tritium (hydrogen isotopes) combine to form helium and a neutron.

**Solution:**

1. In (a), a heavy nucleus splits into smaller ones, so this is fission.
2. In (b), light nuclei join into a heavier one, so this is fusion.

### #9: Comparing Energy by Basis

A student says, "Fusion always releases more energy than fission." Test this using about $200$ MeV for one uranium-$235$ fission and about $17.6$ MeV for one deuterium-tritium fusion.

**Solution:**

1. Per reaction, compare $200$ MeV with $17.6$ MeV. Fission is larger.
2. Per nucleon, divide by the nucleons in the reaction. Fission uses $236$ nucleons.

$$\dfrac{200}{236}\approx 0.85\ \text{MeV per nucleon}$$

3. Fusion uses $5$ nucleons.

$$\dfrac{17.6}{5}\approx 3.5\ \text{MeV per nucleon}$$

4. Per nucleon, and so per unit mass, fusion is larger. The statement is true on one basis and false on another.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Track $A$ and $Z$ for these decay modes:

- $\alpha$: $A-4$, $Z-2$.
- $\beta^-$: $A$ same, $Z+1$.
- $\beta^+$ or electron capture: $A$ same, $Z-1$.
- $\gamma$: $A$ same, $Z$ same.

To balance any nuclear equation, make the $A$ values add up on both sides, then do the same for $Z$. Count an electron as $Z=-1$ and a positron as $Z=+1$. Neutrinos and photons add nothing to either sum.

For the first-level distinction between fission and fusion, ask whether a heavy nucleus splits or light nuclei combine.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Reading "Beta" as Only One Process

> Beta decay in many questions means $\beta^-$, but a positron also counts as a beta particle.

Check the sign in the question, such as $\beta^-$ or $\beta^+$, or the emitted particle in the equation. The $Z$ change is $+1$ for $\beta^-$ and $-1$ for $\beta^+$.

### #2: Saying Paper Makes Alpha Radiation Harmless

> Alpha particles travel a short distance but ionize strongly.

Paper stops alpha particles from outside. An alpha emitter that is inhaled or swallowed can still harm the body.

### #3: Saying Gamma Shielding Blocks All Radiation

> Shielding reduces gamma intensity. Thicker or denser shielding reduces it more.

"Use lead or concrete" is a practical rule, not a point where all gamma rays are gone.

### #4: Comparing Fission and Fusion Without a Basis

> An energy comparison needs a basis: per reaction, per mole, or per kilogram of fuel.

For these two reactions, fusion gives more energy per kilogram of fuel, but one fission event of uranium-$235$ releases more than one deuterium-tritium fusion event. A statement without a basis cannot be judged.

### #5: Thinking Gamma Emission Changes the Element

> Gamma emission changes the energy of the nucleus, not $A$ or $Z$.

If a question shows a gamma ray as the only particle emitted, the daughter is the same nuclide at lower energy. When a decay also emits $\alpha$ or $\beta$, the element changes because of that particle, not because of the gamma ray.
