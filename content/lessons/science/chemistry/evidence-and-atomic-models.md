## Core Concept

A **model** is a picture or idea that explains what scientists have observed. Each atomic model fit the evidence of its time. New evidence then showed where the model failed, and scientists built a better one.

Many researchers built modern atomic theory. It was not one straight line of five separate discoveries.

### Evidence, Inference, and Model

An **observation** is what an experiment shows, such as a flash of light on a screen. An **inference** is a conclusion drawn from observations. To "infer" means to work out what must be true because of the evidence.

A good model explains the observations made so far. It also predicts what a new experiment will show. If the experiment shows something else, the model has reached its limit.

## Examples

### #1: Testing a Model with a Change

A student repeats the gold-foil work with a foil that is much thicker. What changes and what must stay the same for a fair test? What is likely to happen?

**Solution:**

1. The thing changed on purpose is the foil thickness.
2. Keep the same: the metal, the alpha source, and the screen.
3. A thicker foil has more atoms in the path, so more alpha particles meet a nucleus.
4. More strong deflections are likely, and fewer particles pass straight through. This agrees with the nuclear model.
5. If the foil is thick enough, it can stop the alpha particles, so few or none reach the screen.

### #2: Matching Historical Models

Match each description to the scientist most directly associated with it:

- diffuse positive charge with embedded electrons;
- small, dense positive nucleus;
- quantized hydrogen energy levels.

**Solution:**

1. Thomson: diffuse positive charge with embedded electrons.
2. Rutherford: a nucleus, based on scattering evidence.
3. Bohr: quantized energy levels that explain the hydrogen line spectrum.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Use this evidence chain:

- Dalton: atoms, from mass laws.
- Thomson: electron, from cathode rays.
- Geiger-Marsden-Rutherford: nucleus, from alpha scattering.
- Bohr: energy levels, from the hydrogen line spectrum.
- quantum mechanics after $1920$: orbitals and probability.

To tell an observation from an inference, ask: "Could I see this in the data?" If yes, it is an observation. If it explains the data, it is an inference.
