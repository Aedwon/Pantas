## Core Concept

**Organic chemistry** is the branch of chemistry about carbon compounds, mainly those built on carbon-carbon and carbon-hydrogen frameworks. A **bond** is the link between two atoms. A **covalent bond** forms when two atoms share electrons.

Not every carbon compound counts as organic. Carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide and carbonates are usually treated as inorganic. Pure carbon, such as diamond, is not a compound at all.

Carbon forms four covalent bonds. It also bonds strongly to other carbon atoms.

Think of each carbon as a building block with four connectors. The blocks join into chains, rings and branches.

### Hydrocarbons

A **hydrocarbon** contains only carbon and hydrogen. A **saturated** hydrocarbon has no carbon-carbon double or triple bonds. An **unsaturated** hydrocarbon has at least one.

- **Alkanes** have only single bonds. They can be straight chains, branched chains or rings. Rings of this kind are called **cycloalkanes**.
- **Alkenes** have at least one carbon-carbon double bond ($C=C$).
- **Alkynes** have at least one carbon-carbon triple bond ($C\equiv C$).

Methane, $CH_4$, is an alkane with only one carbon. It has no carbon-carbon bond at all.

A **chain** with no ring is **acyclic**. A single formula pattern then follows from the bonds. With $n$ carbons:

$$\text{alkane: } C_nH_{2n+2} \qquad \text{alkene: } C_nH_{2n} \qquad \text{alkyne: } C_nH_{2n-2}$$

The alkene and alkyne formulas hold for acyclic molecules with exactly one double bond or one triple bond. The alkane formula holds for acyclic alkanes only.

A ring removes two hydrogens. A cycloalkane with $n$ carbons is $C_nH_{2n}$, the same formula as an acyclic alkene. So a formula alone cannot always tell them apart.

Benzene, $C_6H_6$, is a hydrocarbon with a special ring of shared electrons. It belongs to a separate family called aromatic hydrocarbons, so this lesson's alkane, alkene and alkyne rules do not cover it.

### Functional Groups

A **functional group** is a small group of atoms that gives a compound its typical reactions. The letter $R$ stands for the rest of the molecule, a carbon group.

Names treat groups in different ways. Some groups set a name ending, called a **suffix**, as listed below. Halogen groups such as $-Cl$ and $-Br$ appear only as a prefix, as in chloromethane. Ethers, $R-O-R$, take no suffix in common names, as in diethyl ether.

- **Alcohol**: a **hydroxyl** group, $-OH$, on a carbon that has only single bonds. Written $R-OH$. Name ending: **-ol**.
- **Carbonyl** group: a carbon double-bonded to oxygen, $C=O$. It appears in the next three families.
- **Aldehyde**: the carbonyl carbon sits at the end of the chain. It is bonded to at least one hydrogen. Its other bond goes to a carbon group, or to a second hydrogen in methanal, $HCHO$, the simplest aldehyde. Written $R-CHO$, where $R$ may be a hydrogen. Name ending: **-al**.
- **Ketone**: the carbonyl carbon sits inside the chain. It is bonded to two carbon groups. Written $R-CO-R$. Name ending: **-one**.
- **Carboxylic acid**: a carbonyl carbon that also carries an $-OH$ group. Written $R-COOH$. Name ending: **-oic acid**.
- **Amine**: a compound derived from ammonia, $NH_3$, by replacing one or more hydrogens with carbon groups. A primary amine has $-NH_2$. Secondary and tertiary amines have $-NHR$ and $-NR_2$. Name ending: **-amine**.
- **Ester**: the carbonyl carbon is bonded to an $-O-R$ group. It forms when a carboxylic acid joins an alcohol. Name ending: **-oate**, as in ethyl ethanoate.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: five structures side by side with the functional group circled and labeled: CH3CH2OH (alcohol), CH3CH2CHO (aldehyde), CH3COCH3 (ketone), CH3COOH (carboxylic acid), CH3NH2 (amine) -->

When a compound has more than one group, only the main one sets the suffix. The others are named as prefixes, such as hydroxy- for $-OH$ and amino- for $-NH_2$.

An aldehyde and a ketone with the same atoms are different compounds. $C_3H_6O$ can be propanal, $CH_3CH_2CHO$, with the $C=O$ at the chain end. It can also be propanone, $CH_3COCH_3$, with the $C=O$ in the middle. Compounds with the same formula and a different arrangement are called **isomers**.

### How Groups Change Properties

A bond is **polar** when the electrons are shared unevenly. The $O-H$ bond is polar. It lets an $-OH$ group form **hydrogen bonds** with neighboring molecules.

A hydrogen bond is an attraction between a hydrogen attached to oxygen, nitrogen or fluorine and an oxygen, nitrogen or fluorine atom on another molecule. Alcohols, water and $HF$ all show it.

Hydrogen bonds hold molecules together. More energy is then needed to boil the liquid, so the **boiling point** rises. Groups that form hydrogen bonds with water also help a compound dissolve in water.

A carboxylic acid group has an $O-H$ and also a polar $C=O$ oxygen. Two acid molecules can pair up through two hydrogen bonds. So an acid usually holds together more strongly than an alcohol of similar size.

Size also matters. A long carbon chain behaves like a hydrocarbon and mixes poorly with water. The same $-OH$ group gives good water solubility to a small alcohol and much less to a large one.

Functional groups are strong predictors of behavior. Chain length, branching and other groups present also matter.

### Introductory IUPAC Naming

**IUPAC** is the body that sets the international rules for chemical names. A full name is built from these parts:

- a **parent**: the main chain, named by its number of carbons;
- a **suffix** for the main functional group or bond type;
- **substituents**: branches or groups attached to the parent;
- **locants**: numbers that say which carbon holds a substituent, bond or group.

The carbon roots are:

$$1=\text{meth-},\ 2=\text{eth-},\ 3=\text{prop-},\ 4=\text{but-},\ 5=\text{pent-},\ 6=\text{hex-},\ 7=\text{hept-},\ 8=\text{oct-},\ 9=\text{non-},\ 10=\text{dec-}$$

The endings are **-ane**, **-ene** and **-yne** for single, double and triple carbon-carbon bonds. A carbon branch has the root name with **-yl**: methyl is $-CH_3$, ethyl is $-C_2H_5$.

For a simple branched alkane, follow these steps:

1. Find the longest continuous carbon chain. This is the parent. If two chains tie, choose the one with more branches.
2. Number the chain from the end that gives the first branch the lowest number. If that ties, compare the whole set of numbers and choose the lower one.
3. Name each branch and give its number. Use **di-**, **tri-** for repeats, with a number for each one. List different branches in alphabetical order.

The name is written as locants, then branches, then the parent: $2$-methylbutane.

When a compound has a double bond, a triple bond or a main functional group, the parent must contain it. Halogen groups are the exception: they are only prefixes, so the parent need not contain them. Number the chain from the end that gives the group or bond the lowest number. The longest chain is not always the parent. For example, the group $-OH$ makes an alcohol, and the parent must contain the carbon that holds it.

### Carbon Compounds of Living Things

Living things are built from large carbon compounds called **biomolecules**. Many are **polymers**, long molecules made by joining many small units called **monomers**. Their functional groups explain their structure and behavior.

- **Carbohydrates** contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Sugars such as glucose, $C_6H_{12}O_6$, have many $-OH$ groups, so they dissolve in water. Starch, glycogen and cellulose are polymers made of glucose units.
- **Proteins** are polymers of **amino acids**. Each amino acid has an amine group and a carboxylic acid group. A **peptide bond** joins the carboxylic acid of one to the amine of the next.
- **Lipids** include fats and oils. A fat or oil is an ester of **glycerol**, an alcohol with three $-OH$ groups, with three **fatty acids**. A fatty acid is a carboxylic acid with a long hydrocarbon chain. The chains make fats mix poorly with water. Lipids are not all fats and oils. Lipids are also not true polymers, because they are not long chains of repeating monomers.
- **Nucleic acids**, DNA and RNA, are polymers of **nucleotides**. Each nucleotide has a sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogen-containing base.

How these molecules work inside cells belongs to biology.

## Examples

### #1: Organic or Not

Which of these is usually treated as organic: methane, $CH_4$; carbon dioxide, $CO_2$; glucose, $C_6H_{12}O_6$; calcium carbonate, $CaCO_3$?

**Solution:**

1. Methane has carbon bonded to hydrogen. It is the simplest hydrocarbon, so it is organic.
2. Glucose has a carbon framework with hydrogen and oxygen. It is organic.
3. Carbon dioxide and carbonates such as calcium carbonate are usually treated as inorganic.

Methane and glucose are organic. Carbon dioxide and calcium carbonate are not usually called organic.

### #2: Hydrocarbon Family from the Bonds

Classify (a) a hydrocarbon with only single bonds, (b) a hydrocarbon with one $C\equiv C$ bond, (c) a hydrocarbon with one $C=C$ bond.

**Solution:**

1. (a) Only single bonds: an alkane, which is saturated.
2. (b) A triple bond: an alkyne, which is unsaturated.
3. (c) A double bond: an alkene, which is unsaturated.

### #3: Family from a Formula

Each compound is a hydrocarbon with five carbons: $C_5H_{12}$, $C_5H_{10}$, $C_5H_8$. Which family can each be, if it has no ring and one multiple bond at most?

**Solution:**

1. For $n=5$, an acyclic alkane is $C_5H_{12}$. So $C_5H_{12}$ is an alkane.
2. An alkene is $C_nH_{2n}$, which is $C_5H_{10}$.
3. An alkyne is $C_nH_{2n-2}$, which is $C_5H_8$.

If rings were allowed, $C_5H_{10}$ could also be cyclopentane, a cycloalkane. Rings change the hydrogen count.

### #4: Naming a Straight Chain and Its Isomer

Name the straight four-carbon alkane. Then write the formula of an isomer with a branch.

**Solution:**

1. Four carbons give the root **but-**. Single bonds give **-ane**. The name is **butane**, $C_4H_{10}$.
2. Move one carbon to a branch: three carbons in the chain and a methyl group on carbon $2$.
3. This is $2$-methylpropane, $CH_3-CH(CH_3)-CH_3$. It also has the formula $C_4H_{10}$, so it is an isomer of butane.

### #5: Why Cyclohexane Is Saturated

Cyclohexane is $C_6H_{12}$. Acyclic hexane is $C_6H_{14}$. Is cyclohexane unsaturated?

**Solution:**

1. Check the bonds. Cyclohexane has a ring of six carbons joined only by single bonds.
2. It has no $C=C$ or $C\equiv C$ bond, so it is saturated.
3. Closing the ring removes two hydrogens. Fewer hydrogens do not make it unsaturated.

Cyclohexane is a saturated cycloalkane.

### #6: Spot the Functional Group

Name the main functional group of each: $CH_3CH_2OH$, $CH_3CH_2CHO$, $CH_3COCH_3$, $CH_3COOH$, $CH_3NH_2$.

**Solution:**

1. $CH_3CH_2OH$ has $-OH$ on a carbon with single bonds: an alcohol.
2. $CH_3CH_2CHO$ has $C=O$ at the end of the chain: an aldehyde.
3. $CH_3COCH_3$ has $C=O$ between two carbons: a ketone.
4. $CH_3COOH$ has $C=O$ with an $-OH$ on the same carbon: a carboxylic acid.
5. $CH_3NH_2$ has nitrogen bonded to carbon and two hydrogens: a primary amine.

### #7: Aldehyde or Ketone

$C_3H_6O$ has a carbonyl group. Give the two possible compounds and say how to tell them apart.

**Solution:**

1. Place the $C=O$ at the end of the chain with a hydrogen on it: $CH_3CH_2CHO$, an aldehyde. It is named propanal.
2. Place the $C=O$ on the middle carbon: $CH_3COCH_3$, a ketone. It is named propanone.
3. Look at the carbonyl carbon, once you know it has no $-OH$ or $-O-R$ on it. A hydrogen on it means an aldehyde. Two carbon groups on it means a ketone.

### #8: Comparing Ethane, Ethanol and Ethanoic Acid

Ethane is $CH_3CH_3$, ethanol is $CH_3CH_2OH$, and ethanoic acid is $CH_3COOH$. Their boiling points are about $-89\,^\circ C$, $78\,^\circ C$ and $118\,^\circ C$. Explain the order.

**Solution:**

1. Ethane has only nonpolar $C-C$ and $C-H$ bonds. It cannot form hydrogen bonds, so it boils at a very low temperature.
2. Ethanol has an $-OH$ group. Its molecules form hydrogen bonds, so more energy is needed to separate them.
3. Ethanoic acid has $-COOH$, with both an $O-H$ and a polar $C=O$. Two molecules pair up through two hydrogen bonds, which is stronger attraction than ethanol's. It has the highest boiling point.

The three molecules have a similar size. The functional group causes the difference.

### #9: Naming a Branched Alkane

Name $CH_3CH_2CH(CH_3)CH_2CH_2CH_3$.

**Solution:**

1. Find the longest chain. Ignore the $CH_3$ in brackets. The chain has six carbons, so the parent is hexane.
2. The bracketed $CH_3$ is a methyl branch on the third carbon from the left. Numbered from the right, it is on the fourth.
3. Choose the lower number: $3$.

The name is $3$-methylhexane.

### #10: Two Branches

Name $CH_3CH(CH_3)CH(CH_3)CH_2CH_3$.

**Solution:**

1. The longest chain has five carbons. The parent is pentane.
2. Two methyl branches. From the left they sit on carbons $2$ and $3$. From the right they sit on $3$ and $4$.
3. Compare the sets. $2,3$ is lower than $3,4$, so number from the left.
4. Two identical branches use the prefix **di-**.

The name is $2,3$-dimethylpentane.

### #11: Locants for a Double Bond and for an Alcohol

Name (a) $CH_3CH=CHCH_3$ and (b) $CH_3CH(OH)CH_2CH_3$.

**Solution:**

1. (a) Four carbons give but-. The double bond is between carbons $2$ and $3$. Numbering from either end gives $2$. The ending is -ene: but-$2$-ene.
2. (b) Four carbons with single bonds give butane. The $-OH$ is on the second carbon from the left. It would be third from the right. Choose the lowest number, $2$.
3. For the alcohol, change -e to -ol and give the locant: butan-$2$-ol.

### #12: When the Longest Chain Is Not the Parent

Name the alcohol with this skeleton: a central carbon bonded to $CH_2OH$, to $CH_2CH_3$ and to $CH_2CH_2CH_3$.

**Solution:**

1. The longest chain overall runs through the ethyl and propyl groups and has six carbons. It leaves out the carbon that holds $-OH$.
2. The parent must contain that carbon. The longest chain that does has five carbons: $HOCH_2$, the central carbon and the propyl group. The parent is pentane.
3. Number from the end that gives $-OH$ the lowest number. The $-OH$ is on carbon $1$.
4. The ethyl branch is on carbon $2$.

The name is $2$-ethylpentan-$1$-ol.

### #13: Functional Groups in Biomolecules

(a) Which two functional groups does every amino acid have? (b) Fat is made from glycerol and fatty acids. Which functional group joins them?

**Solution:**

1. (a) An amino acid has an amine group and a carboxylic acid group.
2. (b) Glycerol is an alcohol, and a fatty acid is a carboxylic acid.
3. An alcohol and a carboxylic acid join to form an ester, so fats are esters.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For a hydrocarbon family, look for $C=C$ or $C\equiv C$ in the structure. If you see neither, it is saturated. This shortcut covers alkanes, alkenes and alkynes only. Benzene is a separate case.

For a formula, test $C_nH_{2n+2}$ first. A match for an acyclic hydrocarbon means an alkane. Two hydrogens fewer means one double bond or one ring.

To name an alkane, circle the longest chain first. Then number from the end nearest a branch. Use the carbon roots meth- to dec- and the endings -ane, -ene, -yne.

First check the $C=O$ carbon for an $-OH$ or an $-O-R$. If it has one, the compound is a carboxylic acid or an ester, not an aldehyde or ketone.

Methanoic acid, $HCOOH$, has a hydrogen there and is still an acid. If the carbon has neither group, a hydrogen on it means an aldehyde, and two carbon groups mean a ketone.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Calling Every Carbon Compound Organic

> Carbon dioxide and carbonates are carbon compounds that are usually treated as inorganic.

The word organic refers to the chemistry of carbon frameworks, not to the mere presence of carbon. Carbon monoxide is another usual exception.

### #2: Thinking Saturated Means Maximum Hydrogens

> Saturated means no carbon-carbon double or triple bonds.

A cycloalkane has fewer hydrogens than the acyclic alkane with the same carbons. It is still saturated.

### #3: Defining Every Amine as $-NH_2$

> $-NH_2$ is a primary amine. Secondary and tertiary amines also exist.

All amines have nitrogen bonded to carbon groups, as in ammonia with some hydrogens replaced.

### #4: Using the Longest Chain Every Time

> Longest chain is the rule for simple alkanes. It does not decide the parent when a main group is present.

If a double bond, triple bond or functional group is present, the parent must contain it. A longer chain that leaves it out is the wrong parent. A halogen is the exception: it is only a prefix.

### #5: Numbering from the Wrong End

> Number from the end that gives the lowest locants.

Check both directions. In $3$-methylhexane, numbering from the other end would give $4$, which is higher.

### #6: Mixing Up Aldehyde and Ketone

> Both have $C=O$. The position of the carbonyl carbon decides.

An aldehyde has the carbonyl carbon at the chain end, with at least one hydrogen on it. A ketone has it inside the chain, between two carbons.
