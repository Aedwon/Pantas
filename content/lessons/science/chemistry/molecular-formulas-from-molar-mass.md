## Core Concept

To find the molecular formula, divide the compound's molar mass by the **molar mass calculated from the empirical formula**, such as $12+2(1)+16=30$ g/mol for $CH_2O$. The answer $k$ should be close to a whole number:

$$k=\dfrac{\text{molar mass of compound}}{\text{molar mass from empirical formula}}$$

Multiply every subscript in the empirical formula by $k$.

Ionic compounds have no separate molecules, so they have no molecular formula. Their formulas are usually already in the simplest ratio, as in $NaCl$, $CaCO_3$ and $Na_2SO_4$. A few are not: sodium peroxide is written $Na_2O_2$, but its simplest atom ratio is $NaO$.

## Examples

### #1: Molecular Formula

A compound has empirical formula $CH_2O$ and a molar mass of $180$ g/mol. Use $12$, $1$, and $16$ g/mol for $C$, $H$, and $O$. Find the molecular formula.

**Solution:**

1. Molar mass from the empirical formula: $12+2(1)+16=30$ g/mol.
2. Find the multiplier:

$$k=\dfrac{180}{30}=6$$

3. Multiply every subscript by $6$. The molecular formula is $C_6H_{12}O_6$.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

The multiplier $k$ must be close to a whole number. If it is not, recheck the empirical formula or the molar mass.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Calling Every Formula Molecular

> Ionic solids have formula units, not molecules.

For an ionic compound such as $NaCl$, the formula shows the ratio of its ions. Only a molecular compound can have a molecular formula that is a multiple of its empirical formula. To find the empirical atom ratio of $Na_2O_2$, reduce the subscripts to get $NaO$. Keep $Na_2O_2$ as the ionic formula because it shows the peroxide ion, $O_2^{2-}$.
