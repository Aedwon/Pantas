## Core Concept

Atoms and molecules are far too small and too many to count one by one. A drop of water holds more molecules than anyone could count in a lifetime. Chemists solve this by counting in very large groups, the way a store counts eggs by the dozen.

A dozen is $12$ of anything. The **mole** (symbol mol) is a much bigger counting group. It is the SI unit of **amount of substance**, which is how many particles a sample holds, written in moles.

### The Mole and the Avogadro Constant

The SI definition of the mole is exact:

$$1\ \mathrm{mol}=6.02214076\times10^{23}\ \text{specified elementary entities}$$

The symbol $10^{23}$ means $1$ followed by $23$ zeros. So $6.02\times10^{23}$ is about $602$ thousand billion billion.

The **Avogadro constant**, written $N_A$, is the number of entities in one mole. It is exactly

$$N_A=6.02214076\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}.$$

The unit $\mathrm{mol^{-1}}$ is read "per mole". Most problems use the rounded value $6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}$ or $6.02\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}$. Use the value the problem supplies, or the precision the data allow.

### Naming the Entity

An **entity** is the kind of particle you are counting. It can be an atom, a molecule, an ion (an atom or group of atoms with an electric charge), an electron, or a formula unit. A **formula unit** is the smallest repeating group in the formula of an ionic compound. In solid $NaCl$, one formula unit is one $Na^+$ ion with one $Cl^-$ ion.

A molecular substance such as water is counted in molecules. An ionic compound such as $NaCl$ is made of a lattice of ions, not separate molecules, so it is counted in formula units. State the entity whenever the words could mean more than one thing. "One mole of oxygen" could mean $O$ atoms or $O_2$ molecules.

Some elements exist as molecules with two atoms. Hydrogen $H_2$, nitrogen $N_2$, oxygen $O_2$, fluorine $F_2$, chlorine $Cl_2$, bromine $Br_2$, and iodine $I_2$ are the common ones, called **diatomic** elements. One mole of $O_2$ molecules contains $2$ mol of $O$ atoms.

The mole also tells you how many atoms are inside the particles. One mole of $H_2O$ molecules contains $1$ mol of $O$ atoms and $2$ mol of $H$ atoms. The subscripts in a formula give the ratio of atoms in one molecule, so they give the same ratio of moles.

### Atomic Mass and Molar Mass

Atoms of one element can differ slightly in mass. Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons are **isotopes**. The **mass number** of one isotope is a whole number: its count of protons plus neutrons.

The **relative atomic mass** of an element compares the average mass of its atoms, over the natural mix of isotopes, with the unified atomic mass unit. That unit is one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-$12$ atom. Relative atomic mass is a ratio, so it has no unit. The periodic table lists it, and it is rarely a whole number. Carbon is about $12.011$ and chlorine is about $35.45$.

**Molar mass**, symbol $M$, is the mass of one mole of a substance. It equals mass divided by amount of substance, and it is usually given in $\mathrm{g/mol}$. For an element, the molar mass in $\mathrm{g/mol}$ has the same number as the relative atomic mass, to a very close approximation. So carbon is about $12.011\ \mathrm{g/mol}$.

Since the $2019$ SI redefinition, the mole is fixed by the exact value of $N_A$, not by the mass of carbon-$12$. The numbers still agree to about nine significant digits, so school calculations are not affected.

To get the molar mass of a compound, add the molar masses of all the atoms in its formula. Multiply each element's molar mass by its subscript first. If parentheses have a subscript, it multiplies every atom inside them.

### Converting Between Mass, Moles, and Entities

The amount in moles equals mass divided by molar mass:

$$n=\dfrac{m}{M}$$

Here $n$ is the amount in moles, $m$ is the sample mass in grams, and $M$ is the molar mass in $\mathrm{g/mol}$. Rearranged, the mass of a given amount is $m=nM$.

The number of entities is

$$N=nN_A$$

so the amount in moles is $n=\dfrac{N}{N_A}$.

To go from mass to entities, use both relations: mass to moles with $M$, then moles to entities with $N_A$. To go from entities to mass, do the reverse. Moles are the middle step because $M$ and $N_A$ both connect to them. You can write it as one line of **dimensional analysis**, which means arranging conversion factors so the unwanted units cancel. The setup must have the units cancel correctly.

### Significant Figures

**Significant figures** are the digits of a measured number that carry meaning. A final answer should not show more digits than the least precise given number. The Avogadro constant is exact, so it never limits this. The measured mass and the molar mass values you are given decide how many digits to report.

## Examples

### #1: Molar Mass of a Compound

What is the molar mass of water, $H_2O$? Use $H=1.008\ \mathrm{g/mol}$ and $O=16.00\ \mathrm{g/mol}$.

**Solution:**

1. A water molecule has $2$ H atoms and $1$ O atom. So multiply the H value by $2$.
2. Add the O value.

$$M(H_2O)=2(1.008)+16.00=18.016\approx18.02\ \mathrm{g/mol}$$

A problem may round the atomic masses to $1$ and $16$, which gives $18\ \mathrm{g/mol}$. Follow the values the problem supplies.

### #2: Formula With Parentheses

Find the molar mass of calcium hydroxide, $Ca(OH)_2$. Use $Ca=40$, $O=16$, and $H=1$, all in $\mathrm{g/mol}$.

**Solution:**

1. The subscript $2$ multiplies everything inside the parentheses. The formula has $1$ Ca atom, $2$ O atoms, and $2$ H atoms.
2. Multiply each by its count and add.

$$M=40+2(16)+2(1)=74\ \mathrm{g/mol}$$

### #3: Mass to Moles

How many moles are in $36$ g of water? Use $M=18\ \mathrm{g/mol}$.

**Solution:**

$$n=\dfrac{36\ \mathrm g}{18\ \mathrm{g/mol}}=2.0\ \mathrm{mol}$$

### #4: Moles to Mass of a Diatomic Element

What is the mass of $2.5$ mol of oxygen gas, $O_2$? Use $O=16.00\ \mathrm{g/mol}$.

**Solution:**

1. Oxygen gas is made of $O_2$ molecules. Each molecule has $2$ atoms, so $M(O_2)=2(16.00)=32.00\ \mathrm{g/mol}$.
2. Use $m=nM$.

$$m=(2.5\ \mathrm{mol})(32.00\ \mathrm{g/mol})=80\ \mathrm g$$

If the problem meant $2.5$ mol of $O$ atoms, the molar mass would be $16.00\ \mathrm{g/mol}$ and the mass would be $40$ g. The entity must be named.

### #5: Moles to Molecules

How many water molecules are in $2.0$ mol of $H_2O$?

**Solution:**

$$N=nN_A=(2.0\ \mathrm{mol})(6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}})$$

$$N\approx1.2\times10^{24}\ \text{molecules}$$

The data have $2$ significant figures, so the answer does too.

### #6: Mass to Molecules

How many water molecules are in $9.0$ g of water? Use $M=18\ \mathrm{g/mol}$.

**Solution:**

1. Find the moles.

$$n=\dfrac{9.0\ \mathrm g}{18\ \mathrm{g/mol}}=0.50\ \mathrm{mol}$$

2. Convert moles to molecules.

$$N=(0.50)(6.022\times10^{23})\approx3.0\times10^{23}\ \text{molecules}$$

### #7: Molecules to Mass

What is the mass of $3.01\times10^{23}$ molecules of carbon dioxide, $CO_2$? Use $C=12$ and $O=16$, in $\mathrm{g/mol}$, and $N_A=6.02\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}$.

**Solution:**

1. Molar mass: $M(CO_2)=12+2(16)=44\ \mathrm{g/mol}$.
2. Moles of molecules.

$$n=\dfrac{N}{N_A}=\dfrac{3.01\times10^{23}}{6.02\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}}=0.500\ \mathrm{mol}$$

3. Mass.

$$m=nM=(0.500\ \mathrm{mol})(44\ \mathrm{g/mol})=22\ \mathrm g$$

### #8: Counting Atoms Inside Molecules

How many hydrogen atoms are in $9.0$ g of water? Use $M=18\ \mathrm{g/mol}$ and $N_A=6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}$.

**Solution:**

1. Moles of water: $n=\dfrac{9.0}{18}=0.50\ \mathrm{mol}$ of $H_2O$.
2. Each molecule has $2$ H atoms, so the moles of H atoms are $2(0.50)=1.0$ mol.
3. Convert to atoms.

$$N=(1.0\ \mathrm{mol})(6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}})\approx6.0\times10^{23}\ \text{atoms}$$

### #9: Formula Units and Ions of an Ionic Compound

A sample has $5.85$ g of sodium chloride, $NaCl$. Use $Na=23.0$ and $Cl=35.5$, in $\mathrm{g/mol}$, and $N_A=6.02\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}$. How many formula units are there, and how many ions in total?

**Solution:**

1. Molar mass: $M(NaCl)=23.0+35.5=58.5\ \mathrm{g/mol}$.
2. Moles of formula units.

$$n=\dfrac{5.85}{58.5}=0.100\ \mathrm{mol}$$

3. Formula units.

$$N=(0.100)(6.02\times10^{23})=6.02\times10^{22}\ \text{formula units}$$

4. Each formula unit gives $1$ $Na^+$ ion and $1$ $Cl^-$ ion, so the ions are twice as many: $1.20\times10^{23}$ ions.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Set up with dimensional analysis. Write each conversion factor so the unit you do not want sits on the opposite side from where it started, and it cancels.

For a compound, multiply each element's molar mass by its subscript before you add. This works for any formula, including ones with parentheses once you multiply through.

A quick check for a gas or other molecular element: if the formula has a subscript $2$, such as $O_2$, double the atomic molar mass. This applies only to the diatomic elements listed above, and only when the problem means the molecule.

Estimate the answer's size first. A sample of $9$ g of water is less than one mole, because one mole of water is about $18$ g. So the molecule count must be less than $6\times10^{23}$.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Using a Rounded $N_A$ as the Exact Value

> The exact SI value is $6.02214076\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}$.

The value $6.022\times10^{23}$ is a rounding. It is fine for most problems, but it is not the exact definition.

### #2: Blaming $N_A$ for Significant Figures

> $N_A$ is exact in the SI.

The final significant figures come from the measured or given data, such as the mass. The exact value of $N_A$ does not limit them.

### #3: Calling Every Particle a Molecule

> Molecular substances are counted in molecules. Ionic compounds such as $NaCl$ are counted in formula units.

Name the entity. Some substances fit neither word well. A metal such as copper is counted in atoms.

### #4: Forgetting That an Element May Be Diatomic

> "One mole of oxygen" can mean $16.00$ g or $32.00$ g.

Check whether the problem means atoms, $O$, or molecules, $O_2$. If the problem names the gas as $O_2$, use $32.00\ \mathrm{g/mol}$.

### #5: Mixing Up Mass Number, Atomic Mass, and Molar Mass

> Mass number is a whole count for one isotope. Relative atomic mass is a unitless average. Molar mass is mass per mole.

For chlorine, the relative atomic mass is about $35.45$, the molar mass is about $35.45\ \mathrm{g/mol}$, and the mass numbers of its main isotopes are $35$ and $37$. The three ideas are related but not interchangeable.

### #6: Multiplying When You Should Divide

> To get moles from mass, divide by $M$. To get mass from moles, multiply by $M$.

Check the units. Grams divided by $\mathrm{g/mol}$ gives moles. If your units do not cancel to the unit you want, you used the wrong operation.
