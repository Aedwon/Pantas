## Core Concept

The **periodic table** arranges every known element by increasing atomic number, but its real power is that it groups elements with SIMILAR properties together.

### Families and Groups

The VERTICAL columns are called **groups** (or **families**), numbered $1$ through $18$. Elements in the SAME group share similar chemical behavior, because they have the same number of outer (valence) electrons. The HORIZONTAL rows are called **periods**.

Elements are also classified into three broad categories: **metals** (left and center of the table; shiny, good conductors, tend to LOSE electrons), **nonmetals** (right side; poor conductors, tend to GAIN electrons), and **metalloids** (the staircase boundary between them; properties in between, useful in electronics).

### Periodic Trends (Electronegativity, Ionization Energy, Atomic Radius, Electron Affinity)

Four major properties change in predictable patterns (**trends**) as you move across a period or down a group.

**Atomic radius** (the size of an atom): DECREASES going left to right across a period (more protons pull electrons in closer); INCREASES going down a group (each row adds a whole new electron energy level, making atoms bigger).

**Ionization energy** (the energy needed to remove an electron): INCREASES going left to right across a period (electrons are held more tightly); DECREASES going down a group (outer electrons are farther from the nucleus and easier to remove).

**Electronegativity** (how strongly an atom attracts electrons in a bond): follows the SAME pattern as ionization energy — INCREASES left to right, DECREASES down a group.

**Electron affinity** (how much energy is released when an atom gains an electron): generally INCREASES left to right across a period (nonmetals, on the right, tend to gain electrons more readily than metals).

Notice that atomic radius moves OPPOSITE to the other three trends: it is largest at the bottom-left of the table (where the other three properties are smallest), and smallest at the top-right (where the other three are largest, ignoring noble gases).

## Examples

### #1: Reading Groups and Periods

Two elements are found in the same VERTICAL column of the periodic table but in different rows. What can you predict about their chemical behavior, and why?

**Solution:**
Connect column position to shared valence electrons.

1. Elements in the same COLUMN (group) share the SAME number of valence (outer) electrons.
2. Since chemical behavior is largely determined by valence electrons, these two elements are expected to behave SIMILARLY in chemical reactions, even though they are in different periods (rows) and have different atomic sizes.
3. This is exactly why the periodic table is organized by columns — it groups elements with shared chemical personality together.

### #2: Comparing Atomic Radius

Without looking up exact values, predict which is larger: an atom of sodium (period $3$, group $1$) or an atom of chlorine (period $3$, group $17$).

**Solution:**
Both are in the same period, so apply the LEFT-TO-RIGHT trend.

1. Atomic radius DECREASES moving left to right across a period.
2. Sodium is on the LEFT side of period $3$ (group $1$); chlorine is on the RIGHT side (group $17$).
3. Since sodium is farther left, it has the LARGER atomic radius.

### #3: Comparing Ionization Energy Down a Group

Predict which requires MORE energy to remove an electron: an atom of lithium (period $2$) or an atom of potassium (period $4$), both in group $1$.

**Solution:**
Both are in the same group, so apply the DOWN-A-GROUP trend.

1. Ionization energy DECREASES going down a group, since outer electrons sit farther from the nucleus and are held less tightly.
2. Lithium is HIGHER in the group (period $2$); potassium is LOWER (period $4$).
3. Since lithium is higher up, it has the HIGHER (greater) ionization energy — removing an electron from lithium takes MORE energy than from potassium.

### #4: Metal, Nonmetal, or Metalloid?

An element is shiny, conducts electricity well, and is found on the far left side of the periodic table. A different element is dull, brittle, and a poor conductor, found on the far right side. Classify each.

**Solution:**
Match the described properties and position to the correct category.

1. Shiny, good conductor, far LEFT side: this matches the properties and position of a METAL.
2. Dull, brittle, poor conductor, far RIGHT side: this matches the properties and position of a NONMETAL.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Remember the trend arrows as one diagonal: ionization energy, electronegativity, and electron affinity all INCREASE toward the upper-RIGHT corner of the periodic table (fluorine's neighborhood). Atomic radius does the OPPOSITE, increasing toward the lower-LEFT corner (francium's neighborhood).

For any "which element has a higher/lower X" comparison question, first check: are they in the SAME period (compare left-to-right) or the SAME group (compare top-to-bottom)? Then apply the single matching trend rule.

Remember the metal/nonmetal split with one phrase: "metals lose, nonmetals gain" (electrons), which also explains why metals sit on the side with LOWER ionization energy and electronegativity, and nonmetals sit on the side with HIGHER values for both.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Mixing Up the Direction of the Atomic Radius Trend

> Atomic radius DECREASES left to right, but INCREASES going down — the OPPOSITE direction from the other three major trends.

Since ionization energy, electronegativity, and electron affinity all increase toward the upper right, students often assume atomic radius does too. It is the one trend that runs in reverse, growing largest toward the lower left of the table.

### #2: Comparing Elements That Are Neither Same Period nor Same Group

> The straightforward trend rules only apply directly when comparing elements in the SAME period or SAME group.

Comparing two elements that share neither a period NOR a group (diagonal neighbors, for instance) requires combining both trends carefully, and the result is not always obvious from a single rule. Always check position relationships before applying a trend blindly.

### #3: Assuming All Metalloids Behave Identically

> Metalloids have properties BETWEEN metals and nonmetals, but they are not all identical to each other.

The metalloid category is a spectrum along the staircase boundary, not a single uniform behavior. Their most exam-relevant shared trait is being useful as semiconductors (partial electrical conductors), which is neither fully metallic nor fully nonmetallic.
