## Core Concept

**Chemical nomenclature** is the system of rules for naming compounds, so that any chemist anywhere can know exactly which substance a name refers to. Ionic and covalent compounds follow DIFFERENT naming rules.

### Naming Ionic Compounds

An ionic compound's name is built as: **cation name** (the metal, unchanged) + **anion name** (the nonmetal, with its ending changed to "-ide"). For example, sodium ($Na^+$) and chlorine ($Cl^-$) combine to form sodium CHLORIDE.

If the metal can form MORE THAN ONE possible charge (common for transition metals), a Roman numeral in parentheses shows which charge is used. For example, iron can form $Fe^{2+}$ or $Fe^{3+}$, so $FeCl_2$ is named iron (II) chloride, and $FeCl_3$ is named iron (III) chloride.

Some ionic compounds contain **polyatomic ions**: groups of atoms that stay bonded together and carry an overall charge, like the sulfate ion ($SO_4^{2-}$) or the nitrate ion ($NO_3^-$). These keep their own special name and are NOT renamed with "-ide." A useful pattern: names ending in "-ate" have MORE oxygen atoms than the matching name ending in "-ite" (like sulfATE $SO_4^{2-}$ versus sulfITE $SO_3^{2-}$).

### Naming Covalent Compounds

Covalent compounds form between two NONMETALS, and their names use GREEK PREFIXES to show exactly HOW MANY atoms of each element are present, since nonmetals can combine in several different ratios.

$$1 = \text{mono-} \quad 2 = \text{di-} \quad 3 = \text{tri-} \quad 4 = \text{tetra-} \quad 5 = \text{penta-} \quad 6 = \text{hexa-}$$

The FIRST element keeps its normal name (usually skipping "mono-" for just one atom); the SECOND element's ending changes to "-ide," and always gets a prefix, even if there is only $1$ atom. For example, $CO$ is carbon MONoxide (mono- is used here specifically because leaving it out could be confused with a different compound, $CO_2$), and $CO_2$ is carbon DIoxide.

## Examples

### #1: Naming a Simple Ionic Compound

Name the ionic compound formed between potassium ($K$) and bromine ($Br$).

**Solution:**
Apply the cation-plus-anion pattern.

1. The metal (cation), potassium, keeps its name unchanged: potassium.
2. The nonmetal (anion), bromine, changes its ending to "-ide": bromide.
3. Combined name: potassium BROMIDE.

### #2: Naming an Ionic Compound with a Variable-Charge Metal

Copper can form either $Cu^+$ or $Cu^{2+}$. Name the compound $CuO$ (copper combined with oxide, $O^{2-}$).

**Solution:**
Work out copper's charge in this specific compound, then add the Roman numeral.

1. Oxide has a charge of $2-$. For the compound to be neutral overall, copper's charge here must be $2+$.
2. Since copper CAN form more than one charge, a Roman numeral is required to specify which one is used here: (II).
3. Name: copper (II) oxide.

### #3: Naming a Covalent Compound with Prefixes

Name the covalent compound $N_2O_5$ (two nitrogen atoms and five oxygen atoms).

**Solution:**
Apply prefixes to both elements based on their counts.

1. The first element, nitrogen, has $2$ atoms: prefix "di-", giving "dinitrogen."
2. The second element, oxygen, has $5$ atoms and its ending changes to "-ide": prefix "penta-", giving "pentoxide" (the "a" in "penta-" is dropped before a vowel, forming "pentoxide" instead of "pentaoxide").
3. Full name: dinitrogen pentoxide.

### #4: Writing a Formula from a Polyatomic Ion Name

Write the chemical formula for calcium nitrate, given that calcium forms $Ca^{2+}$ and the nitrate ion is $NO_3^-$.

**Solution:**
Balance the total positive and negative charge to reach zero overall.

1. Calcium contributes a charge of $2+$; each nitrate ion contributes a charge of $1-$.
2. To balance $2+$, TWO nitrate ions are needed, contributing a total charge of $2 \times (1-) = 2-$.
3. Since nitrate is a polyatomic ion made of multiple atoms, its formula must be placed in parentheses when more than one is needed: $Ca(NO_3)_2$.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Quick rule to pick which naming system applies: metal + nonmetal → ionic naming (cation name + anion "-ide," with Roman numerals if needed). Nonmetal + nonmetal → covalent naming (Greek prefixes on both elements).

For writing ionic formulas fast, use the "criss-cross method": take each ion's charge NUMBER (ignoring the sign) and use it as the SUBSCRIPT for the OTHER ion. For calcium ($2+$) and nitrate ($1-$), cross the numbers: calcium gets subscript $1$ (usually omitted), nitrate gets subscript $2$, giving $Ca(NO_3)_2$ directly.

Memorize the "-ate has more oxygen than -ite" rule for polyatomic ions: if you know sulfate is $SO_4^{2-}$, you can figure out sulfite is $SO_3^{2-}$ (one fewer oxygen), without memorizing both from scratch.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Forgetting the Roman Numeral for Variable-Charge Metals

> Metals that can form MORE THAN ONE common charge (like iron, copper, and most transition metals) NEED a Roman numeral in their name.

Naming $FeCl_3$ simply as "iron chloride" is incomplete and ambiguous, since $FeCl_2$ would ALSO be called "iron chloride" under that same incomplete naming. The Roman numeral (III) specifically distinguishes $FeCl_3$ from $FeCl_2$ (which would be iron (II) chloride).

### #2: Dropping the "Mono-" Prefix on the Second Element

> In covalent naming, the SECOND element ALWAYS gets a prefix, even for just $1$ atom — only the FIRST element skips "mono-".

Carbon monoxide ($CO$) keeps "mono-" on oxygen specifically to distinguish it clearly from carbon dioxide ($CO_2$). Dropping "mono-" from the second element's name removes information that could cause confusion between different compounds.

### #3: Renaming a Polyatomic Ion with "-ide"

> Polyatomic ions (like sulfate, nitrate, or hydroxide) keep their OWN special names — do not change them to "-ide," which is reserved for simple, single-atom anions.

A compound like $NaOH$ is named sodium HYDROXIDE (keeping the polyatomic ion's own name), not "sodium hydroxide-ide" or some other altered form. Only simple, single-element anions (like chloride, oxide, or bromide) get the straightforward "-ide" treatment.

### #4: Mixing Up "-ate" and "-ite"

> "-ATE" endings have MORE oxygen atoms than the matching "-ITE" ending for the same element.

Sulfate ($SO_4^{2-}$) and sulfite ($SO_3^{2-}$) are easy to confuse, since they differ only in one oxygen atom and one letter. Getting this backwards writes the wrong formula entirely, even though the compound's overall charge pattern looks similar.
