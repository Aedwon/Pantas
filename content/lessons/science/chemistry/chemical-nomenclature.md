## Core Concept

**Chemical nomenclature** uses standardized naming conventions so chemical formulas and names communicate composition clearly. Different classes of substances use different systems. This lesson focuses on **simple ionic compounds** and **binary molecular compounds**; acids, coordination compounds, and organic compounds have additional rules.

### Naming Ionic Compounds

For an ionic compound, name the **cation first**, then the **anion**.

For a simple compound of a monatomic cation and monatomic anion:

- the cation keeps its element name;
- the monatomic anion uses the element root plus **-ide**.

Example: $NaCl$ is **sodium chloride**.

Ionic compounds are not limited to "metal + nonmetal." Polyatomic ions can be cations or anions. For example, $NH_4Cl$ contains the polyatomic cation ammonium, $NH_4^+$, and is named **ammonium chloride**.

If a metal can form more than one relevant charge, the **Stock system** uses a Roman numeral with no space before the parenthesis: $FeCl_2$ is **iron(II) chloride** and $FeCl_3$ is **iron(III) chloride**. The Roman numeral gives the metal's oxidation state/charge in the simple ionic model, not the number of metal atoms.

Polyatomic ions such as sulfate ($SO_4^{2-}$), sulfite ($SO_3^{2-}$), nitrate ($NO_3^-$), hydroxide ($OH^-$), and ammonium ($NH_4^+$) retain their established ion names.

Within a related **oxyanion series of the same central element**, an **-ate** ion commonly has more oxygen than the corresponding **-ite** ion: sulfate vs. sulfite, nitrate vs. nitrite. Do not generalize this into a rule for unrelated ions, and do not assume every -ate/-ite pair differs by exactly one oxygen unless that pair is known.

### Naming Binary Molecular Compounds

For many **binary molecular compounds** made from two nonmetals, Greek numerical prefixes indicate atom counts:

$$1=\text{mono-},\ 2=\text{di-},\ 3=\text{tri-},\ 4=\text{tetra-},\ 5=\text{penta-},\ 6=\text{hexa-}$$

The first element keeps its element name and usually omits **mono-** when only one atom is present. The second element receives a prefix and an **-ide** ending.

Examples:

- $CO$: carbon monoxide.
- $CO_2$: carbon dioxide.
- $N_2O_5$: dinitrogen pentoxide.

Vowels are sometimes dropped for smoother pronunciation, as in **monoxide** and **pentoxide**.

This prefix system is for the relevant class of binary molecular compounds, not a rule for every substance containing two nonmetals. Organic molecules and many familiar compounds use other systematic or retained names.

### Writing Ionic Formulas

A correct ionic formula has **overall charge zero** and uses the **smallest whole-number ratio of ions**.

For example, calcium is $Ca^{2+}$ and oxide is $O^{2-}$. One of each balances charge:

$$CaO$$

not $Ca_2O_2$.

For calcium nitrate, $Ca^{2+}$ requires two $NO_3^-$ ions:

$$Ca(NO_3)_2$$

Parentheses are used around a polyatomic ion when more than one whole ion is present.

## Examples

### #1: Simple Ionic Compound

Name $KBr$.

**Solution:**

1. $K^+$: potassium.
2. $Br^-$: bromide.
3. Name: **potassium bromide**.

### #2: Variable-Charge Metal

Name $CuO$.

**Solution:**

1. Oxide is $O^{2-}$.
2. Charge neutrality therefore requires $Cu^{2+}$.
3. Name: **copper(II) oxide**.

### #3: Binary Molecular Compound

Name $N_2O_5$.

**Solution:**

1. Two N atoms: **dinitrogen**.
2. Five O atoms: **pentoxide**.
3. Full name: **dinitrogen pentoxide**.

### #4: Polyatomic Ion Formula

Write the formula for calcium nitrate.

1. $Ca^{2+}$ and $NO_3^-$ must sum to zero charge.
2. Two nitrate ions are required per calcium ion.
3. Formula:

$$Ca(NO_3)_2$$

### #5: Why the Criss-Cross Shortcut Must Be Reduced

A student uses the criss-cross shortcut on $Ca^{2+}$ and $O^{2-}$ and writes $Ca_2O_2$. What is wrong?

**Solution:**

1. The charges already cancel in a $1:1$ ratio.
2. Ionic formulas use the simplest whole-number ratio.
3. Reduce $2:2$ to $1:1$.
4. Correct formula: $CaO$.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For simple ionic formulas, solve **charge balance first**. The criss-cross method can be a bookkeeping shortcut, but always reduce subscripts to the smallest ratio and add parentheses correctly for repeated polyatomic ions.

For binary molecular compounds, prefixes encode the subscripts directly.

For Stock names, calculate the variable-charge metal's oxidation state from charge neutrality, then write the Roman numeral immediately after the metal name: **iron(III)**, not "iron (III)."

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Defining Ionic Compounds as Only Metal + Nonmetal

> Ionic compounds can contain polyatomic ions such as ammonium, nitrate, sulfate, or hydroxide.

The defining feature is ionic composition/electrostatic bonding, not a rigid metal/nonmetal checklist.

### #2: Applying Greek Prefixes to Every Covalent Compound

> The mono-, di-, tri- system is used for many binary molecular compounds.

Other classes, especially organic compounds, follow different nomenclature systems.

### #3: Using Criss-Cross Without Reducing

> Ionic formulas use the lowest whole-number ratio consistent with charge neutrality.

$Ca^{2+}$ and $O^{2-}$ form $CaO$, not $Ca_2O_2$.

### #4: Treating -ate/-ite as a Universal Oxygen-Counting Algorithm

> The pattern applies within corresponding oxyanion families, such as nitrate/nitrite and sulfate/sulfite.

Learn the actual common ions; the suffix alone does not determine a formula from scratch for every possible species.
