## Core Concept

Chemists describe acids and bases with more than one model. This lesson uses two. Both work for **aqueous** solutions, which are solutions in water.

### Two Ways to Define Acids and Bases

An **ion** is an atom or group of atoms with an electric charge. In the **Arrhenius** model, an acid makes more hydronium ions, $H_3O^+$, in water. A base makes more hydroxide ions, $OH^-$.

A hydronium ion is a water molecule that has picked up a hydrogen ion, $H^+$. Many books write $H^+$ for it. In water, the two mean the same thing.

The **Brønsted-Lowry** model is wider. A proton here means an $H^+$ ion. An acid is a **proton donor**. A base is a **proton acceptor**. So an acid-base reaction is a **proton transfer**, and it does not need $OH^-$. Water can act as an acid or as a base, depending on its partner.

### pH

The pH of a solution tells how acidic it is. First, some words. The unit $M$ (molar) means moles per liter. A mole is a counting unit for tiny particles. Square brackets mean concentration, so $[H_3O^+]$ is the concentration of hydronium ions, in $M$.

In school problems, pH comes from the hydronium concentration:

$$\mathrm{pH}=-\log_{10}[H_3O^+]$$

The $\log_{10}$ asks: what power of $10$ gives this number? For $10^{-3}$, the answer is $-3$. So when $[H_3O^+]=1\times10^{-n}\ M$, the pH is $n$.

At **25 °C**, pure water has

$$[H_3O^+]=[OH^-]=1.0\times10^{-7}\,M$$

so its pH is $7$. At this temperature:

- pH $<7$: acidic.
- pH $=7$: neutral.
- pH $>7$: basic.

Each step of $1$ in pH changes $[H_3O^+]$ by a factor of $10$. A solution with pH $4$ has $100$ times the hydronium of one with pH $6$. A lower pH means more hydronium.

For a base, use the hydroxide concentration. The **pOH** is $-\log_{10}[OH^-]$. At $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, $[H_3O^+]\times[OH^-]=1.0\times10^{-14}$. So the two numbers add up to $14$:

$$\mathrm{pH}+\mathrm{pOH}=14$$

The numbers $7$ and $14$ hold only near $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$. At other temperatures, pure water is still neutral, because $[H_3O^+]=[OH^-]$. But its pH is not exactly $7$. Very concentrated solutions can also have a pH below $0$ or above $14$. Advanced chemistry uses "activity" in place of concentration, but school problems use concentration.

### Strong and Weak

A **strong acid** ionizes completely in water. Every molecule gives up its proton. A **weak acid** ionizes only a little. Most molecules stay whole, and the mixture settles into a balance called **equilibrium**.

Common strong acids are $HCl$, $HNO_3$, and $H_2SO_4$. Acetic acid, the acid in vinegar, is a common weak acid.

Strong bases are soluble hydroxides, such as $NaOH$ and $Ca(OH)_2$. They split completely into ions. Ammonia, $NH_3$, is a weak base. It reacts only a little with water:

$$NH_3+H_2O\rightleftharpoons NH_4^++OH^-$$

The double arrow means the reaction can go both ways.

**Strength** is how much a substance ionizes. **Concentration** is how much is dissolved in each liter. They are different things. For a strong acid with one hydrogen, such as $HCl$, $[H_3O^+]$ equals the acid's concentration. For a weak acid, $[H_3O^+]$ is much smaller.

### Neutralization

**Neutralization** is the reaction of an acid with a base. For a strong acid and a strong hydroxide, the net ionic equation is

$$H_3O^+ + OH^- \rightarrow 2H_2O$$

A **spectator ion** is an ion that is present before and after the reaction and does not change.

If both are strong and they supply equal amounts of $H_3O^+$ and $OH^-$, the solution is neutral. If acid is left over, it is acidic. If base is left over, it is basic. If one of them is weak, the final solution may not be neutral, even with equal amounts.

Making water is common, but not universal. In the reaction $NH_3 + HCl \rightarrow NH_4Cl$, $NH_3$ takes a proton from $HCl$. No water forms.

## Examples

### #1: Classifying pH at 25 °C

At $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, classify aqueous solutions with pH $2$, $7$, and $11$.

**Solution:**

1. pH $2<7$: acidic.
2. pH $7$: neutral.
3. pH $11>7$: basic.

### #2: Comparing Hydronium Concentrations

At the same temperature, solution A has pH $4$ and solution B has pH $6$. How many times greater is the hydronium concentration of A than of B?

**Solution:**

1. A: $[H_3O^+]=10^{-4}\ M$. B: $[H_3O^+]=10^{-6}\ M$.
2. Divide.

$$\dfrac{10^{-4}}{10^{-6}}=10^{2}=100$$

A has $100$ times the hydronium concentration of B.

### #3: pH of a Strong Acid

Find the pH of $0.0010\ M$ $HCl$ at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$.

**Solution:**

1. $HCl$ is a strong acid with one hydrogen. It ionizes completely, so
$[H_3O^+]=0.0010\ M=1.0\times10^{-3}\ M$.
2. Apply the pH formula.

$$\mathrm{pH}=-\log_{10}(10^{-3})=3$$

### #4: pH of a Strong Base

Find the pH of $0.0050\ M$ $Ca(OH)_2$ at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$.

**Solution:**

1. $Ca(OH)_2$ is a strong base. Each unit gives $2$ hydroxide ions.

$$[OH^-]=2\times0.0050=0.010\ M=10^{-2}\ M$$

2. Find the pOH.

$$\mathrm{pOH}=-\log_{10}(10^{-2})=2$$

3. Subtract from $14$.

$$\mathrm{pH}=14-2=12$$

### #5: Strength and Concentration

A $0.10\ M$ solution of $HCl$ and a $0.10\ M$ solution of acetic acid are compared at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$. Which has the lower pH?

**Solution:**

1. Both have the same concentration, but $HCl$ is strong and acetic acid is weak.
2. $HCl$ ionizes completely, so $[H_3O^+]=0.10\ M$ and the pH is $1$.
3. Acetic acid ionizes only partly, so its $[H_3O^+]$ is less than $0.10\ M$ and its pH is higher than $1$.

The $HCl$ solution has the lower pH.

### #6: Finding the Proton Donor

In each reaction, name the Brønsted-Lowry acid and the base.

(a) $HCl+H_2O\rightarrow H_3O^++Cl^-$

(b) $NH_3+H_2O\rightleftharpoons NH_4^++OH^-$

(c) $NH_3+HCl\rightarrow NH_4Cl$

**Solution:**

1. In (a), $HCl$ gives a proton to water. $HCl$ is the acid and $H_2O$ is the base.
2. In (b), water gives a proton to $NH_3$. $H_2O$ is the acid and $NH_3$ is the base.
3. In (c), $HCl$ gives a proton to $NH_3$. $HCl$ is the acid and $NH_3$ is the base.

Water is a base in (a) and an acid in (b). Reaction (a) and reaction (c) have no $OH^-$ at all, yet both are acid-base reactions.

### #7: Strong Acid and Strong Base

Hydrochloric acid reacts with sodium hydroxide:

$$HCl+NaOH\rightarrow NaCl+H_2O$$

Write the net ionic equation, name the spectator ions, and describe the solution if equal amounts react at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$.

**Solution:**

1. In water, both are strong. The solution holds $H_3O^+$, $Cl^-$, $Na^+$, and $OH^-$.
2. Only $H_3O^+$ and $OH^-$ react.

$$H_3O^+ + OH^- \rightarrow 2H_2O$$

3. $Na^+$ and $Cl^-$ are spectator ions.
4. Equal amounts use up all the $H_3O^+$ and $OH^-$. The solution is neutral, with pH $7$.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For a strong acid with one hydrogen, whose concentration is $1\times10^{-n}\ M$, the pH is $n$.

For a strong base, find the pOH first, then use pH $=14-$ pOH. Count the $OH^-$ ions in each unit.

If the front number is not $1$, the pH sits between two whole numbers. For example, $5\times10^{-4}\ M$ lies between $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-3}$, so the pH is between $3$ and $4$.

Use the pH $7$ divider only when the problem assumes about $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$.

For acid-base reactions, look for a proton moving from one species to another.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Treating 0 to 14 as Absolute Limits

> The familiar scale suits most dilute solutions. It is not a boundary.

Very concentrated acids and bases can fall outside it.

### #2: Saying Neutral Always Means pH 7

> Neutral means equal $H_3O^+$ and $OH^-$. The value $7$ is for about $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$.

At other temperatures, pure water is still neutral, but its pH is not $7$.

### #3: Confusing Strong with Concentrated

> Strength is how much an acid ionizes. Concentration is how much is dissolved.

Both affect pH. A very concentrated weak acid can have a lower pH than a very dilute strong acid.

### #4: Saying Every Neutralization Makes Water

> Brønsted-Lowry neutralization is proton transfer.

A strong acid plus a hydroxide makes water. But $NH_3+HCl\rightarrow NH_4Cl$ shows that "salt plus water" is not universal.

### #5: Forgetting the Hydroxide Count

> pH $+$ pOH $=14$ works only after you find $[OH^-]$ correctly.

$Ca(OH)_2$ gives $2$ hydroxide ions per unit. Using the base's own concentration gives the wrong pOH.
