## Core Concept

**Matter** is anything that has mass and takes up space. Chemistry studies what matter is made of, how it behaves, and how it changes.

### The Particles of Matter

Matter is made of tiny particles. An **atom** is the smallest unit of an element that still has that element's chemical identity. A **molecule** is a group of two or more atoms joined together, such as $H_2O$. An **ion** is an atom or a group of atoms with an electric charge, such as the sodium ion in table salt.

Ordinary matter is made of atoms, molecules, or ions. How these particles are arranged and how they move explain the properties you see.

### Classifying Matter

A **pure substance** has one fixed chemical composition, and it is the same all the way through. Pure substances come in two kinds.

- **Element**: a pure substance made of one kind of atom. Oxygen gas, $O_2$, is an element. Its molecules have two atoms, but both are oxygen atoms.
- **Compound**: a pure substance made of two or more elements joined chemically in a fixed ratio. Water, $H_2O$, always has $2$ hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom.

A compound can be broken down into simpler substances by a chemical reaction. An element cannot be broken down this way.

A compound also has properties different from the elements in it. Sodium is a soft, reactive metal and chlorine is a poisonous gas, but sodium chloride is the table salt we eat.

A **mixture** has two or more substances that are only mixed together, not chemically joined. The amounts can vary. Each substance keeps its own properties.

- A **homogeneous mixture** has the same composition in every part, even down to the particles. Salt dissolved in water and air are examples. A homogeneous mixture is often called a **solution**.
- A **heterogeneous mixture** has parts you can tell apart, such as sand mixed with iron filings.

Looking the same is not enough. Milk looks the same throughout, but it holds tiny droplets of fat mixed in water, so it is not a solution. Such a mixture is called a colloid.

Some mixtures are not liquid or gas. An alloy such as brass is a solid mixture of copper and zinc. Its amounts can vary, so it is a mixture and not a compound.

### Separating a Mixture

The parts of a mixture can be separated by **physical methods**. These are methods that use a difference in a physical property and do not change the substances themselves.

- **Filtration** separates a solid from a liquid by size. The liquid passes through a filter and the solid stays behind.
- **Magnet**: separates a magnetic material, such as iron, from materials that are not attracted.
- **Evaporation** boils or dries away a liquid to leave behind a dissolved solid.
- **Distillation** boils a liquid, then cools the vapor to collect the liquid again. It separates liquids that boil at different temperatures.

Each method works only when the parts differ in the property the method uses. A compound cannot be separated by these methods. It needs a chemical reaction.

### States of Matter

The three common states are solid, liquid, and gas. The **particle model** explains each state with three ideas: how close the particles are, how they move, and how strongly they attract each other.

- **Solid**: the particles touch their neighbors and attract each other strongly. They cannot move past each other. They only vibrate in place. So a solid keeps its own shape and volume.
- **Liquid**: the particles are still close together, but the attraction is weaker than in a solid. They slide past each other. So a liquid has a fixed volume but takes the shape of its container.
- **Gas**: the particles are far apart and move quickly in all directions. The attractions between them are very weak. So a gas spreads out to fill its container, with no fixed shape or volume.

The spacing also explains other behavior. A gas can be squeezed into a smaller space because most of it is empty space between particles. A liquid or solid is hard to squeeze because the particles already touch.

Gas particles also spread out and mix with other gases by themselves. This spreading is called **diffusion**.

**Temperature** measures the average energy of motion of the particles. Warmer particles move faster. Heating a sample usually raises its temperature, so its particles move faster. The exception is a phase change, explained below, where heat goes in and the temperature stays the same.

Solids come in two kinds. A **crystalline solid**, such as salt, has particles in a repeating pattern. An **amorphous solid**, such as glass, has particles close and fixed but not in a repeating pattern.

A fourth state is **plasma**. It is a gas in which many atoms have lost or gained electrons, so it contains charged particles. Plasma is found in stars, lightning, and fluorescent lamps.

Very high temperature can make a plasma, but plasma is defined by its charged particles, not by being hot.

### Phase Changes

A **phase change** is a change from one state to another. Each phase change has a name.

- **Melting**: solid to liquid.
- **Freezing**: liquid to solid.
- **Vaporization**: liquid to gas. **Evaporation** happens at the surface of a liquid and can happen at any temperature. **Boiling** happens throughout the liquid, with bubbles of vapor forming inside it.
- **Condensation**: gas to liquid.
- **Sublimation**: solid straight to gas, with no liquid in between. Dry ice, which is solid carbon dioxide, does this at normal air pressure.
- **Deposition**: gas straight to solid. Frost forming from water vapor is an example.

A phase change is a **physical change**, because the particles stay the same. Only their arrangement and movement change. Which state is stable depends on temperature and pressure.

Energy explains the direction. Melting, vaporization, and sublimation take in energy from the surroundings, because attractions between particles must be loosened or broken. A change that takes in energy is **endothermic**.

Freezing, condensation, and deposition give energy out to the surroundings, because attractions form. A change that gives out energy is **exothermic**. This follows the attractions, not the spacing: ice is less dense than liquid water, yet melting ice still takes in energy.

During a phase change, the added energy loosens the attractions between particles. It does not speed them up. So the temperature of a pure substance stays constant while it melts or boils, if the pressure stays constant. Ice at $0^\circ\text{C}$ stays at $0^\circ\text{C}$ until all of it has melted.

At normal air pressure, water boils at $100^\circ\text{C}$. At lower pressure, such as on a high mountain, water boils at a lower temperature.

### Physical and Chemical Properties and Changes

A **physical property** is something you can observe or measure without changing what the substance is made of. Examples are color, density, melting point, boiling point, hardness, solubility (how much dissolves in a liquid), and electrical conductivity.

A **chemical property** describes how a substance can react to become a different substance. Flammability (it can burn) and the ability to rust are examples. You can only observe a chemical property when a reaction happens.

A **physical change** changes the form, state, or arrangement of a substance, but the substance is still the same. Melting, tearing paper, crushing a can, and making a mixture are physical changes.

Dissolving is usually a physical change too. Salt or sugar stirred into water can be recovered by evaporating the water.

A **chemical change**, also called a chemical reaction, turns the starting substances into new substances with different properties. The starting substances are the **reactants** and the new substances are the **products**.

Burning wood, rusting iron, cooking an egg, and digesting food are chemical changes.

Signs of a chemical change include a new color, a new gas, a solid forming in a liquid, a new smell, and heat or light given off. These signs are only clues. Boiling water makes bubbles and ice melting takes in heat, yet both are physical. The real test is whether a new substance forms.

## Examples

### #1: Classifying a Sample

Classify (a) pure $O_2$, (b) pure $NaCl$, and (c) lemonade as an element, a compound, or a mixture.

**Solution:**

1. $O_2$ has only oxygen atoms: **element**.
2. $NaCl$ is a pure substance with sodium and chlorine in a fixed ratio: **compound**.
3. Lemonade has water, sugar, and acids in amounts that can vary: **mixture**.

### #2: Compound or Mixture?

A student heats a gray powder of iron filings and yellow sulfur powder. Before heating, a magnet pulls the iron out of the powder. After strong heating, the product is a dark solid that the magnet does not pull. What is the powder before heating, and what is the dark solid?

**Solution:**

1. Before heating, a magnet separates the iron, so each part keeps its own properties: **mixture**.
2. After heating, the iron and sulfur have reacted and the magnet no longer separates them.
3. The product has new properties and a fixed composition: **compound**.

### #3: Choosing a Separation Method

A sample is salt mixed with sand. How can the two be separated, and why does the method work?

**Solution:**

1. Salt dissolves in water, but sand does not. Add water and stir.
2. Filter the mixture. The sand stays on the filter.
3. Evaporate the water from the liquid that passed through. The salt is left behind.

### #4: Particles and States

A syringe with the tip sealed is full of air. A second sealed syringe is full of water. Both plungers are pushed in. Which syringe can be pushed in farther, and why?

**Solution:**

1. Gas particles are far apart, with mostly empty space between them.
2. Pushing the plunger moves the gas particles closer together, so the air syringe goes in farther.
3. Liquid particles already touch their neighbors, so the water syringe barely moves.

### #5: Identifying a Phase Change

Water vapor touches a cold surface and forms liquid droplets. What process happened, and was energy taken in or given out by the water?

**Solution:**

1. Gas to liquid is **condensation**.
2. Condensation gives energy out to the surroundings, so it is exothermic for the water.

### #6: Temperature During a Phase Change

A pure sample of ice at $-10^\circ\text{C}$ is heated steadily at normal air pressure. What happens to its temperature as it warms, melts, and then warms again as liquid? Where does the energy go while it melts?

**Solution:**

1. The ice warms up to $0^\circ\text{C}$ because its particles move faster.
2. At $0^\circ\text{C}$ it melts. The temperature stays at $0^\circ\text{C}$ until all the ice is liquid.
3. The energy added during melting loosens the attractions that hold particles in place, so it does not raise the temperature.
4. After all the ice has melted, the liquid warms above $0^\circ\text{C}$.

### #7: Sublimation

A block of dry ice, at normal air pressure, shrinks and gives off cold gas without leaving a puddle. Name the process and say whether it takes in or gives out energy.

**Solution:**

1. Solid to gas with no liquid is **sublimation**.
2. Sublimation takes in energy from the surroundings, so it is endothermic. This is why the air around dry ice feels cold.
3. It is a physical change, because the carbon dioxide is still carbon dioxide.

### #8: Property or Change?

Classify each. (a) Iron is attracted by a magnet. (b) Paper is torn. (c) Iron rusts. (d) Iron can react with oxygen.

**Solution:**

1. Attracted by a magnet: **physical property**.
2. Tearing: **physical change**.
3. Rusting: **chemical change**, because a new substance forms.
4. Ability to react with oxygen: **chemical property**.

### #9: Physical or Chemical Change?

Classify each change. (a) Sugar dissolves in tea. (b) A candle burns. (c) Wax near the candle flame melts. (d) A silver spoon turns dark in air with sulfur compounds.

**Solution:**

1. Dissolving sugar: physical. The sugar can be recovered by evaporating the tea.
2. Burning candle: chemical. New substances form, and heat and light are given off.
3. Melting wax: physical. It is still wax.
4. Silver turning dark: chemical. A new substance forms on the surface.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

To classify a sample, first ask whether it is pure. If it is not pure, it is a mixture. If it is pure, ask whether it has one kind of atom (element) or more than one (compound).

For a phase change, find the starting and ending states, then name the change. Melting, vaporization, and sublimation take in energy. Freezing, condensation, and deposition give out energy. The reason is the attractions between particles, not the spacing, so this holds even for water, where ice is less dense than liquid. It holds when the substance is not reacting.

For property and change questions, ask whether a new substance forms. A change that makes a new substance is chemical. A property that describes the ability to make a new substance is a chemical property.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Saying an Element Can Never Change Into Another Element

> Ordinary chemical reactions cannot break an element down, but nuclear reactions can change one element into another.

The chemistry definition of an element refers to chemical reactions only.

### #2: Thinking Every Solid Has a Perfect Crystal Pattern

> Crystalline solids have a repeating pattern. Amorphous solids such as glass do not.

The picture of particles in a neat grid fits crystals, not every solid.

### #3: Calling Plasma Just a Very Hot Gas

> Plasma has many charged particles. Heat is one way to make them, but it is not the definition.

A very hot gas can still have no charged particles. Plasma is defined by the charges.

### #4: Mixing Up Evaporation and Boiling

> Both are vaporization, but evaporation happens at the surface at any temperature, and boiling happens throughout the liquid at the boiling point.

A puddle dries without ever reaching $100^\circ\text{C}$. That is evaporation, not boiling.

### #5: Treating Bubbles or Color as Proof of a Chemical Change

> Bubbles, heat, or a color change are clues, not proof. Check whether a new substance forms.

Boiling water makes bubbles, and dissolving a colored powder in water changes the color of the water, but no new substance forms in either.

### #6: Thinking the Temperature Rises Through a Whole Heating Process

> A pure substance holds a constant temperature during melting or boiling at constant pressure.

Mixtures, such as many alloys, often melt over a range of temperatures instead.
