## Core Concept

**Stoichiometry** is the math of amounts in a chemical reaction. The coefficients in a balanced equation tell you how many moles of each substance react or form. This lesson uses the mole and molar mass from the mole concept lesson.

### Mole Ratios

Look at $2H_2+O_2\rightarrow2H_2O$. The coefficients say that $2$ mol $H_2$ react with $1$ mol $O_2$ to form $2$ mol $H_2O$.

The coefficients give a ratio of moles, not of grams. The equation must be balanced first. Then any two substances in it make a mole ratio, such as $\dfrac{2\text{ mol }H_2O}{1\text{ mol }O_2}$.

### From Mass to Mass in Three Steps

A balance measures grams, but the equation works in moles. So a mass problem has three steps:

1. Change the given mass to moles: $n=\dfrac{m}{M}$.
2. Use the mole ratio from the equation to find the moles of the substance you want.
3. Change those moles to grams: $m=nM$.

Here $n$ is moles, $m$ is mass, and $M$ is molar mass. If the problem gives moles, skip step $1$. If it asks for moles, skip step $3$.

### Limiting and Excess Reactants

Reactants are often not supplied in the exact ratio of the equation. The **limiting reactant** is the one that runs out first. It stops the reaction and sets how much product can form.

A reactant that is left over is **in excess**.

To find the limiting reactant, calculate how much of the same product each reactant could make if the others were unlimited. The reactant that gives the smaller amount is limiting.

### Theoretical and Percent Yield

The **theoretical yield** is the amount of product you calculate from the limiting reactant. The **actual yield** is the amount you really collect in the experiment. Percent yield compares them, in the same unit:

$$\%\text{ yield}=\dfrac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}}\times100\%$$

Actual yield is usually smaller. Some reactant may not react, side reactions may use it, or product may be lost while collecting it.

### Empirical and Molecular Formulas

An **empirical formula** gives the simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms in a compound. A **molecular formula** gives the real number of each atom in one molecule. It is the empirical formula multiplied by a whole number.

To find an empirical formula from composition data:

1. Change each element's amount to moles. If you are given percents, assume a $100$ g sample, so each percent becomes grams.
2. Divide all the mole amounts by the smallest one.
3. If a ratio is very close to a whole number, such as $2.01$, round it.
4. If a ratio is a simple fraction, multiply all the ratios by the same small whole number: $\times2$ for $1.5$, $\times3$ for $1.33$ or $1.67$, and $\times4$ for $1.25$ or $1.75$, and $\times5$ for $1.2$, $1.4$, $1.6$ or $1.8$.

To find the molecular formula, divide the compound's molar mass by the empirical formula mass. The **empirical formula mass** is the mass of one empirical formula unit: add up the molar masses of its atoms, using each subscript. For $CH_2O$ it is $12+2(1)+16=30$ g/mol. The answer $k$ should be close to a whole number:

$$k=\dfrac{\text{molar mass of compound}}{\text{empirical formula mass}}$$

Multiply every subscript in the empirical formula by $k$.

Ionic compounds have no separate molecules, so they have no molecular formula. The formula of most simple ionic compounds, such as $NaCl$, already shows the simplest ratio of atoms. An exception is a compound with a polyatomic ion made of two or more atoms. Sodium peroxide is written $Na_2O_2$, but its simplest atom ratio is $NaO$.

## Examples

Use the molar masses given in each problem.

### #1: Moles to Moles and to Mass

For $N_2+3H_2\rightarrow2NH_3$, how many moles and how many grams of $NH_3$ form from $6$ mol $H_2$ with excess $N_2$? The molar mass of $NH_3$ is $17$ g/mol.

**Solution:**

1. The ratio is $2$ mol $NH_3$ for every $3$ mol $H_2$.
2. Find the moles of $NH_3$:

$$6\text{ mol }H_2\times\dfrac{2\text{ mol }NH_3}{3\text{ mol }H_2}=4\text{ mol }NH_3$$

3. Change moles to grams:

$$4\text{ mol}\times17\text{ g/mol}=68\text{ g }NH_3$$

### #2: Mass to Mass

For $2H_2+O_2\rightarrow2H_2O$, find the mass of water formed from $8.0$ g $H_2$ with excess $O_2$. The molar masses are $2.0$ g/mol for $H_2$ and $18$ g/mol for $H_2O$.

**Solution:**

1. Change grams to moles:

$$\dfrac{8.0\text{ g}}{2.0\text{ g/mol}}=4.0\text{ mol }H_2$$

2. The ratio is $2$ mol $H_2O$ for every $2$ mol $H_2$, so $4.0$ mol $H_2O$ form.
3. Change moles to grams:

$$4.0\text{ mol}\times18\text{ g/mol}=72\text{ g }H_2O$$

### #3: Limiting Reactant, Product, and Excess Left

For $N_2+3H_2\rightarrow2NH_3$, a chemist mixes $4$ mol $N_2$ and $9$ mol $H_2$. Identify the limiting reactant, the moles of $NH_3$ that can form, and the moles of excess reactant left.

**Solution:**

1. Find the $NH_3$ each reactant could make:

$$4\text{ mol }N_2\times\dfrac{2\text{ mol }NH_3}{1\text{ mol }N_2}=8\text{ mol }NH_3$$

$$9\text{ mol }H_2\times\dfrac{2\text{ mol }NH_3}{3\text{ mol }H_2}=6\text{ mol }NH_3$$

2. $H_2$ gives the smaller amount, so $H_2$ is limiting and $6$ mol $NH_3$ can form.
3. All $9$ mol $H_2$ react with $3$ mol $N_2$, because the ratio is $1:3$.
4. The excess left is $4-3=1$ mol $N_2$.

### #4: Limiting Reactant from Masses

For $2Mg+O_2\rightarrow2MgO$, $12$ g $Mg$ react with $4.0$ g $O_2$. The molar masses are $24$ g/mol for $Mg$, $32$ g/mol for $O_2$, and $40$ g/mol for $MgO$. Find the theoretical yield of $MgO$ and the mass of excess reactant left.

**Solution:**

1. Moles of $Mg$: $\dfrac{12}{24}=0.50$ mol. Moles of $O_2$: $\dfrac{4.0}{32}=0.125$ mol.
2. Find the $MgO$ each reactant could make:

$$0.50\text{ mol }Mg\times\dfrac{2\text{ mol }MgO}{2\text{ mol }Mg}=0.50\text{ mol }MgO$$

$$0.125\text{ mol }O_2\times\dfrac{2\text{ mol }MgO}{1\text{ mol }O_2}=0.25\text{ mol }MgO$$

3. $O_2$ gives the smaller amount, so $O_2$ is limiting.
4. The theoretical yield is:

$$0.25\text{ mol}\times40\text{ g/mol}=10\text{ g }MgO$$

5. $0.125$ mol $O_2$ react with $0.25$ mol $Mg$. That leaves $0.50-0.25=0.25$ mol $Mg$, which is $0.25\times24=6.0$ g.

### #5: Percent Yield from Reactant Data

Heating $50$ g $CaCO_3$ follows $CaCO_3\rightarrow CaO+CO_2$. A student collects $21$ g $CaO$. The molar masses are $100$ g/mol for $CaCO_3$ and $56$ g/mol for $CaO$. What is the percent yield of $CaO$?

**Solution:**

1. Moles of $CaCO_3$: $\dfrac{50}{100}=0.50$ mol.
2. The ratio is $1:1$, so the theoretical yield is $0.50$ mol $CaO$.
3. Theoretical mass:

$$0.50\text{ mol}\times56\text{ g/mol}=28\text{ g }CaO$$

4. Percent yield:

$$\dfrac{21}{28}\times100\%=75\%$$

### #6: Empirical Formula from Percent Composition

A compound is $40.0\%$ carbon, $6.7\%$ hydrogen, and $53.3\%$ oxygen by mass. Use $12$ g/mol for $C$, $1$ g/mol for $H$, and $16$ g/mol for $O$. Find its empirical formula.

**Solution:**

1. Assume $100$ g. The masses are $40.0$ g $C$, $6.7$ g $H$, and $53.3$ g $O$.
2. Change to moles: $\dfrac{40.0}{12}=3.33$ for $C$, $\dfrac{6.7}{1}=6.7$ for $H$, and $\dfrac{53.3}{16}=3.33$ for $O$.
3. Divide by the smallest, $3.33$:

$$C:H:O=1:2.01:1$$

4. $2.01$ is very close to $2$, so round it. The empirical formula is $CH_2O$.

### #7: Empirical Formula Needing a Multiplier

A sample has $11.2$ g iron and $4.8$ g oxygen. The molar masses are $56$ g/mol for $Fe$ and $16$ g/mol for $O$. Find the empirical formula.

**Solution:**

1. Moles of $Fe$: $\dfrac{11.2}{56}=0.20$. Moles of $O$: $\dfrac{4.8}{16}=0.30$.
2. Divide by the smallest, $0.20$:

$$Fe:O=1:1.5$$

3. $1.5$ is not a whole number, so multiply both by $2$:

$$Fe:O=2:3$$

The empirical formula is $Fe_2O_3$.

### #8: Molecular Formula

A compound has empirical formula $CH_2O$ and a molar mass of $180$ g/mol. Use $12$, $1$, and $16$ g/mol for $C$, $H$, and $O$. Find the molecular formula.

**Solution:**

1. Empirical formula mass: $12+2(1)+16=30$ g/mol.
2. Find the multiplier:

$$k=\dfrac{180}{30}=6$$

3. Multiply every subscript by $6$. The molecular formula is $C_6H_{12}O_6$.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

To find the limiting reactant fast, divide each reactant's moles by its coefficient. The smaller result is limiting. In Example #3, $\dfrac{4}{1}=4$ for $N_2$ and $\dfrac{9}{3}=3$ for $H_2$, so $H_2$ is limiting.

For empirical formulas, divide by the smallest first and then look at the ratios before you round. Ratios like $1.5$, $1.33$, and $1.25$ need a multiplier. A value such as $2.01$ or $0.98$ is just rounding.

The multiplier $k$ must be close to a whole number. If it is not, recheck the empirical formula or the molar mass.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Comparing Raw Amounts

> The reactant with fewer moles or fewer grams is not always limiting.

The coefficients decide how much of each reactant the equation needs. In Example #3, $4$ mol $N_2$ is fewer than $9$ mol $H_2$, but $N_2$ is the one in excess.

### #2: Treating Coefficients as Grams

> Coefficients are mole ratios, not mass ratios.

In $2H_2+O_2\rightarrow2H_2O$, the ratio of $H_2$ to $O_2$ is $2:1$ in moles. In grams it is $4:32$. Change to moles before you use the ratio.

### #3: Using the Excess Reactant for Yield

> The theoretical yield comes from the limiting reactant only.

The excess reactant would give a larger product amount. That amount can never form, because the limiting reactant runs out first.

### #4: Rounding $1.5$ to $2$

> Scale the whole ratio together.

A ratio of $1:1.5$ becomes $2:3$, not $1:2$. Rounding one number alone changes the compound.

### #5: A Yield Over $100\%$

> Real product cannot exceed the theoretical yield.

If a measured mass gives more than $100\%$, the product was probably wet or impure, or the measurement was off. It does not mean the reaction made extra product.

### #6: Calling Every Formula Molecular

> Ionic solids have formula units, not molecules.

For an ionic compound such as $NaCl$, the formula shows the ratio of its ions. Only a molecular compound can have a molecular formula that is a multiple of its empirical formula. Check for a polyatomic ion, as in $Na_2O_2$, before you reduce an ionic formula.
