## Core Concept

A **solution** is a mixture that looks the same all the way through, even under a microscope. Scientists call this **homogeneous**. The particles are mixed at the level of single molecules or ions.

Most solutions we meet are liquids, like salt in water. But solutions can be solid or gas too. Air is a gas solution. Brass is a solid solution of zinc in copper.

The **solvent** is the part that decides the physical state of the solution. It is often the part present in the largest amount. The other parts are the **solutes**. In salt water, water is the solvent and salt is the solute.

"The solute is the smaller amount" works for most simple cases. It is a handy rule, not the true definition. In a mixture of $50\%$ alcohol and $50\%$ water, the amounts are equal, so size cannot decide.

### Moles, in Brief

Some measures below use moles. A **mole** is a counting unit for tiny particles, like a dozen is a counting unit for eggs. One mole is $6.022\times10^{23}$ particles. The **molar mass** of a substance is the mass of one mole of it, in grams per mole ($\mathrm{g/mol}$).

To change grams to moles, divide the mass by the molar mass:

$$\text{moles}=\dfrac{\text{mass in g}}{\text{molar mass in g/mol}}$$

The Mole Concept lesson teaches this fully.

### Solubility

**Solubility** is the most solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent under stated conditions. It is often written as grams of solute per $100$ g of water. The temperature must be stated. For a gas, the pressure must be stated too.

A **saturated** solution holds all the solute it can at that temperature. If extra solute is present, it stays undissolved. Dissolving and coming out of solution then happen at the same rate, so the amount dissolved stays the same.

An **unsaturated** solution holds less solute than that limit. More solute can still dissolve.

A **supersaturated** solution holds more solute than the limit. This can happen when a saturated solution is cooled slowly and no crystals form. It is unstable. A tiny crystal or a shake can make the extra solute crystallize at once.

### Temperature and Pressure

For many solids in water, solubility rises as the temperature rises. This is a common pattern, not a law. Some solids change very little. A few, such as cerium(III) sulfate, dissolve less when hot.

Gases behave differently. In liquids, the solubility of a gas usually falls as the temperature rises. It rises as the pressure of that gas above the liquid rises. A sealed soft drink holds dissolved carbon dioxide under pressure. Opening the bottle lowers the pressure, and gas bubbles out.

A **solubility curve** is a graph of solubility against temperature. Each point on the curve is a saturated solution. A point below the curve is unsaturated. A point above it is supersaturated, or has undissolved solute.

### Concentration Measures

**Concentration** tells how much solute is in a certain amount of solution or solvent. Read the denominator of each measure with care. Each one divides by something different.

**Molarity** ($M$) is moles of solute per liter of **solution**. A liter of solution is the final volume, after the solute has dissolved:

$$M=\dfrac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{liters of solution}}$$

A solution that is $0.50\,M$ is "$0.50$ molar". Its unit is $\mathrm{mol/L}$. Change milliliters to liters by dividing by $1000$.

**Molality** ($m$, a small letter) is moles of solute per kilogram of **solvent**. Only the solvent is weighed here:

$$m=\dfrac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{kilograms of solvent}}$$

Its unit is $\mathrm{mol/kg}$. Change grams to kilograms by dividing by $1000$.

**Percent by mass** compares the mass of solute with the mass of the whole solution. The mass of the solution is the solute mass plus the solvent mass, if nothing is lost:

$$\%\text{ by mass}=\dfrac{\text{mass of solute}}{\text{mass of solution}}\times100\%$$

**Percent by volume** is used for mixtures of liquids. It compares the volume of the solute with the final volume of the solution:

$$\%\text{ by volume}=\dfrac{\text{volume of solute}}{\text{volume of solution}}\times100\%$$

Use the measured final volume. Do not add the volumes of the parts. When ethanol and water are mixed, the final volume is a little less than the sum. The molecules pack more closely.

### Which Measures Change with Temperature

Mass does not change when a sealed solution is warmed. So molality and percent by mass stay the same.

Volume does change. Liquids usually expand when warmed. The same amount of solute is then in more liters, so molarity falls. Percent by volume also changes, because the volumes change.

### Dilution

To **dilute** a solution means to add more solvent. The amount of solute stays the same. Only the volume gets bigger, so the concentration gets smaller.

Let $M_1$ and $V_1$ be the molarity and volume before. Let $M_2$ and $V_2$ be the molarity and final volume after. Moles of solute equal $M\times V$, and these moles are the same before and after:

$$M_1V_1=M_2V_2$$

Use the same volume unit on both sides, such as mL. The relation works in the same way for any concentration measure that is "amount per volume". It does not work when two different solutions with different solutes are mixed, or when a reaction happens.

### Reading Product Labels and Handling Chemicals Safely

Many household products are solutions. A label may say "$70\%$ ethyl alcohol by volume". This means $70$ mL of ethanol in every $100$ mL of product. The rest is water and other ingredients.

Read the label for the concentration, the directions, and the warnings. Dilute a product only as the label says. Keep it in its original, labeled container, away from food and from children.

Do not mix cleaning products unless the label says it is safe. Household bleach mixed with ammonia makes toxic vapors. Bleach mixed with an acid, such as some toilet cleaners, releases chlorine gas.

## Examples

### #1: Saturated or Not

A made-up solid X has a solubility of $30$ g per $100$ g of water at $20^\circ\mathrm{C}$. A student stirs $40$ g of X into $100$ g of water at $20^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Describe the mixture. Then describe $20$ g of X in the same water.

**Solution:**

1. At $20^\circ\mathrm{C}$, only $30$ g of X can dissolve in $100$ g of water.
2. With $40$ g added, $30$ g dissolves and $10$ g stays undissolved. The solution is saturated.
3. With $20$ g added, all of it dissolves. The solution holds less than $30$ g, so it is unsaturated. It could take $10$ g more.

### #2: Reading a Solubility Curve

For solid X, the solubility is $50$ g per $100$ g of water at $60^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $30$ g per $100$ g of water at $20^\circ\mathrm{C}$. A saturated solution is made with $200$ g of water at $60^\circ\mathrm{C}$. It is cooled to $20^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and crystals form until it is saturated again. How many grams of X crystallize?

**Solution:**

1. At $60^\circ\mathrm{C}$, $200$ g of water holds $2\times50=100$ g of X.
2. At $20^\circ\mathrm{C}$, $200$ g of water holds $2\times30=60$ g of X.
3. The extra solute leaves the solution as crystals:

$$100-60=40\ \text{g}$$

If the solution is cooled slowly with no crystals forming, it holds $100$ g at $20^\circ\mathrm{C}$. It is then supersaturated.

### #3: Molarity from a Mass

A student dissolves $11.7$ g of $NaCl$ in water to make $500$ mL of solution. The molar mass of $NaCl$ is $58.5\ \mathrm{g/mol}$. Find the molarity.

**Solution:**

1. Find the moles of solute:

$$\dfrac{11.7}{58.5}=0.200\ \text{mol}$$

2. Change the volume to liters: $500\ \text{mL}=0.500\ \text{L}$.
3. Divide:

$$M=\dfrac{0.200}{0.500}=0.400\,M$$

### #4: Molality from a Mass

A solution is made by dissolving $18.0$ g of glucose in $500$ g of water. The molar mass of glucose is $180\ \mathrm{g/mol}$. Find the molality.

**Solution:**

1. Find the moles of glucose:

$$\dfrac{18.0}{180}=0.100\ \text{mol}$$

2. Change the mass of solvent to kilograms: $500\ \text{g}=0.500\ \text{kg}$.
3. Divide:

$$m=\dfrac{0.100}{0.500}=0.200\ \mathrm{mol/kg}$$

The volume of the final solution was not needed.

### #5: Percent by Mass

A student dissolves $20$ g of salt in $180$ g of water. No material is lost. Find the percent by mass of salt.

**Solution:**

1. Find the mass of the solution: $20+180=200$ g.
2. Divide the solute mass by it:

$$\dfrac{20}{200}\times100\%=10\%$$

### #6: Mass of Solute in a Percent Solution

A bottle holds $250$ g of a solution that is $8\%$ sugar by mass. How many grams of sugar and how many grams of water does it hold?

**Solution:**

1. Write $8\%$ as $0.08$ and multiply by the solution mass:

$$0.08\times250=20\ \text{g of sugar}$$

2. The rest is water:

$$250-20=230\ \text{g of water}$$

### #7: Percent by Volume

A drink is made by mixing $40$ mL of ethanol with water until the final volume is $200$ mL. Find the percent by volume of ethanol.

**Solution:**

1. The solute volume is $40$ mL. The solution volume is the final volume, $200$ mL.
2. Divide:

$$\dfrac{40}{200}\times100\%=20\%$$

The volume of water added is not $160$ mL, so do not use $40+160$. Use the measured final volume.

### #8: Dilution

A student takes $25.0$ mL of a $2.0\,M$ solution and adds water until the final volume is $100.0$ mL. What is the new molarity? How much of the $2.0\,M$ solution is needed to make $250$ mL of $0.20\,M$ solution?

**Solution:**

1. The moles of solute stay the same, so $M_1V_1=M_2V_2$.
2. For the first part:

$$M_2=\dfrac{M_1V_1}{V_2}=\dfrac{2.0\times25.0}{100.0}=0.50\,M$$

3. For the second part, solve for $V_1$:

$$V_1=\dfrac{M_2V_2}{M_1}=\dfrac{0.20\times250}{2.0}=25\ \text{mL}$$

Take $25$ mL of the $2.0\,M$ solution and add water until the final volume is $250$ mL.

### #9: Reading a Label

A bottle holds $500$ mL of rubbing alcohol. The label says "$70\%$ ethyl alcohol by volume". How many milliliters of ethyl alcohol are in the bottle?

**Solution:**

1. Percent by volume is the solute volume divided by the product volume, times $100\%$.
2. Write $70\%$ as $0.70$ and multiply:

$$0.70\times500=350\ \text{mL}$$

The bottle holds $350$ mL of ethyl alcohol. The rest is water and other ingredients.

### #10: Why Molarity Can Change with Temperature

A sealed solution is warmed with nothing lost or added. Why can its molarity change while its molality stays constant?

**Solution:**

1. The moles of solute and the mass of solvent stay fixed.
2. Molality divides by kilograms of solvent. That mass does not change, so molality does not change.
3. Molarity divides by liters of solution. Warming can change that volume.
4. So molarity can change, even though the amount of each substance is the same.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Read the denominator first. Liters of solution means molarity. Kilograms of solvent means molality. Mass of solution means percent by mass. Volume of solution means percent by volume.

Before using a mass or volume, check its unit. Molarity needs liters. Molality needs kilograms. Change mL and g first.

For dilution, use $M_1V_1=M_2V_2$ when the same solute is only being diluted, with the same unit for $V_1$ and $V_2$. The new volume must be larger than the old one, and the new molarity must be smaller. If not, check your work.

For a solubility question, find the temperature first. Then compare the amount of solute with the solubility at that temperature. If the amount is less, the solution is unsaturated. If it is equal, saturated. If it is more, then some solute stays undissolved, or the solution is supersaturated.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Assuming Solubility Rises with Temperature

> Many solids dissolve more when hot, but not all do, and gases usually dissolve less.

Check the substance and the state. A warm soft drink loses its gas faster than a cold one, because gas solubility usually falls as temperature rises.

### #2: Ignoring Pressure for Gases

> The solubility of a gas rises with the pressure of that gas above the liquid.

Pressure has little effect on the solubility of most solids and liquids in water. For gases it matters a lot. This is why a drink fizzes when the bottle is opened.

### #3: Confusing Molarity and Molality

> Molarity divides by solution volume. Molality divides by solvent mass.

The volume of solvent alone is not the denominator of molarity. Mixing $1$ mol of solute with $1$ L of water does not give a $1\,M$ solution, because the final volume may not be $1$ L.

### #4: Adding Liquid Volumes

> Use the measured final volume, not the sum of the parts.

Volumes of liquids may not add exactly. Percent by volume and molarity both need the final volume. Mass percent is safer here, because masses add when nothing is lost.

### #5: Using the Dilution Relation in the Wrong Place

> $M_1V_1=M_2V_2$ works only when the amount of solute is the same before and after.

It fits adding solvent to one solution. It does not fit mixing two solutions of different solutes, or a reaction that uses up the solute. Also, $V_2$ is the final volume, not the volume of water added.

### #6: Mixing Products to Make Them Stronger

> Do not mix household cleaners unless the label says it is safe.

Different products can react. Bleach with ammonia or with an acid releases toxic gases. Use one product at a time and follow its label.
