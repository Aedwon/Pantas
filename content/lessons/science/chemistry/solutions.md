## Core Concept

A **solution** is a homogeneous (uniform) mixture where one substance dissolves completely into another. The **solute** is the substance being dissolved (usually the smaller amount, like sugar). The **solvent** is the substance doing the dissolving (usually the larger amount, like water).

**Solubility** describes the maximum amount of a solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent, at a certain temperature. A solution holding as much dissolved solute as possible is **saturated**; one holding less than the maximum is **unsaturated**. For most solid solutes, solubility increases with temperature (more sugar dissolves in hot tea than in cold tea).

Chemists use several different ways to describe how concentrated a solution is.

**Molarity** ($M$): moles of solute per liter of solution.

$$M = \dfrac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{liters of solution}}$$

**Molality** ($m$): moles of solute per kilogram of solvent only (not the whole solution). Because it is based on the solvent's mass rather than the solution's volume, molality does not change with temperature (unlike molarity, since volume can expand or shrink with heat).

$$m = \dfrac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{kilograms of solvent}}$$

**Percent by mass**: the solute's mass as a percentage of the total solution's mass.

$$\text{percent by mass} = \dfrac{\text{mass of solute}}{\text{mass of solution}} \times 100\%$$

**Percent by volume**: the solute's volume as a percentage of the total solution's volume, commonly used for liquid-in-liquid solutions (like alcohol in water).

$$\text{percent by volume} = \dfrac{\text{volume of solute}}{\text{volume of solution}} \times 100\%$$

## Examples

### #1: Calculating Molarity

A solution contains $2$ moles of $NaCl$ dissolved in enough water to make $4$ liters of solution. Find the molarity.

**Solution:**
Apply the molarity formula directly.

1. Divide moles of solute by liters of solution.

$$M = \dfrac{2 \text{ mol}}{4 \text{ L}}$$

2. Compute.

$$M = 0.5 \text{ mol/L} = 0.5 \text{ M}$$

### #2: Calculating Molality

A solution is made by dissolving $1$ mole of sugar into $2$ kilograms of water. Find the molality.

**Solution:**
Apply the molality formula, using the mass of the solvent only.

1. Divide moles of solute by kilograms of solvent.

$$m = \dfrac{1 \text{ mol}}{2 \text{ kg}}$$

2. Compute.

$$m = 0.5 \text{ mol/kg} = 0.5 \text{ m}$$

### #3: Calculating Percent by Mass

A solution is made by dissolving $20$ grams of salt into $180$ grams of water. Find the percent by mass of the salt.

**Solution:**
Find the total solution mass first, then apply the percent-by-mass formula.

1. Total solution mass = mass of solute + mass of solvent.

$$20 + 180 = 200 \text{ g}$$

2. Apply the formula.

$$\text{percent by mass} = \dfrac{20}{200} \times 100\%$$

3. Compute.

$$\dfrac{20}{200} = 0.10 \quad \Rightarrow \quad 10\%$$

### #4: Molarity vs. Molality — Why the Difference Matters

Explain why molality, unlike molarity, does not change when a solution's temperature changes.

**Solution:**
Compare what each formula's denominator actually measures.

1. Molarity's denominator is the volume of the solution (in liters). Volume can expand when heated or contract when cooled, due to normal thermal expansion of liquids.
2. Molality's denominator is the mass of the solvent (in kilograms). Mass does not change with temperature — heating or cooling a substance does not add or remove any matter.
3. Because molarity depends on a value (volume) that shifts with temperature, a solution's molarity can change slightly as its temperature changes, even though the amount of dissolved solute stays the same. Molality stays constant regardless of temperature, which makes it the preferred measurement for experiments involving significant temperature changes.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Remember the units to instantly identify which formula a question wants: "moles per liter of solution" is molarity; "moles per kilogram of solvent" is molality; "grams per $100$ grams" or plain percentages of mass are percent by mass; percentages of volume (common for liquid mixtures like alcohol content) are percent by volume.

For percent-by-mass problems, always calculate the total solution mass (solute plus solvent) before dividing — a common shortcut mistake is dividing by the solvent's mass alone.

If a problem specifically mentions temperature changes or requires precise, temperature-independent measurements, it is testing molality, not molarity — this is molality's signature real-world use case.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Confusing Molarity's and Molality's Denominators

> Molarity divides by liters of solution. Molality divides by kilograms of solvent only.

These sound almost identical and are the most commonly swapped concentration terms. A quick check: "molARITY" pairs with "voluме" (both contain similar letter patterns you can associate together), while "molALITY" pairs with "mass" of solvent alone.

### #2: Using the Solvent's Mass Instead of the Total Solution's Mass for Percent by Mass

> Percent by mass divides by the total solution mass (solute + solvent), not the solvent's mass alone.

In Example 3, dividing $20$ grams of salt by just $180$ grams of water (instead of the full $200$ grams of solution) gives an inflated, incorrect percentage. Always add solute and solvent together first.

### #3: Assuming Solubility Is the Same at Every Temperature

> Solubility generally changes with temperature — usually increasing for solids as the solvent gets hotter.

A question describing more sugar dissolving as tea is heated is demonstrating this exact temperature dependence. Treating solubility as one single fixed number, without considering temperature, misses this important variable.

### #4: Treating "Saturated" as Meaning "Very Concentrated" Universally

> "Saturated" specifically means the solution holds the maximum solute it can dissolve at that temperature — it is not just a vague description of "a lot" of solute.

A solution can be saturated with only a small amount of solute if that solute has low solubility, or it can require a very large amount of solute to become saturated if that solute has high solubility. Saturation is always relative to the specific solute's maximum solubility at the current temperature, not an absolute amount.
