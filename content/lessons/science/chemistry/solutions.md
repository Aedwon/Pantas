## Core Concept

A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture at the molecular or ionic scale. The component that determines the solution's physical state and is often present in the greatest amount is called the **solvent**; the other component or components are **solutes**. "Solute = smaller amount" is a useful simple-case convention, not the definition in every mixture.

### Solubility

**Solubility** is the maximum concentration of a solute that can be present in a stable solution under specified conditions. Temperature matters, and pressure can matter strongly for gases.

- A **saturated** solution is in equilibrium with undissolved solute at the solubility limit under the stated conditions.
- An **unsaturated** solution contains less dissolved solute than that limit.
- A **supersaturated** solution contains more dissolved solute than the ordinary equilibrium solubility and is metastable.

The solubility of **many** solids in water increases as temperature rises, but this is not universal. Gas solubility in liquids commonly decreases as temperature rises and increases with the gas's partial pressure above the solution.

### Concentration Measures

**Molarity** ($M$) is amount of solute per volume of **solution**:

$$M=\frac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{liters of solution}}$$

**Molality** ($m$) is amount of solute per mass of **solvent**:

$$m=\frac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{kilograms of solvent}}$$

If no material is lost or gained, masses do not change merely because temperature changes, so molality is temperature-independent. Molarity can change with temperature because solution volume can change.

**Percent by mass**:

$$\%\text{ by mass}=\frac{\text{mass of solute}}{\text{mass of solution}}\times100\%$$

**Percent by volume** for appropriate liquid mixtures:

$$\%\text{ by volume}=\frac{\text{volume of solute}}{\text{final volume of solution}}\times100\%$$

Use the **final solution volume**, not an assumption that component volumes simply add; mixing liquids can involve volume contraction or expansion.

## Examples

### #1: Molarity

A solution contains $2$ mol $NaCl$ in a **final solution volume** of $4$ L.

$$M=\frac{2}{4}=0.50\,\mathrm{mol/L}=0.50\,M$$

### #2: Molality

A solution contains $1$ mol solute per $2$ kg of solvent.

$$m=\frac{1}{2}=0.50\,\mathrm{mol/kg}$$

### #3: Percent by Mass

Dissolve $20$ g salt in $180$ g water, with no material lost.

1. Solution mass: $20+180=200$ g.
2. Therefore:

$$\%\text{ by mass}=\frac{20}{200}\times100\%=10\%$$

### #4: Why Molarity Can Change with Temperature

A sealed solution is warmed without losing solute or solvent. Why can its molarity change while molality stays constant?

**Solution:**

1. The amounts and masses of solute and solvent remain fixed.
2. Molality uses kilograms of solvent, so its denominator is unchanged.
3. Molarity uses liters of solution. Thermal expansion or contraction can change that volume.
4. Therefore molarity can change with temperature even when composition is unchanged.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Read the denominator: **liters of solution = molarity; kilograms of solvent = molality**.

For mass percent, divide by total solution mass. For volume percent, divide by measured final solution volume.

For solubility questions, never discuss temperature without first asking whether the solute is a solid, liquid, or gas and what solvent/pressure conditions are specified.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Saying Solid Solubility Always Increases with Temperature

> Many solids become more soluble as temperature rises, but some change little and some become less soluble.

Solubility behavior is substance-specific.

### #2: Ignoring Pressure for Dissolved Gases

> Gas solubility generally increases with gas partial pressure above a liquid and commonly decreases as temperature rises.

This is why pressure is important in carbonated beverages.

### #3: Confusing Molarity and Molality

> Molarity uses **solution volume**; molality uses **solvent mass**.

The solvent alone is not the denominator of molarity.

### #4: Adding Liquid Volumes Blindly

> Final solution volume is measured or specified; component volumes are not guaranteed to be exactly additive.

Use the final volume when calculating volume percent or molarity.
