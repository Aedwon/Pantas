## Core Concept

Compare spots with known substances run on the same paper. A spot at the same height as a known substance may match it. A mixture that gives several spots contains several components.

A pure substance usually gives one spot in a given solvent. But two different substances can sometimes give the same spot in one solvent. So another solvent is a better test.

For a spot, the **retention factor** compares the distance it moved with the distance the solvent moved. Both distances are measured from the baseline, and the spot is measured at its center.

$$R_f = \dfrac{\text{distance moved by the spot}}{\text{distance moved by the solvent front}}$$

The value is between $0$ and $1$. It is the same for the same substance, solvent, paper, and conditions. It changes when the solvent or conditions change.

## Examples

### #1: Reading a Chromatogram

A black ink is run on paper. The solvent front moves $6.0$ cm from the baseline. The ink gives three spots. One spot, A, is $3.0$ cm from the baseline. A known blue dye run on the same paper gives a spot at $3.0$ cm. What do the results show, and what is the $R_f$ of A?

**Solution:**

1. Three spots mean the ink has at least three components.
2. Spot A is at the same height as the blue dye, so A may be that dye. A second solvent would test this.
3. Use the $R_f$ formula for spot A.

$$R_f = \dfrac{3.0}{6.0} = 0.50$$

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For a chromatogram, count spots to count components. The visible spots show the least number of components. Two components can overlap in one solvent, and some spots may be colorless.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Chromatography Mistakes

> A spot that travels far is not "stronger." It is held less by the paper and dissolves better in the solvent.

In simple paper chromatography, a far-traveling spot is held weakly by the paper. Measure both distances from the baseline, not from the paper edge. A baseline drawn in ink or set under the solvent spoils the result.
