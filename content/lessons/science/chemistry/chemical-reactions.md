## Core Concept

A **chemical equation** shows the substances that start a reaction (**reactants**, on the left) turning into new substances (**products**, on the right), separated by an arrow.

### The Law of Conservation of Mass

Matter cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction. This means a chemical equation must be **balanced**: the same number of atoms of each element must appear on both sides of the arrow.

You balance an equation by adjusting **coefficients** (the big numbers placed in front of a formula, showing how many molecules or units are involved). You may never change a **subscript** (the small number within a formula, like the $2$ in $H_2O$), because that would change the substance's identity into something completely different.

### Five Types of Reactions

- **Synthesis (combination)**: two or more simple substances combine into one product. $A + B \rightarrow AB$.
- **Decomposition**: one compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances. $AB \rightarrow A + B$.
- **Single replacement**: one element swaps places with another element in a compound. $A + BC \rightarrow AC + B$.
- **Double replacement**: two compounds swap partners, exchanging their positive and negative parts. $AB + CD \rightarrow AD + CB$.
- **Combustion**: a substance (usually containing carbon and hydrogen) reacts with oxygen, releasing energy, usually producing carbon dioxide and water.

## Examples

### #1: Identifying the Reaction Type

Classify each reaction: (a) $2H_2 + O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O$; (b) $2H_2O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O + O_2$; (c) $Zn + 2HCl \rightarrow ZnCl_2 + H_2$.

**Solution:**
Check the pattern of reactants combining or breaking apart.

1. (a) Two simple substances ($H_2$ and $O_2$) combine into one product ($H_2O$): Synthesis.
2. (b) One compound ($H_2O_2$) breaks down into two simpler substances: Decomposition.
3. (c) Zinc, a single element, swaps places with hydrogen in the compound: Single replacement.

### #2: Balancing a Simple Equation

Balance the equation: $\_\_ N_2 + \_\_ H_2 \rightarrow \_\_ NH_3$.

**Solution:**
Count atoms on each side and adjust coefficients until they match.

1. Start with $1$ each: $N_2 + H_2 \rightarrow NH_3$. Left side: $2$ nitrogen, $2$ hydrogen. Right side: $1$ nitrogen, $3$ hydrogen. Not balanced.
2. Place a coefficient of $2$ in front of $NH_3$ to balance nitrogen: $N_2 + H_2 \rightarrow 2NH_3$. Now nitrogen is $2$ on both sides. Hydrogen: left is still $2$, right is now $2 \times 3 = 6$.
3. Place a coefficient of $3$ in front of $H_2$ to balance hydrogen: $N_2 + 3H_2 \rightarrow 2NH_3$. Now hydrogen is $3 \times 2 = 6$ on the left and $6$ on the right.
4. Final check: nitrogen $2 = 2$; hydrogen $6 = 6$. Balanced equation: $N_2 + 3H_2 \rightarrow 2NH_3$.

### #3: Balancing a Combustion Reaction

Balance the combustion of methane: $\_\_ CH_4 + \_\_ O_2 \rightarrow \_\_ CO_2 + \_\_ H_2O$.

**Solution:**
Balance carbon first, then hydrogen, then oxygen last (oxygen usually needs the most adjusting).

1. Carbon: $1$ on the left ($CH_4$), $1$ on the right ($CO_2$). Already balanced.
2. Hydrogen: $4$ on the left ($CH_4$), only $2$ on the right ($H_2O$). Place a coefficient of $2$ in front of $H_2O$: $CH_4 + O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O$. Now hydrogen is $4$ on both sides.
3. Oxygen: on the right, $CO_2$ contributes $2$ and $2H_2O$ contributes $2 \times 1 = 2$, totaling $4$ oxygen atoms. On the left, oxygen comes only from $O_2$, so place a coefficient of $2$ in front of it: $CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O$.
4. Final check: carbon $1 = 1$; hydrogen $4 = 4$; oxygen $2 \times 2 = 4$ on the left, $2 + 2 = 4$ on the right. Balanced.

### #4: Recognizing an Unbalanced Equation

Is the equation $Fe + O_2 \rightarrow Fe_2O_3$ balanced? If not, fix it.

**Solution:**
Count atoms of each element on both sides.

1. Left side: $1$ iron, $2$ oxygen. Right side: $2$ iron, $3$ oxygen. Not balanced.
2. Multiply the whole equation to find a common oxygen count: the least common multiple of $2$ and $3$ is $6$, so aim for $6$ oxygen atoms on both sides.
3. Place a $4$ in front of $Fe$ and a $3$ in front of $O_2$ on the left (giving $4$ iron and $6$ oxygen), and a $2$ in front of $Fe_2O_3$ on the right (giving $2 \times 2 = 4$ iron and $2 \times 3 = 6$ oxygen): $4Fe + 3O_2 \rightarrow 2Fe_2O_3$.
4. Final check: iron $4 = 4$; oxygen $6 = 6$. Balanced.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Balance elements in this order for the smoothest process: balance any element that appears in only one reactant and one product first, save elements that appear alone (like $O_2$) for last, since their coefficient is easiest to adjust at the very end.

For combustion reactions specifically, always balance in this order: carbon, then hydrogen, then oxygen last: oxygen's coefficient usually needs to accommodate both $CO_2$ and $H_2O$, so it should be handled after everything else is fixed.

To quickly identify a reaction type, count how many separate substances start on the left and end on the right: one reactant → many products (decomposition); many reactants → one product (synthesis); anything involving $O_2$ as a reactant with a carbon-hydrogen compound (combustion).

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Changing a Subscript Instead of a Coefficient

> Only coefficients (numbers in front of a formula) may be changed. Subscripts (numbers within a formula) must stay fixed.

Changing $H_2O$ to $H_2O_2$ to "balance" an equation actually creates hydrogen peroxide, a completely different substance, not balanced water. Only adjust the whole-formula coefficient, never the internal subscript.

### #2: Stopping Before Every Element Matches

> An equation is balanced only when every element's atom count matches on both sides, not just one or two elements.

A student may balance nitrogen correctly but forget to re-check hydrogen after adjusting a coefficient. Always do a final full recount of every element before declaring an equation balanced.

### #3: Confusing Synthesis and Decomposition

> Synthesis combines multiple substances into one. Decomposition breaks one substance into multiple.

These are mirror-image reactions, and it is easy to swap them when reading quickly. Check the arrow direction: many things merging into one on the right is synthesis; one thing splitting into many on the right is decomposition.

### #4: Misidentifying Single vs. Double Replacement

> Single replacement involves one free element swapping into a compound. Double replacement involves two compounds trading their parts with each other.

If only one reactant is a lone element (like $Zn$ or $Cl_2$) and the other is a compound, it is single replacement. If both reactants are already compounds (with no lone elements), and they simply trade partners, it is double replacement.
