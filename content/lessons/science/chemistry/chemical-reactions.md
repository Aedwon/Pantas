## Core Concept

A **chemical equation** represents reactants converting into products. Chemical equations must satisfy conservation of each element's atoms and, for ionic equations, conservation of net charge.

### Balancing Equations

Balance equations by changing **stoichiometric coefficients** in front of formulas. Do not change a compound's subscripts merely to make atom counts match, because changing a subscript changes the chemical formula and usually the substance being represented.

A conventionally balanced equation is normally written using the **smallest whole-number coefficient ratio**. Multiplying every coefficient by the same number gives an equivalent stoichiometric ratio but not the simplest conventional form.

### Common Introductory Reaction Patterns

Many introductory courses teach the following pattern labels. They are useful, but they are **not an exhaustive or mutually exclusive classification of chemical reactions**. The same reaction can also be classified by a different chemical idea such as oxidation-reduction, acid-base chemistry, or precipitation.

- **Combination (synthesis)**: multiple reactants form one product in the simple pattern $A+B\rightarrow AB$.
- **Decomposition**: one reactant forms multiple products in the simple pattern $AB\rightarrow A+B$.
- **Single-displacement (single-replacement)**: an element displaces another from a compound, such as $A+BC\rightarrow AC+B$ when the reaction is chemically favorable.
- **Double-displacement (metathesis)**: ions in solution exchange partners. A reaction proceeds when there is a driving force such as formation of a precipitate, weak electrolyte/water, or gas; merely swapping symbols does not prove a reaction occurs.
- **Combustion**: rapid reaction with an oxidant, commonly $O_2$, that releases energy. In introductory hydrocarbon/oxygen problems, **complete combustion** produces $CO_2$ and $H_2O$. Incomplete combustion can also form $CO$, elemental carbon/soot, and other products.

| Introductory pattern | Typical form |
| --- | --- |
| Combination | $A+B\rightarrow AB$ |
| Decomposition | $AB\rightarrow A+B$ |
| Single displacement | $A+BC\rightarrow AC+B$ |
| Double displacement | $AB+CD\rightarrow AD+CB$ when a reaction is driven |
| Complete hydrocarbon combustion | $C_xH_y+O_2\rightarrow CO_2+H_2O$ |

## Examples

### #1: Recognizing Useful Pattern Labels

Classify these using the simple pattern scheme:

(a) $2H_2+O_2\rightarrow2H_2O$  
(b) $2H_2O_2\rightarrow2H_2O+O_2$  
(c) $Zn+2HCl\rightarrow ZnCl_2+H_2$

**Solution:**

1. (a) Multiple reactants form one product: combination. It is also an oxidation-reduction reaction.
2. (b) One reactant forms multiple products: decomposition. It also involves redox chemistry.
3. (c) Zn displaces hydrogen: single displacement and redox.

The overlap illustrates why the five-pattern scheme is a convenient classroom description rather than a complete one-label taxonomy.

### #2: Balancing Ammonia Formation

Balance:

$$N_2+H_2\rightarrow NH_3$$

1. Put $2$ before $NH_3$ to balance N.
2. That gives $6$ H atoms on the product side, so put $3$ before $H_2$.

$$N_2+3H_2\rightarrow2NH_3$$

The coefficients $1:3:2$ are already the smallest whole-number ratio.

### #3: Complete Combustion of Methane

Balance the **complete** combustion equation:

$$CH_4+O_2\rightarrow CO_2+H_2O$$

1. C is already balanced.
2. Put $2$ before $H_2O$ to balance H.
3. Products now contain four O atoms, so put $2$ before $O_2$.

$$CH_4+2O_2\rightarrow CO_2+2H_2O$$

If oxygen supply is insufficient, this complete-combustion product set is not guaranteed.

### #4: Balancing Iron(III) Oxide Formation

$$Fe+O_2\rightarrow Fe_2O_3$$

Using six oxygen atoms as the common count gives:

$$4Fe+3O_2\rightarrow2Fe_2O_3$$

Check: $4$ Fe atoms and $6$ O atoms appear on each side.

### #5: Does a Double-Displacement Pattern Guarantee a Reaction?

Suppose two soluble ionic compounds are mixed and the written ion-swap products are also all soluble strong electrolytes. Is simply writing $AB+CD\rightarrow AD+CB$ enough to prove a reaction occurred?

**Solution:**

No. If all ions remain unchanged in solution, the complete ionic equation can reduce to spectator ions on both sides, giving no net ionic reaction. A double-displacement reaction needs an appropriate driving force such as precipitation, gas formation, or formation of a weakly ionized product.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Balance by atom counts, usually leaving elements that appear in several compounds until later. For simple hydrocarbon complete-combustion equations, balancing C, then H, then O is efficient.

Use the five pattern labels only when the question is using that classification scheme. If a question asks about **redox**, **acid-base**, or **precipitation**, use the chemistry defining that category instead.

For ionic metathesis, check whether a precipitate, gas, or weak electrolyte forms; do not assume every symbolic partner swap is a real reaction.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Changing Subscripts to Balance

> Balance by coefficients, not by altering chemical formulas.

Changing $H_2O$ to $H_2O_2$ changes water into hydrogen peroxide.

### #2: Calling the Five Patterns the Only Reaction Types

> Reaction classifications overlap and other important schemes include acid-base, precipitation, and oxidation-reduction reactions.

A reaction can receive more than one valid label depending on the question being asked.

### #3: Saying All Combustion Produces Only $CO_2$ and $H_2O$

> That product pattern describes complete combustion of suitable carbon/hydrogen fuels in sufficient oxygen.

Incomplete combustion and combustion of compounds containing other elements can produce additional products.

### #4: Assuming Every Double Replacement Proceeds

> Exchanging ion labels is not itself evidence of a chemical change.

Look for a thermodynamic/chemical driving force and, in aqueous problems, check the net ionic equation.
