## Core Concept

### Mendel's Laws and Punnett Squares

The **Law of Segregation** says the two alleles of a gene separate when **gametes** (egg and sperm) form. Each gamete gets one allele.

A **Punnett square** shows the possible gametes of each parent along its sides. Each cell inside shows one offspring genotype.

In this model, each parent passes on either allele with an equal chance. The parents pass on their alleles independently, so all pairs in the square are equally likely. Then the chance of a trait is the number of cells with it divided by the total number of cells.

## Examples

### #1: Basic Punnett Square Cross

Two heterozygous pea plants are crossed: $Tt \times Tt$. Allele $T$ is dominant and gives tall plants. Allele $t$ is recessive and gives short plants. What is the probability that an offspring is tall?

**Solution:**

1. Each parent makes gametes $T$ and $t$.
2. The four cells are $TT$, $Tt$, $Tt$, and $tt$.
3. Three of the four cells contain $T$, so they are tall.

$$P(\text{tall})=\dfrac{3}{4}$$

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Under this model, a cross of two heterozygous parents ($Tt \times Tt$) has an expected genotype ratio of $1:2:1$. The expected phenotype ratio is $3:1$ under complete dominance and $1:2:1$ under incomplete dominance or codominance.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Reading a Punnett Ratio as a Promise

> A Punnett square gives chances, not exact counts.

Four children of two heterozygous parents may not include exactly three tall ones. Each child has a $\dfrac{3}{4}$ chance.
