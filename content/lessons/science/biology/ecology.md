## Core Concept

**Ecology** is the study of how living things interact with one another and with the nonliving parts of their environment.

### Ecosystems and Biomes

An **ecosystem** includes living things and the nonliving environment around them. Matter and energy move between these parts.

A **biome** is a large region with a usual climate and common kinds of plants. It contains many ecosystems and the animals, fungi, and tiny living things found there.

### Food Chains, Food Webs, and Trophic Levels

A **food chain** is a simple path that shows who eats whom. An arrow points from the organism being eaten to the organism that gets energy from it.

A **food web** joins many food chains in one ecosystem. Some consumers eat at more than one trophic level. A change in one population can therefore affect several other populations.

A **trophic level** is a feeding position in a food chain or food web:

- **Primary producers** make food using light or chemical energy.
- **Primary consumers** eat producers.
- **Secondary and higher consumers** eat other consumers.
- **Decomposers and detritivores** break down dead material and waste, helping matter return to the environment.

Energy does not pass fully from one trophic level to the next. Organisms use energy to live, move, grow, and keep their bodies working. Some material is not eaten, and some energy leaves as heat or waste.

The familiar **$10\%$ rule** is a classroom estimate, not a rule that every ecosystem follows exactly. If a problem tells you to use $10\%$, multiply by $0.10$ for each trophic step.

A **kilojoule (kJ)** is a unit of energy.

### Populations and Communities

A **population** is a group of the same species living in the same area. A **community** includes the different populations living and interacting in one area.

Population size can change when organisms are born, die, enter an area, or leave it. Food, water, space, disease, predators, and other conditions can also limit growth.

**Carrying capacity** is the largest population an environment can support for a long time with its available resources. It can change when resources or conditions change.

Species in a community may compete for the same resources. Predators, prey, parasites, and helpful partners can also change one another's population sizes.

### Biogeochemical Cycles

Matter is reused in ecosystems. A **reservoir** is a place where matter can stay for some time, such as the air, water, soil, rocks, or living things.

The **carbon cycle** moves carbon among air, oceans, organisms, soil, rocks, and long-term stores such as fossil carbon. Photosynthesis takes in $CO_2$. Respiration and decomposition return carbon to the environment.

The **nitrogen cycle** moves nitrogen through air, soil, water, and living things. **Nitrogen fixation** changes atmospheric $N_2$ into ammonia, which is often present as ammonium $NH_4^+$ in water or soil. Some bacteria then carry out **nitrification**, which changes ammonium into nitrite and nitrate. Plants take up usable nitrogen compounds, while **denitrification** returns nitrogen gases to the air.

The **phosphorus cycle** moves phosphorus mainly through rocks, soil, water, and living things. Weathering releases phosphate from rocks. Plants take it up from soil or water, animals get it by feeding, and waste and decay return it. Runoff can carry phosphate into bodies of water, where some may later become part of sediment.

Unlike the carbon and nitrogen cycles, the phosphorus cycle has no major gas reservoir in the atmosphere.

The **water cycle** moves water through air, land, bodies of water, groundwater, and living things. **Evaporation** changes liquid water into water vapor. **Condensation** changes water vapor into tiny liquid drops, which can form clouds. **Precipitation** returns water to the surface as rain or other forms of falling water.

Water can then flow over land as **runoff**, soak into the ground, or move through plants. Plants can release water vapor from their leaves through **transpiration**.

### Symbiotic Relationships

**Symbiosis** is a close relationship between different species. Simple ecology questions often group these relationships by their effects:

- **Mutualism**: both partners benefit $(+,+)$.
- **Commensalism**: one benefits while the other has no clear net effect $(+,0)$.
- **Parasitism**: the parasite benefits while the host is harmed $(+,-)$.

These labels depend on the evidence in the question. A relationship that looks neutral at first may show a cost or benefit when studied more closely.

### Biodiversity, Human Impacts, and Conservation

**Biodiversity** means the variety of living things in an area. It includes different species and the variety found within ecosystems.

Losing a species can change feeding relationships, competition, and other links in a community. The effect depends on the role of that species and on the rest of the food web.

Human activities can change ecosystems. Common causes include habitat loss, pollution, taking organisms faster than their populations can recover, and changes in climate. An **invasive species** is an introduced species that spreads and causes harm in its new area.

**Conservation** protects species, habitats, and natural resources so they can keep supporting life. Actions may include protecting or restoring habitat, reducing pollution, controlling invasive species, limiting harvest so populations can replace what is taken, and monitoring populations.

Environmental management should match the problem. Protecting habitat will not by itself remove a pollutant, and removing a pollutant will not by itself restore a lost habitat.

## Examples

### #1: Building a Food Chain

Arrange grass, grasshopper, frog, and hawk into a simple food chain and identify their trophic roles in that chain.

**Solution:**

1. Grass is the producer.
2. Grasshopper eats the producer, so it is a primary consumer.
3. Frog eats the grasshopper, so it is a secondary consumer.
4. Hawk eats the frog, so it is a tertiary consumer in this simple chain.
5. The energy path is grass → grasshopper → frog → hawk.

### #2: Reading a Branching Food Web

In a food web, grass is eaten by rabbits and grasshoppers. Frogs eat grasshoppers. Hawks eat both rabbits and frogs. If the grasshopper population drops sharply, what direct effect is most likely on frogs, and why might hawks be affected less strongly?

**Solution:**

1. Frogs have less food because they eat grasshoppers. Their population may decrease if they cannot replace that food source.
2. Hawks may also lose some food if there are fewer frogs.
3. Hawks can still eat rabbits in this web, so they have another food path. The change may therefore affect hawks less directly than frogs.

### #3: Applying a Given $10\%$ Approximation

A problem tells you to assume $10\%$ trophic transfer efficiency. Producers contain $10{,}000$ kJ of usable energy. Under that stated assumption, estimate the energy transferred to primary and then secondary consumers.

**Solution:**

1. Find the energy for primary consumers:

$$10{,}000(0.10)=1{,}000\text{ kJ}$$

2. Find the energy for secondary consumers:

$$1{,}000(0.10)=100\text{ kJ}$$

3. This is an estimate based on the problem's $10\%$ assumption. It is not a law that every ecosystem follows exactly.

### #4: Identifying Symbiosis

"Classify" means put each example into the correct group.

Classify these simplified relationships: (a) a tapeworm gets nutrients from a dog and harms it; (b) a pollinator gets food while helping pollinate a plant; (c) an organism gains shelter while measurements show no clear effect on its host.

**Solution:**

1. (a) Parasitism: $(+,-)$.
2. (b) Mutualism: $(+,+)$.
3. (c) Commensalism under the stated evidence: $(+,0)$.

### #5: Tracing Carbon and Water

A plant takes in $CO_2$ during photosynthesis. Later, decomposers break down dead plant material. In the same ecosystem, lake water becomes water vapor, forms clouds, and later falls as rain. Name the main cycle processes described.

**Solution:**

1. Photosynthesis moves carbon from $CO_2$ into plant material.
2. Decomposition returns carbon from dead material to the environment.
3. Evaporation changes lake water into water vapor.
4. Condensation helps water vapor form cloud droplets.
5. Precipitation returns water from the atmosphere to the surface.

### #6: Why Nitrogen Fixation Matters

Why can most plants not use atmospheric $N_2$ directly, and what process first changes it into a more usable form?

**Solution:**

1. Most plants cannot take $N_2$ from the air and use it directly.
2. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria change $N_2$ into ammonia.
3. Other bacteria can later change ammonium into nitrite and nitrate through nitrification.
4. Keep the processes separate: **fixation produces ammonia; nitrification produces nitrite and nitrate**.

### #7: Reading Population Change

A deer population grows after several wet seasons increase plant food. Later, a drought reduces food and water. What ecological idea best explains why the population may stop growing or decrease?

**Solution:**

1. The drought reduces important resources.
2. With fewer resources, the environment can support fewer deer.
3. The carrying capacity has fallen, so the population may stop growing or decrease.

### #8: Choosing an Environmental Response

A wetland has lost native plants because nearby land was cleared, and polluted runoff now enters the area. Which response best addresses both problems?

**Solution:**

1. Restore native wetland habitat to replace lost shelter and food.
2. Reduce or stop the polluted runoff entering the wetland.
3. Monitor the community to see whether native populations recover.
4. One action is not enough because habitat loss and pollution are different problems.

## Exam Strategies

For food-web arrows, read each arrow as **energy moves to** the consumer.

For trophic-energy calculations, use $10\%$ only when the problem gives that approximation or tells you to assume it.

For the nitrogen cycle, keep the verbs separate: **fix $N_2$ → ammonia; nitrify ammonium → nitrite/nitrate; denitrify nitrate → nitrogen gases**.

For population questions, first look for a change in food, water, space, predators, disease, or movement into or out of the area.

For environmental problems, match the response to the cause. Habitat loss needs habitat protection or restoration, while pollution needs the pollutant reduced or removed.

For symbiosis, $(+,+)$ = mutualism, $(+,0)$ = commensalism, and $(+,-)$ = parasitism.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Treating the $10\%$ Rule as Exact

> Trophic transfer efficiency varies; $10\%$ is a useful estimate for simple problems.

Real transfer can be higher or lower.

### #2: Calling Nitrate a Direct Product of Nitrogen Fixation

> Nitrogen fixation changes $N_2$ into ammonia. Nitrification carries out the later steps toward nitrite and nitrate.

Do not combine different nitrogen-cycle processes into one step.

### #3: Forcing Every Organism Into One Fixed Trophic Level

> Omnivores can feed at different trophic levels, and decomposers connect many parts of a food web.

A simple producer-consumer ladder is useful, but a real food web has many links.

### #4: Assuming Symbiosis Categories Are Always Obvious

> Commensalism means one partner benefits while the other has no clear net effect.

If evidence shows both partners benefit, the relationship is mutualism. If one is harmed, it is parasitism.

### #5: Assuming One Population Changes by Itself

> Populations are linked to resources and to other populations in a community.

A rise or fall in one species can change food, competition, or predation for another species.

### #6: Using One Conservation Action for Every Problem

> Match the action to the cause of the damage.

Habitat loss, pollution, invasive species, and overharvesting need different responses, and some problems need more than one action.
