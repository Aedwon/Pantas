## Core Concept

**Ecology** is the study of how living things interact with one another and with the nonliving parts of their environment.

### Ecosystems and Biomes

An **ecosystem** includes living things and the nonliving environment around them. Matter and energy move between these parts.

A **biome** is a large region with a usual climate and common kinds of plants. It contains many ecosystems, including their animals, fungi, and tiny living things.

### Food Chains, Food Webs, and Trophic Levels

A **food chain** is a simple path that shows who eats whom. An arrow points from the organism being eaten to the organism that gets energy from it.

A **food web** joins many food chains in one ecosystem. Some consumers eat at more than one trophic level. A change in one population can therefore affect several other populations.

A **trophic level** is a feeding position in a food chain or food web:

- **Primary producers** make food using light or chemical energy.
- **Primary consumers** eat producers.
- **Secondary and higher consumers** eat other consumers.
- **Decomposers** break down dead material and waste. **Detritivores** eat dead material and waste, breaking it into smaller pieces.

Both decomposers and detritivores help return matter to the environment.

Energy does not pass fully from one trophic level to the next. Organisms use energy to live, move, and grow, and much of that energy leaves as heat. Some material is not eaten or leaves as waste.

The familiar **$10\%$ rule** is a classroom estimate, not a rule that every ecosystem follows exactly. If a problem tells you to use $10\%$, multiply by $0.10$ for each trophic step.

A **kilojoule (kJ)** is a unit of energy. Energy flows through an ecosystem and eventually leaves as heat, while matter can be reused.

### Populations and Communities

A **population** is a group of the same species living in the same area. A **community** includes the different populations living and interacting in one area.

Population size changes through births, deaths, movement into an area, and movement out. Food, water, space, disease, predators, and other conditions can limit growth.

**Carrying capacity** is the population an environment can support over time with its available resources. It can change when resources or conditions change. A population can briefly grow beyond this level, but a shortage of resources can then reduce it.

**Competition** happens when organisms need the same limited resource, such as food or space. Predators eat prey, so changes in either population may affect the other. These effects depend on other food sources and conditions too.

### Biogeochemical Cycles

Matter is reused in ecosystems through **biogeochemical cycles**. These are paths that matter follows through living things and the nonliving environment. A **reservoir** is a place where matter stays for some time, such as air, water, soil, or rocks.

The **carbon cycle** moves carbon among air, oceans, organisms, soil, and rocks. Fossil fuels also store carbon over long periods. Carbon dioxide, written as $CO_2$, is a gas that contains carbon.

**Photosynthesis** uses light energy to make food from carbon dioxide and water. It moves carbon into plant material. Feeding then moves that carbon to animals.

**Cellular respiration** releases energy from food and returns carbon dioxide to the environment. **Decomposition**, the breakdown of dead material and waste, also returns carbon to the environment. Burning fossil fuels releases stored carbon as carbon dioxide.

The **nitrogen cycle** moves nitrogen through air, soil, water, and living things. Nitrogen gas is written as $N_2$. Most plants cannot use this gas directly because they lack a way to break it apart and change it into usable compounds.

**Nitrogen fixation** changes nitrogen gas into ammonia. Certain bacteria carry out this process. In soil or water, ammonia can become **ammonium**, written as $NH_4^+$.

**Nitrification** is the process in which bacteria change ammonium into nitrite and then nitrate. Plants take up usable nitrogen compounds through their roots. Animals get nitrogen by eating plants or other animals.

Decay returns nitrogen from dead material and waste to the soil. **Denitrification** changes nitrate into nitrogen gases that return to the air.

The **phosphorus cycle** moves phosphorus mainly through rocks, soil, water, and living things. **Weathering**, the breakdown of rocks, releases phosphate, a form of phosphorus that plants can take up.

Plants absorb phosphate from soil or water, and animals get it by feeding. Waste and decay return phosphorus to the environment. Water flowing over land can carry phosphate into rivers and lakes.

Some phosphorus settles into **sediment**, material that collects at the bottom of water. Over long periods, sediment can become rock. Unlike the carbon and nitrogen cycles, the phosphorus cycle has no major gas reservoir in the atmosphere.

The **water cycle** moves water through air, land, bodies of water, and living things. **Evaporation** changes liquid water into water vapor, the gas form of water.

**Condensation** changes water vapor into tiny liquid drops, which can form clouds. **Precipitation** returns water to the surface as rain or other forms of falling water.

Water can flow over land as **runoff** or soak into the ground. Some underground water and runoff flow back into rivers, lakes, and oceans. Plants absorb water and release some as water vapor from their leaves through **transpiration**.

### Symbiotic Relationships

**Symbiosis** is a close relationship between different species. Simple ecology questions group these relationships by their effects. A plus sign ($+$) means a benefit, a minus sign ($-$) means harm, and zero ($0$) means no clear net effect.

- **Mutualism**: both partners benefit $(+,+)$.
- **Commensalism**: one benefits while the other has no clear net effect $(+,0)$.
- **Parasitism**: the parasite benefits while the host is harmed $(+,-)$.

These labels depend on the evidence in the question. A relationship that seems neutral at first may show a cost or benefit when studied more closely.

### Biodiversity, Human Impacts, and Conservation

**Biodiversity** means the variety of life. It includes differences within a species, the range of species, and the range of ecosystems.

Losing a species can change feeding relationships, competition, and other links in a community. Losing a pollinator, for example, may reduce seed production in plants that depend on it. The effect depends on the species and the other links in the ecosystem.

Human activities can change ecosystems through habitat loss, pollution, and climate change. Taking organisms faster than their populations can replace them is **overharvesting**. An **invasive species** is an introduced species that spreads and causes harm in its new area.

**Conservation** protects species, habitats, and natural resources. It includes protecting or restoring habitats, reducing pollution, controlling invasive species, and limiting harvest. Checking populations over time helps show whether these actions are working.

Environmental management should match the problem. Habitat protection does not by itself remove a pollutant. Pollution control does not by itself replace a lost habitat.

## Examples

### #1: Building a Food Chain

"Arrange" means put in order. "Identify" means name the requested item or role.

Arrange grass, grasshopper, frog, and hawk into a simple food chain and identify their trophic roles in that chain.

**Solution:**

1. Grass is the producer.
2. Grasshopper eats the producer, so it is a primary consumer.
3. Frog eats the grasshopper, so it is a secondary consumer.
4. Hawk eats the frog, so it is a tertiary consumer in this chain.
5. The energy path is grass → grasshopper → frog → hawk.

### #2: Reading a Branching Food Web

In a food web, grass is eaten by rabbits and grasshoppers. Frogs eat grasshoppers. Hawks eat both rabbits and frogs. If the grasshopper population drops sharply, what effect is likely on frogs, and why might hawks be affected less strongly?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: Branching food web with arrows grass to rabbits, grass to grasshoppers, grasshoppers to frogs, rabbits to hawks, and frogs to hawks; label arrows "energy moves to" and place a downward arrow beside grasshoppers. -->

**Solution:**

1. Frogs have less food because they eat grasshoppers. Their population may decrease if they cannot replace that food source.
2. Hawks may also lose some food if there are fewer frogs.
3. Hawks can still eat rabbits in this web. This other food source may reduce the effect on them, but it does not guarantee their population will stay the same.

### #3: Applying a Given $10\%$ Approximation

"Estimate" means find an approximate amount.

Assume that $10\%$ of the energy at each trophic level reaches the next level. Producers hold $10{,}000$ kJ of energy available for transfer. Estimate the energy transferred to primary and then secondary consumers.

**Solution:**

1. Find the energy for primary consumers:

$$10{,}000(0.10)=1{,}000\text{ kJ}$$

2. Find the energy for secondary consumers:

$$1{,}000(0.10)=100\text{ kJ}$$

3. These results use the problem's $10\%$ assumption. Real ecosystems do not all transfer energy at this exact rate.

### #4: Identifying Symbiosis

"Classify" means put each example into the correct group.

Classify these relationships: (a) a tapeworm gets nutrients from a dog and harms it; (b) a pollinator gets food while helping pollinate a plant; (c) an organism gains shelter while measurements show no clear effect on its host.

**Solution:**

1. In (a), the tapeworm benefits and the dog is harmed. This is parasitism, $(+,-)$.
2. In (b), both partners benefit. This is mutualism, $(+,+)$.
3. In (c), one partner benefits and the other has no clear net effect. Under the stated evidence, this is commensalism, $(+,0)$.

### #5: Tracing Carbon and Water

A plant takes in $CO_2$ during photosynthesis. Later, decomposers break down dead plant material. In the same ecosystem, lake water becomes vapor, forms cloud droplets, and falls as rain. Name the processes and explain how matter moves.

**Solution:**

1. Photosynthesis moves carbon from carbon dioxide into plant material.
2. Decomposition returns carbon from dead material to the environment.
3. Evaporation changes lake water into water vapor.
4. Condensation changes vapor into liquid cloud droplets.
5. Precipitation returns water to the surface as rain. Runoff and underground flow can later carry water back to lakes or oceans.

### #6: Why Nitrogen Fixation Matters

Why can most plants not use atmospheric $N_2$ directly, and what process changes it into ammonia?

**Solution:**

1. Most plants lack a way to break nitrogen gas apart and change it into compounds they can use.
2. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria change $N_2$ into ammonia. This process is nitrogen fixation.
3. Other bacteria can later change ammonium into nitrite and nitrate through nitrification. Fixation and nitrification are different steps.

### #7: Following Phosphorus

Rain and weathering release phosphate from exposed rock into nearby soil. Grass grows there, and grazing animals eat it. After the organisms die, some phosphate returns to the soil and some is carried into a lake. Describe the path of phosphorus in this example.

**Solution:**

1. Weathering releases phosphate from rock into soil.
2. Grass takes up phosphate through its roots. Grazing moves phosphorus from grass into animals.
3. Decay returns phosphorus from dead organisms to the environment.
4. Runoff carries some phosphate to the lake, where some may settle into sediment. This path does not need a major gas stage in the air.

### #8: Reading Population Change

A deer population grows after several wet seasons increase plant food. Later, a drought reduces food and water. What ecological idea best explains why the population may stop growing or decrease?

**Solution:**

1. The drought reduces important resources.
2. With fewer resources, the environment can support fewer deer.
3. The carrying capacity has fallen. Deaths or movement out of the area may then reduce the population.

### #9: Losing a Species

A plant species depends on a particular insect for pollination. The insect disappears from the area, and no other pollinator takes its place. What is a likely effect on the plant and the animals that eat its seeds?

**Solution:**

1. With less pollination, the plants may produce fewer seeds.
2. Seed-eating animals may then have less food.
3. Losing one species can affect other species through their links in a community. The effect on the animals also depends on their other food sources.

### #10: Choosing an Environmental Response

A wetland has lost native plants because nearby land was cleared, and polluted runoff now enters the area. Which actions best address both problems and help check recovery?

**Solution:**

1. Restore native wetland habitat to replace lost shelter and food.
2. Reduce or stop the polluted runoff entering the wetland.
3. Monitor native populations and water quality to check recovery.
4. Both habitat work and pollution control are needed because the wetland faces different causes of damage.

## Exam Strategies

Read each food-web arrow as energy moving to the consumer. Trace every food path before predicting the effect of a population change.

Use $10\%$ in energy calculations only when the problem gives that approximation or tells you to assume it.

Keep the nitrogen-cycle steps separate. Fixation changes nitrogen gas into ammonia. Nitrification changes ammonium into nitrite and nitrate, while denitrification returns nitrogen gases to the air.

In cycle questions, follow the material and name the process that moves it. Water becoming vapor is evaporation. Phosphate leaving rock through weathering belongs to the phosphorus cycle.

Population questions may describe changes in resources, predators, disease, births, deaths, or movement. A change in carrying capacity does not mean the population changes at once.

Match each environmental response to a cause. A plan may need habitat protection, pollution control, harvest limits, or control of an invasive species.

Check the effect on each partner in symbiosis questions. Mutualism is $(+,+)$, commensalism is $(+,0)$, and parasitism is $(+,-)$.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Treating the $10\%$ Rule as Exact

> Energy transfer varies; $10\%$ is an estimate for problems that state this assumption.

Real transfer can be higher or lower. Use the rate given in the question.

### #2: Calling Nitrate a Direct Product of Nitrogen Fixation

> Nitrogen fixation changes $N_2$ into ammonia.

Nitrification carries out the later steps toward nitrite and nitrate. Calling both steps fixation mixes up different processes.

### #3: Forcing Every Organism Into One Fixed Trophic Level

> Consumers that eat both plants and animals can feed at different trophic levels.

Their feeding position depends on the food path being traced. Decomposers also receive material from many levels of a food web.

### #4: Assuming Symbiosis Categories Are Always Obvious

> Use the stated effect on each partner.

Commensalism requires no clear net effect on one partner. If both benefit, the relationship is mutualism. If one benefits and the other is harmed, it is parasitism.

### #5: Assuming One Population Changes by Itself

> Populations are linked to resources and to other populations in a community.

A rise or fall in one species can change food, competition, or predation for another. An alternative food source can reduce an effect, but it does not guarantee that nothing changes.

### #6: Using One Conservation Action for Every Problem

> Match the action to the cause of the damage.

Habitat loss, pollution, invasive species, and overharvesting need different responses. Some damaged ecosystems need several actions.

### #7: Treating Every Cycle as a Gas Cycle

> Phosphorus has no major gas reservoir in the atmosphere.

Trace its path through rocks, soil, water, and organisms. Do not add an air stage just because carbon and nitrogen have one.
