## Core Concept

### From Cells to Systems

The body is built in levels. A **cell** is the smallest unit of life. A **tissue** is a group of similar cells that do one job together. An **organ** is made of several tissues working as one unit. An **organ system** is a group of organs whose functions fit together.

Think of bricks, walls, rooms, and a house. The cells are the bricks, the tissues are the walls, the organs are the rooms, and the system is the whole house plan. The body has four basic tissue types: epithelial (covers surfaces and lines spaces), connective (supports and joins), muscle (contracts), and nervous (carries signals).

The heart shows the levels. Cardiac muscle cells form cardiac muscle tissue. That tissue, with connective and nervous tissue, forms the heart. The heart and blood vessels form the circulatory system.

This lesson surveys ten systems. Many textbooks also list the integumentary system (skin, hair, nails) separately. Many also join the immune structures with the lymphatic system.

### Digestive System

The digestive system breaks food into small molecules the body can absorb, and then absorbs them. Digestion has two kinds. **Mechanical digestion** physically breaks food into smaller pieces, such as chewing. **Chemical digestion** uses **enzymes**, which are proteins that speed up chemical reactions, to split food molecules.

The path of food is: **mouth** → **esophagus** → **stomach** → **small intestine** → **large intestine**. In the mouth, teeth chew and saliva contains salivary amylase, an enzyme that starts to split starch. The esophagus pushes food down by wave-like squeezing. The stomach churns food and adds acid and an enzyme that begins protein digestion.

The **small intestine** is where most chemical digestion and most nutrient absorption happen. Its inner wall has tiny finger-like folds called villi. They give a very large surface for absorbing nutrients into the blood. The **large intestine** absorbs water and electrolytes (dissolved salts) and compacts the leftover material into feces.

**Accessory organs** help digestion but food does not pass through them. They include the salivary glands, liver, gallbladder, and pancreas. The **liver** makes **bile**. The **gallbladder** stores and concentrates bile. The **pancreas** sends digestive enzymes and bicarbonate (which neutralizes stomach acid) into the small intestine.

Bile **emulsifies** fats, which means it breaks large fat drops into tiny droplets. This gives the fat-digesting enzyme **lipase** more surface to work on. Bile is not itself an enzyme.

### Respiratory System

The respiratory system moves air in and out and exchanges gases. Air passes through the nose or mouth, pharynx (throat), larynx (voice box), trachea (windpipe), bronchi, and smaller and smaller airways. It ends at the **alveoli**, tiny air sacs in the lungs.

**Ventilation** is the movement of air in and out. At rest, the **diaphragm**, a sheet of muscle under the lungs, contracts and flattens. The chest space gets bigger, the pressure inside drops, and air flows in. When the diaphragm relaxes, the chest space gets smaller and air flows out.

**Gas exchange** happens by **diffusion**, the movement of a substance from where it is more concentrated to where it is less concentrated. Alveolar air has more oxygen than the blood arriving in the lung capillaries. So oxygen diffuses into the blood. The arriving blood has more carbon dioxide than the alveolar air. So carbon dioxide diffuses out.

The exchange is fast because the alveolus wall and the capillary wall are each very thin, and many alveoli give a huge surface. Most oxygen then travels inside red blood cells, bound to a protein called **hemoglobin**.

### Cardiovascular / Circulatory System

The heart and blood vessels carry blood, which transports gases, nutrients, hormones, heat, and wastes. The heart has two upper chambers called **atria** and two lower chambers called **ventricles**. Valves keep blood moving one way.

**Arteries** carry blood away from the heart. **Veins** carry blood toward the heart. **Capillaries** are the tiny thin-walled vessels between them, where materials pass between blood and tissues.

Direction relative to the heart, not oxygen content, defines arteries and veins. This matters in the lungs. The **pulmonary arteries** carry oxygen-poor blood from the heart to the lungs. The **pulmonary veins** carry oxygen-rich blood from the lungs back to the heart. After birth, in the rest of the body the pattern is reversed: systemic arteries carry oxygen-rich blood and systemic veins carry oxygen-poor blood. (Before birth, the umbilical vein carries oxygen-rich blood to the fetus.)

The blood travels in two circuits, one after the other. **Pulmonary circulation** goes between the heart and the lungs. **Systemic circulation** goes between the heart and the rest of the body.

Full path: body → venae cavae (the large veins) → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta (the large artery) → body.

### Nervous System

The nervous system detects changes, processes the information, and sends signals that coordinate a response. Its cells are **neurons**, which carry electrical signals. At a junction between two neurons, called a **synapse**, a chemical messenger passes the signal across.

The **central nervous system (CNS)** is the brain and spinal cord. The **peripheral nervous system (PNS)** is all the nerves outside them. **Sensory neurons** carry signals toward the CNS. **Motor neurons** carry signals away from it to muscles and glands.

A **stimulus** is a change that the body can detect. A **receptor** is a cell or ending that detects it. An **effector** is a muscle or gland that responds. The basic chain is: stimulus → receptor → sensory neuron → CNS → motor neuron → effector → response.

A **reflex** is a fast, automatic response. In a reflex, the signal can turn around in the spinal cord without waiting for the brain. The brain is told at the same time, so you feel the touch just after you pull away.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: reflex arc with a hand touching a hot object, arrows from receptor in skin to sensory neuron to spinal cord to motor neuron to arm muscle, labeled "stimulus", "receptor", "sensory neuron", "spinal cord", "motor neuron", "effector" -->

The **cerebrum** handles perception, thinking, language, memory, and voluntary movement. The **cerebellum** helps with coordination, timing, and balance. The **brain stem** has centers that control breathing and blood pressure.

Motor control has two branches. **Somatic** control moves skeletal muscle and is usually voluntary, but reflexes such as pulling a hand from a hot pan also use it. **Autonomic** control is automatic and adjusts the heart, smooth muscle, and glands. The heart can beat on its own, but autonomic nerves speed it up or slow it down.

### Endocrine System

Endocrine glands release **hormones**, which are chemical messengers carried by the blood. A hormone affects only its **target cells**, the cells that have a matching receptor. Compared with nerve signals, hormone effects are usually slower and last longer.

A **negative feedback loop** keeps a body condition near a set level. When the condition moves away from the level, the body responds in the direction that reverses the change. This is how the body keeps steady conditions, a state called **homeostasis**. In **positive feedback**, the response increases the change instead, as in the contractions of childbirth.

Blood glucose shows a loop. After a meal, blood glucose rises. The pancreas releases **insulin**, which helps cells take in glucose and helps the liver store it as glycogen. Glucose falls, so less insulin is released. If glucose falls too low, the pancreas releases **glucagon**, which makes the liver release stored glucose into the blood.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: two-loop feedback diagram for blood glucose: "blood glucose rises" → "pancreas releases insulin" → "glucose falls"; and "blood glucose falls" → "pancreas releases glucagon" → "glucose rises" -->

The **pituitary** gland sits under the brain. It is nicknamed the "master gland" because its anterior (front) part releases hormones that control the thyroid, the adrenal glands, and the gonads. But the **hypothalamus**, a brain region above it, controls the pituitary. Feedback from the target glands also adjusts the pituitary.

The thyroid sets the pace of metabolism, the body's chemical reactions. The adrenal glands release hormones for stress responses, salt balance, and metabolism. The posterior (back) pituitary releases **antidiuretic hormone (ADH)** and oxytocin that the hypothalamus makes. ADH tells the kidneys to keep more water.

### Skeletal System

The skeleton supports the body, protects organs, stores minerals such as calcium, houses bone marrow where blood cells form, and works with muscles for movement. A typical adult skeleton has $206$ bones, though people vary. The **axial skeleton** is the skull, vertebral column, and rib cage. The **appendicular skeleton** is the limbs and the girdles (shoulder and hip) that attach them.

Bones meet at joints. **Ligaments** join bone to bone. **Tendons** join muscle to bone.

### Muscular System

Muscles make movement by contracting, which means shortening. A muscle can only pull, not push. So skeletal muscles work in pairs, such as the biceps and triceps, which pull the forearm in opposite directions.

There are three muscle types:

- **Skeletal muscle**: attached to bones, striated (striped under a microscope), and usually under voluntary control.
- **Smooth muscle**: not striped, involuntary, found in the walls of organs such as the intestine and blood vessels.
- **Cardiac muscle**: striated, involuntary, found only in the heart.

### Urinary System and Excretion

**Excretion** is the removal of metabolic wastes, which are waste substances made by the body's chemical reactions. The **urinary system** is the kidneys, ureters, bladder, and urethra. It also regulates water, electrolytes, acidity of the blood, and blood volume.

Each kidney has about a million tiny filters and tubes called **nephrons**. Blood is filtered at the start of each nephron. Then the tubule takes back (**reabsorbs**) useful substances and much of the water. It also adds (**secretes**) some extra substances into the fluid. The final fluid is urine: water, urea, other wastes, and ions in amounts the body adjusts.

Urea is a waste that the liver makes when it breaks down amino acids. Urine flows from the kidneys through the ureters to the bladder, and leaves through the urethra.

Excretion is wider than the urinary system. The lungs remove $CO_2$. The skin removes water, salts, and a little urea in sweat. The liver changes some wastes so they can leave in bile or urine.

### Reproductive System

The reproductive system makes **gametes**, which are sex cells (sperm and eggs), and sex hormones. Each gamete carries half the usual number of chromosomes, $23$ instead of $46$. Gametes form by **meiosis**, a special cell division that halves the chromosome number.

The testes make sperm and the hormone testosterone. Sperm leave the testis through the epididymis and the vas deferens, and exit through the urethra. The ovaries hold developing egg cells (**oocytes**) and make the hormones estrogen and progesterone.

In most cycles, an ovary releases one oocyte (**ovulation**) and it enters the uterine tube, also called the fallopian tube. Fertilization most often happens in the uterine tube, when a sperm fuses with the oocyte. The oocyte completes its last division when the sperm enters.

The fertilized cell is a **zygote** and has $46$ chromosomes. It divides as it moves along the tube. It reaches the **uterus** and attaches to the uterine lining. This attachment is **implantation**.

Hormones tie the cycle together. The pituitary hormones FSH and LH act on the ovary. Estrogen and progesterone from the ovary prepare the uterine lining. If implantation does not happen, the lining is shed.

### Immune / Lymphatic Defenses

A **pathogen** is a germ that can cause disease. An **antigen** is a molecule that the immune system can recognize, often on a pathogen. The **lymphatic system** returns extra tissue fluid to the blood and holds immune cells in lymph nodes and other organs.

**Innate immunity** is the rapid, general defense that is ready from birth. It includes barriers (skin, mucus), inflammation, and **phagocytes**, which are cells that engulf and destroy invaders. It reacts the same way each time and does not build memory of a particular antigen.

**Adaptive immunity** is slower on a first meeting but specific to one antigen. It uses white blood cells called **lymphocytes**. An activated B cell becomes a **plasma cell** that releases **antibodies**, proteins that bind a particular antigen. T cells help coordinate the response and kill infected cells.

After the first response, **memory cells** stay behind. On a later meeting with the same antigen, the response is faster and stronger. A vaccine gives the body a safe look at an antigen, so memory cells form without the disease.

### How the Systems Work Together

No system works alone. Every cell needs oxygen and nutrients and must lose wastes. The digestive system absorbs nutrients, the respiratory system takes in oxygen, and the circulatory system delivers both. The urinary and respiratory systems remove wastes. The nervous and endocrine systems adjust all of them.

## Examples

For the questions below, "trace" means follow a path step by step, and "classify" means put each item in its correct group.

### #1: Tracing the Digestive Path

Trace the order in which food passes through these organs: large intestine, stomach, esophagus, small intestine, mouth.

**Solution:**

1. Mouth.
2. Esophagus.
3. Stomach.
4. Small intestine.
5. Large intestine.

### #2: Levels of Organization

Classify each as cell, tissue, organ, or organ system: (a) a neuron, (b) the stomach, (c) the nervous system, (d) smooth muscle in the intestine wall.

**Solution:**

1. A neuron is a single nerve cell: cell.
2. The stomach is made of several tissues: organ.
3. The nervous system is brain, spinal cord, and nerves together: organ system.
4. Smooth muscle is a group of similar cells with one job: tissue.

### #3: Pulmonary vs. Systemic Circulation

Blood travels from the heart to the lungs and back. Another path takes blood from the heart to the leg muscles and back. Identify the circuits.

**Solution:**

1. Heart and lungs: pulmonary circulation.
2. Heart and body tissues such as leg muscles: systemic circulation.

### #4: Vessels and Oxygen Content

Which vessel carries oxygen-poor blood away from the heart? Which carries oxygen-rich blood toward the heart?

**Solution:**

1. Arteries carry blood away from the heart, and veins carry it toward the heart.
2. The vessel going away from the heart with oxygen-poor blood is the pulmonary artery, which leads to the lungs.
3. The vessel coming toward the heart with oxygen-rich blood is the pulmonary vein, which comes from the lungs.

### #5: Voluntary or Involuntary Muscle?

Classify (a) kicking a ball, (b) intestinal wall contractions, and (c) the heartbeat by muscle type and usual control.

**Solution:**

1. Kicking: skeletal muscle, voluntary (somatic) control.
2. Intestinal contractions: smooth muscle, involuntary.
3. Heartbeat: cardiac muscle, involuntary. The heart sets its own rhythm, and autonomic nerves and hormones adjust it.

### #6: Gas Exchange in the Alveoli

After hard exercise, a person breathes faster. Describe how oxygen and carbon dioxide move at an alveolus.

**Solution:**

1. Blood reaching the lung capillaries has little oxygen and much carbon dioxide.
2. Alveolar air has more oxygen and less carbon dioxide than this blood.
3. By diffusion, oxygen moves from the alveolar air into the blood.
4. Carbon dioxide moves from the blood into the alveolar air and leaves when the person breathes out.

### #7: A Stimulus-Response Chain

A person touches a hot pan and pulls the hand away at once. Trace the signal from stimulus to response.

**Solution:**

1. Stimulus: the heat on the skin.
2. Receptors in the skin detect it, and sensory neurons carry the signal to the spinal cord.
3. In the spinal cord the signal passes to motor neurons.
4. Motor neurons carry it to the arm muscles, which are the effector.
5. The muscles contract and the hand moves away. The brain is told too, so the person feels the pain afterward.

### #8: Tracing a Feedback Loop

A student eats a sweet snack and blood glucose rises. Explain how the body returns it to normal.

**Solution:**

1. The pancreas detects the higher glucose and releases insulin.
2. Insulin helps cells take in glucose, and the liver stores some as glycogen.
3. Blood glucose falls toward its usual level.
4. As glucose falls, less insulin is released. The change causes the opposite response, so this is negative feedback.

### #9: Why "Master Gland" Needs a Qualification

Why is the pituitary called the "master gland," and why is the nickname incomplete?

**Solution:**

1. Several anterior pituitary hormones control the thyroid, the adrenal glands, and the gonads.
2. But the hypothalamus controls the anterior pituitary through its own hormones.
3. The hypothalamus also makes the hormones that the posterior pituitary releases.
4. Hormone levels from the target glands feed back to the pituitary too.
5. So the pituitary is part of a network, not an independent boss.

### #10: What Does a Nephron Do?

Describe the nephron's role and name some substances that leave in urine.

**Solution:**

1. Blood plasma is filtered at the start of the nephron.
2. The tubule reabsorbs useful substances and much of the water, and secretes some extra substances.
3. Urine carries urea and other wastes, plus extra water and ions.
4. Extra water is not a waste like urea. Removing it is part of keeping water balance.

### #11: Fertilization Site and Next Steps

A sperm fuses with an oocyte. Where does this usually happen, and what are the next two events?

**Solution:**

1. Fertilization most often happens in the uterine tube.
2. The zygote divides as it moves toward the uterus.
3. It attaches to the uterine lining. This is implantation.

### #12: Innate vs. Adaptive Immunity

A cut triggers swelling and phagocytes right away. Years after a vaccination, memory cells let the body respond fast to the same pathogen. Identify the two kinds of immunity.

**Solution:**

1. The immediate swelling and phagocytes: innate immunity.
2. The antigen-specific memory response: adaptive immunity.

### #13: Systems Working Together

A student eats rice, then runs. Trace how glucose and oxygen reach the leg muscles, and how the wastes leave.

**Solution:**

1. Digestive system: the rice starch is split into glucose, which is absorbed into blood in the small intestine.
2. Respiratory system: oxygen diffuses into the blood at the alveoli.
3. Circulatory system: the heart pumps blood carrying glucose and oxygen to the leg muscles, where capillaries let them pass into the muscle cells.
4. The muscle cells use them and make $CO_2$. Blood carries the $CO_2$ back to the lungs, and it leaves when the person breathes out.
5. Nervous system: the brain stem raises the breathing rate and the heart rate to meet the demand.
6. Urinary system: the kidneys filter the blood and remove wastes in urine.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For arteries and veins, use **direction**: arteries go away from the heart and veins go toward it. Oxygen content does not decide the name.

For the pancreas, remember its two jobs. Its enzymes enter the small intestine. Insulin and glucagon enter the blood.

For immunity, separate fast, general innate defenses from slower, antigen-specific adaptive responses with memory.

For nerve pathways, name the parts in order: stimulus, receptor, sensory neuron, CNS, motor neuron, effector.

For organ systems, do not assume an organ belongs to exactly one system. The pancreas, gonads, thymus, skin, and bones each serve more than one system.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Saying Bile Digests Fat Enzymatically

> Bile emulsifies fats. Lipase does the chemical digestion.

Emulsification only breaks fat into smaller drops, which gives the enzyme more surface.

### #2: Saying Arteries Carry Oxygen-Rich Blood

> Arteries carry blood away from the heart, whatever its oxygen level.

The pulmonary arteries carry oxygen-poor blood, and the pulmonary veins carry oxygen-rich blood.

### #3: Treating the Pituitary as an Uncontrolled "Boss"

> The hypothalamus controls the pituitary, and feedback adjusts the whole chain.

"Master gland" is a shortcut, not the full picture.

### #4: Equating the Urinary System with All Excretion

> Kidneys are central to excreting wastes in urine, but the lungs, liver, and skin also remove or process wastes.

Use "urinary system" only for the kidneys, ureters, bladder, and urethra.

### #5: Saying B Cells Themselves Release Most Antibodies

> Activated B cells turn into plasma cells, and plasma cells release the antibodies.

B cells also form memory cells.

### #6: Calling Every Feedback Loop Positive or Negative by Its Direction

> Negative feedback means the response reverses the change, not that the level goes down.

If glucose is too low and glucagon raises it, that is also negative feedback.
