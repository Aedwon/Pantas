## Core Concept

Cells need energy for every job they do. **Cellular respiration** releases the energy in sugar and moves it into ATP, the molecule cells use for work. Some cells do this with oxygen and some do not.

This lesson uses ATP, glucose and enzymes from the lesson Cellular Metabolism: Enzymes, Biomolecules and Photosynthesis. Read that lesson first if these are new to you.

### Words used in this lesson

**ATP** (adenosine triphosphate) is the molecule that gives a cell energy for its work. **Glucose** ($C_6H_{12}O_6$) is a sugar and the usual fuel. An **enzyme** is a molecule that speeds up a reaction without being used up.

A **prokaryote** is a cell with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles. A **eukaryote** is a cell with a nucleus and organelles. An **organelle** is a small part inside a cell with its own job.

The **cytosol** is the watery fluid in a cell, outside the organelles. A **chloroplast** is the organelle where photosynthesis happens in plants and algae. The **light reactions** are the first stage of photosynthesis, and they need light.

**Catabolic** means breaking big molecules down into smaller ones. An **electron** is a tiny charged particle, and moving electrons carry energy. An **electron carrier** is a molecule that picks up electrons in one place and drops them off in another.

### Cellular Respiration

**Cellular respiration** is a set of catabolic pathways that move energy from organic molecules into ATP. Some of the energy is lost as heat.

In **aerobic respiration** (aerobic means with oxygen), oxygen is the final **electron acceptor**, the last molecule to receive electrons. A simplified overall equation is:

$$C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2 \rightarrow 6CO_2 + 6H_2O + \text{ATP and heat}$$

The carbon in the carbon dioxide comes from glucose. The oxygen gas ends up in water.

Aerobic respiration in a eukaryote has four stages:

1. **Glycolysis** (in the cytosol) splits one glucose, which has six carbons, into two **pyruvate** molecules, which have three carbons each. It gives a net gain of $2$ ATP and makes $2$ NADH. NADH is an electron carrier made when **NAD+** gains electrons. Glycolysis needs no oxygen.
2. **Pyruvate oxidation** (in the mitochondrial matrix) turns each pyruvate into a two-carbon unit called acetyl CoA. One carbon leaves each time as carbon dioxide. More NADH forms.
3. The **citric acid cycle** (in the matrix) breaks acetyl CoA down further. It releases the rest of the carbon as carbon dioxide and makes a little ATP. It also fills NADH and a second carrier, FADH$_2$, with electrons.
4. **Oxidative phosphorylation** (in the inner mitochondrial membrane) is where NADH and FADH$_2$ drop off their electrons. The electrons pass along a chain of proteins, the **electron transport chain**. The energy pumps hydrogen ions (H$^+$) across the membrane.

The hydrogen ions then flow back through an enzyme called **ATP synthase**, which makes most of the ATP. Think of water that builds up behind a dam and turns a turbine as it flows through. At the end of the chain, oxygen takes the electrons and combines with H$^+$ to form water.

A mitochondrion has an outer membrane and a folded inner membrane. The folds are called **cristae**. The space inside the inner membrane is the **matrix**.

One glucose gives only $2$ ATP in glycolysis and a few more in the citric acid cycle. Most ATP comes from oxidative phosphorylation.

Current textbooks estimate about $30$ to $32$ ATP per glucose. Older books say $36$ to $38$. The real number varies with the cell type and how the cell moves NADH into the mitochondrion.

Prokaryotes have no mitochondria. They run the later stages of respiration in the cytosol and across the cell's plasma membrane. So the location split above belongs to eukaryotes.

### Respiration without oxygen

Some cells have no oxygen, or cannot use it. They still need ATP. There are two main ways to make it.

**Anaerobic respiration** is used by some prokaryotes. It has an electron transport chain, like aerobic respiration, but the final electron acceptor is not oxygen. It may be a nitrate or a sulfate compound. It usually makes less ATP than aerobic respiration, but more than fermentation.

**Fermentation** has no electron transport chain. Glycolysis alone makes the ATP, a net of $2$ per glucose.

The problem is that glycolysis turns NAD+ into NADH, and the cell runs out of NAD+. Fermentation fixes this: NADH gives its electrons to pyruvate or to a molecule made from pyruvate. That turns NADH back into NAD+, so glycolysis can continue.

There are two common kinds:

- **Lactic acid fermentation** turns pyruvate into lactate (lactic acid). It happens in some bacteria and in animal muscle cells working hard. It releases no carbon dioxide.
- **Alcohol fermentation** turns pyruvate into ethanol and carbon dioxide. Yeast does this. The carbon dioxide makes bread dough rise.

Many cells, including yeast and human muscle, use aerobic respiration when oxygen is present and ferment when it is not. Some bacteria are **obligate anaerobes**, which means oxygen harms them.

### How the two processes connect

Photosynthesis and aerobic respiration have complementary overall inputs and outputs. They are not the same pathway run backward. They use different enzymes, steps, membranes and ways of making ATP.

Matter cycles: carbon, hydrogen and oxygen move between sugar, carbon dioxide, water and oxygen gas. Energy does not cycle. It enters mostly as light and leaves as heat.

Plants do both processes. Respiration goes on in all living plant cells day and night, including roots, which need oxygen from the soil.

In plants, photosynthesis happens only in cells with chloroplasts, and only when there is light. Some prokaryotes, such as cyanobacteria, also photosynthesize. They have no chloroplasts.

In bright light a leaf makes more sugar and oxygen than it uses. In the dark, most plants take in oxygen and give off carbon dioxide, like an animal cell. CAM plants, such as pineapple, are an exception: they open their pores at night and take in carbon dioxide.

## Examples

### #1: Where Does Each Process Happen?

In a plant cell, name the organelle where photosynthesis takes place. Then say where each stage of aerobic respiration takes place.

**Solution:**

1. In plant cells, photosynthesis takes place in chloroplasts.
2. Glycolysis takes place in the cytosol, outside the mitochondrion.
3. Pyruvate oxidation and the citric acid cycle take place in the mitochondrial matrix.
4. The electron transport chain and ATP synthase are in the inner mitochondrial membrane.
5. In plant and algal cells, "chloroplast means photosynthesis" is a good rule. Cyanobacteria photosynthesize without chloroplasts, so the rule does not cover prokaryotes.
6. "All respiration happens in mitochondria" is too broad. Glycolysis is in the cytosol, and prokaryotes have no mitochondria.

### #2: The Connection Between the Two Processes

Explain how photosynthesis and aerobic respiration are connected in an ecosystem.

**Solution:**

1. Photosynthesis builds glucose and releases oxygen.
2. Aerobic respiration uses glucose and oxygen. It releases carbon dioxide and water and moves energy into ATP.
3. The carbon dioxide and water can be used again in photosynthesis.
4. Matter cycles through both processes. Energy enters as light and leaves as heat.

### #3: Do Plants Perform Cellular Respiration Too?

A student says, "Plants only perform photosynthesis, and only animals perform cellular respiration." Explain the error.

**Solution:**

1. Plant cells need ATP for growth, moving substances, building molecules and repair.
2. Plants make this ATP by cellular respiration, as animals do.
3. Respiration is also done by fungi, bacteria and other organisms.
4. Photosynthesis and respiration are pathways. They are not labels that sort organisms into two groups.

### #4: A Leaf in Light and in Darkness

A sealed jar holds a green leaf. In bright light, the carbon dioxide in the jar goes down. In darkness, the carbon dioxide goes up. Explain both results.

**Solution:**

1. In bright light, photosynthesis in the leaf uses carbon dioxide faster than respiration releases it.
2. The carbon dioxide in the jar goes down.
3. In darkness, the light reactions stop, so there is no photosynthesis.
4. Respiration goes on, so the leaf releases carbon dioxide and the amount in the jar goes up.

At one light level between these two, photosynthesis and respiration run at equal rates. The carbon dioxide in the jar then does not change.

### #5: Following One Glucose Through Aerobic Respiration

In aerobic respiration of one glucose, at which stages is carbon dioxide released, and where is oxygen used?

**Solution:**

1. Glucose has six carbons. Glycolysis splits it into two pyruvate molecules of three carbons each. No carbon is lost here.
2. Pyruvate oxidation removes one carbon from each pyruvate as carbon dioxide. That gives $2$ carbon dioxide.
3. The citric acid cycle releases the remaining four carbons as $4$ carbon dioxide. The total is $6$.
4. Oxygen is used at the end of the electron transport chain. It accepts electrons and forms water.

Carbon dioxide is released in pyruvate oxidation and the citric acid cycle. Oxygen is used in oxidative phosphorylation.

### #6: Fermentation and Anaerobic Respiration

Yeast cells are sealed in a flask of sugar solution with no oxygen. Bubbles form. Name the process and its products. Explain why it keeps going and compare its ATP yield with aerobic respiration.

**Solution:**

1. Without oxygen, the yeast carries out alcohol fermentation. The bubbles are carbon dioxide. The other product is ethanol.
2. Glycolysis makes NADH from NAD+. Fermentation passes the electrons from NADH to a molecule made from pyruvate, so NAD+ forms again.
3. With NAD+ back, glycolysis can run again and keep making ATP.
4. Glycolysis alone nets $2$ ATP per glucose. Aerobic respiration gives about $30$ to $32$ ATP, so fermentation gives far less.

In a hard-working muscle cell short of oxygen, the same logic applies, but the product is lactate and no carbon dioxide forms. Anaerobic respiration is different: it still has an electron transport chain, with an acceptor other than oxygen.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Use the simplified equations to track overall matter. They are not one-step reactions, and they are not exact reverses of each other.

Remember the location rule for aerobic respiration in eukaryotes: **glycolysis in the cytosol, the later stages in the mitochondrion**. It does not hold for prokaryotes, which have no mitochondria.

If a question says oxygen is needed, it describes an **aerobic** pathway. Anaerobic respiration and fermentation make ATP without oxygen.

To tell the two oxygen-free pathways apart, ask whether there is an electron transport chain. Anaerobic respiration has one, with a final acceptor that is not oxygen. Fermentation has none.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Saying All Cellular Respiration Needs Oxygen

> Aerobic respiration uses oxygen, but anaerobic respiration and fermentation do not.

Some prokaryotes use other final electron acceptors. Fermentation lets cells keep glycolysis running without oxygen.

### #2: Saying Cellular Respiration Happens Entirely in Mitochondria

> Glycolysis happens in the cytosol of prokaryotes and eukaryotes alike.

In eukaryotes the mitochondria carry out the later stages of aerobic respiration. They do not carry out every step of glucose breakdown.

### #3: Treating Photosynthesis and Respiration as Exact Reverses

> The simplified equations look like mirror images, but the pathways are different.

They use different enzymes, steps and membranes. Only the overall inputs and outputs are complementary.

### #4: Saying Plants Take In Carbon Dioxide and Give Out Oxygen All the Time

> This is true only while photosynthesis is faster than respiration.

In the dark, or in cells without chloroplasts, most plants take in oxygen and give off carbon dioxide. CAM plants, such as pineapple, also take in carbon dioxide at night.
