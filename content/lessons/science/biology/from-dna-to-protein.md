## Core Concept

### DNA, RNA, and Protein

DNA has four bases: adenine ($A$), thymine ($T$), cytosine ($C$), and guanine ($G$). $A$ pairs with $T$, and $C$ pairs with $G$.

DNA is double-stranded: two strands pair up base to base. RNA is single-stranded and uses uracil ($U$) in place of $T$. In RNA, $A$ pairs with $U$.

In **transcription**, the cell makes a copy of a gene in the form of **mRNA** (messenger RNA). The copy is built along one DNA strand of the gene, called the **template strand**. Each template base is matched to its pair.

In a human cell, the genes on the chromosomes are in the nucleus, so their transcription happens there. A cell without a nucleus, such as a bacterium, transcribes in the cytoplasm. Mitochondria also hold a little DNA of their own, and they transcribe it inside the mitochondrion.

The finished mRNA leaves the nucleus and goes to a **ribosome** in the cytoplasm. A ribosome is a small structure that builds proteins.

In **translation**, the ribosome reads the mRNA in groups of three bases called **codons**. Each codon stands for one amino acid or a stop signal. The amino acids join to form a protein.

## Examples

### #1: From DNA to Protein

A template DNA strand reads $TACGGATTC$. The codon $AUG$ stands for methionine, $CCU$ for proline, and $AAG$ for lysine. Give the mRNA and the amino acid chain.

**Solution:**

1. Pair each DNA base, using $U$ in RNA: $T \to A$, $A \to U$, $C \to G$, $G \to C$.

$$TAC\ GGA\ TTC \rightarrow AUG\ CCU\ AAG$$

2. Read the mRNA in codons: $AUG$, $CCU$, $AAG$.
3. Replace each codon with its amino acid.

The chain is methionine, proline, lysine.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Writing T in RNA

> RNA has uracil, not thymine.

When DNA is copied into mRNA, $A$ becomes $U$. A choice that shows $T$ in mRNA is wrong.
