## Core Concept

### Words You Need First

A **gene** is a section of DNA that helps control a trait. An **allele** is one version of a gene. For example, a beetle gene for color may have a green allele and a brown allele.

A **chromosome** is a long strand of DNA that carries many genes. A **genotype** is the set of alleles an individual carries for a gene.

In a diploid organism made by sexual reproduction, most genes lie on chromosomes that are not sex chromosomes, and they come as a pair, one allele from each parent. In humans, a male has only one allele for a gene on the X chromosome. A haploid organism, such as a bacterium, has one copy of each gene.

A **phenotype** is the trait you can see or measure, such as green or brown color. A **population** is a group of the same kind of organism living in one place and able to breed with each other.

A **gamete** is a sex cell, such as a sperm or an egg. In sexual reproduction, parents pass alleles to offspring through gametes.

In asexual reproduction, such as a bacterium splitting in two, one parent passes its DNA to its offspring and no gametes fuse. The lesson on genetics and heredity explains inheritance in full.

### What Evolution Is

**Evolution** is a change in the inherited makeup of a population from one generation to the next. Individuals do not evolve. Populations do.

Scientists track this change with allele frequency. The **allele frequency** is the share of all copies of a gene that are one particular allele. If a population has $100$ copies of a gene and $30$ are the brown allele, the brown allele frequency is $\dfrac{30}{100} = 0.30$.

Evolution is any change in allele frequency over generations. Four things can cause it: mutation, genetic drift, gene flow and natural selection.

Nonrandom mating, such as choosing mates that look alike, changes how alleles are paired into genotypes. It does not change allele frequency by itself.

### Mutation

A **mutation** is a change in DNA. It is the original source of new alleles. A mutation happens by chance. It does not appear because the organism needs it.

A mutation reaches offspring only when it is in the DNA that the offspring receive. In sexual reproduction, that means the cells that make gametes. In asexual reproduction, such as budding or splitting, it means the cells that form the offspring.

A mutation in a human skin cell stays with that body. In a bacterium that splits in two, a mutation in its DNA passes to the daughter cells that receive that DNA.

Many mutations have no effect on the trait. Some harm the organism, and a few help it in a certain environment.

### Genetic Drift

**Genetic drift** is a change in allele frequency caused by chance alone. Some individuals have more offspring than others just by luck, not because their traits are better.

Drift is strong in small populations and weak in large ones. In a small group, a few unlucky deaths can remove an allele completely. In a very large group, luck averages out and drift is small, though never exactly zero.

Two well-known cases of drift are the bottleneck effect and the founder effect. In a **bottleneck**, a disaster leaves only a few survivors, and their alleles are a chance sample of the old population. In the **founder effect**, a few individuals start a new population, and at first it carries only the alleles those founders had.

### Gene Flow

**Gene flow** is the movement of alleles between populations when individuals or gametes move and then breed. An example is wind carrying pollen from one field to another.

Gene flow can add alleles to a population. It also tends to make two connected populations more alike. When gene flow stops, the populations can drift apart in their alleles.

### Natural Selection

Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace separately developed the idea of evolution by **natural selection**. Their papers were read together at one meeting in $1858$. Darwin published *On the Origin of Species* in $1859$.

Natural selection needs three things.

1. **Variation**: individuals in a population differ.
2. **Heritability**: some of that variation is passed on through alleles.
3. **Differential reproductive success**: in a given environment, some heritable variants leave more surviving offspring than others.

When all three hold, the favored variants usually become more common over generations. **Fitness** means how many offspring survive to reproduce, compared with others in the population. It does not mean strength or a long life.

An **adaptation** is a heritable trait that helps its bearers survive and reproduce in their environment. Selection works on the traits individuals have, but only the population changes.

Selection does not always move a trait in one direction. In stabilizing selection, the middle form does best, so the population stays about the same. Human birth weight is a textbook case.

If differences are not heritable, selection changes nothing in the next generation. A plant that grows tall because it got more water does not pass tallness to its seeds. If there is no variation, there is nothing for selection to favor.

### Comparing the Four Causes

Mutation adds new alleles by chance. Drift changes frequencies by chance. Gene flow moves alleles between populations.

Selection is the only one of the four that regularly improves how well a population fits its environment. It is the cause that builds adaptations. Drift and gene flow can change frequencies without helping the organism.

### Speciation

A **species** is a kind of organism. Scientists use several definitions. The **biological species concept** says a species is a group of populations that can breed with each other in nature and produce fertile offspring, but do not usually breed with other groups.

**Fertile** means able to have offspring. **Sterile** means unable to have offspring. This concept is used mainly for organisms that reproduce sexually.

**Speciation** is the formation of a new species. It happens when **reproductive isolation** evolves. This means two groups can no longer, or hardly ever, exchange alleles through breeding.

Reproductive isolation can take different forms. The groups may breed in different seasons or respond to different courtship signals. They may mate but produce no offspring, or sterile offspring.

A mule, from a horse and a donkey, is almost always sterile.

In **allopatric speciation**, a physical barrier such as a rising sea or a mountain range splits one population into two, and gene flow stops. Each group then changes through its own mutations, drift and selection. If reproductive isolation evolves, they usually stay separate species even when they meet again.

In **sympatric speciation**, a new species forms without a physical barrier. In plants, **polyploidy** is a common route: a polyploid has extra whole sets of chromosomes. A polyploid plant may be unable to breed with its parent species at once.

**Adaptive radiation** is when one ancestor species gives rise to many species that fit different ways of living. Darwin's finches on the Galapagos Islands are an example.

They share a common ancestor and now differ in beaks and food. Their history also includes some interbreeding between species, so it is not a simple one-island-one-food story.

## Examples

### #1: Frequency of an Allele

A beetle population has $50$ individuals. Together they carry $100$ copies of a color gene, and $30$ copies are the brown allele. What is the frequency of the brown allele?

**Solution:**

1. The question gives the total: the $50$ individuals carry $100$ copies of the gene.
2. Divide the copies of the brown allele by all copies.

$$\dfrac{30}{100} = 0.30$$

The brown allele frequency is $0.30$.

### #2: Pesticide Resistance

A population of insects already has heritable variants that differ in resistance to a pesticide. After repeated spraying, resistant insects become much more common. Explain the result.

**Solution:**

1. Heritable variation existed before the spraying. The insects did not grow resistant because they needed to.
2. Susceptible insects died more often and left fewer offspring.
3. Resistant insects left more offspring, so later generations had more of them.
4. The resistance alleles rose in frequency. This is evolution by natural selection.

### #3: Acquired Changes Are Not Inherited

A student says, "An animal built a useful body change by using it often, so its offspring were born with that change." What is wrong with this as an explanation of natural selection?

**Solution:**

1. Natural selection needs heritable variation, which means variation in alleles that parents pass to offspring.
2. A body change gained by use, such as larger muscles from exercise, does not change the alleles that parents pass on.
3. Offspring therefore do not inherit it.
4. A population changes when heritable variants differ in reproductive success across generations.

### #4: Chance Versus Selection

A storm kills most of a beetle population at random, leaving $5$ beetles. Before the storm, the brown allele frequency was $0.30$. Among the $10$ gene copies in the survivors, $6$ are brown. Being brown did not help any beetle survive. Is this natural selection?

**Solution:**

1. Find the new frequency.

$$\dfrac{6}{10} = 0.60$$

2. The frequency rose from $0.30$ to $0.60$, so the population evolved.
3. The storm did not favor any trait. The survivors were a chance sample.
4. This is genetic drift, specifically a bottleneck. It is not natural selection, and the new frequency does not mean brown is better.

### #5: Gene Flow

An island population has a green allele at frequency $0.10$. A mainland population has the same allele at $0.50$. Seeds and pollen from the mainland reach the island each year and the plants breed. What happens to the island's green allele frequency?

**Solution:**

1. The mainland plants carry more copies of the green allele.
2. Their pollen and seeds bring those copies into the island population.
3. As the mixing continues, the island frequency tends to rise toward the mainland frequency.
4. The two populations become more alike. This is gene flow, not mutation, because the allele already existed elsewhere.

### #6: Geographic Isolation and Speciation

A population is split by a new barrier, and gene flow between the two groups stops. Does the split instantly create two species?

**Solution:**

1. No. The barrier stops gene flow but does not by itself create reproductive isolation.
2. Each group changes through its own mutations, drift and selection.
3. If barriers to interbreeding evolve, the groups become separate species. They usually stay separate even if the barrier is removed.
4. The barrier is a starting condition for allopatric speciation. It is not the definition of speciation.

### #7: Speciation Without a Barrier

A plant species grows in one meadow. A mutation doubles the whole chromosome set in one plant, and its offspring cannot breed with the other plants. They breed with each other. Is a new species forming, and is a barrier needed?

**Solution:**

1. The offspring are reproductively isolated from the parent plants.
2. They form a group that exchanges alleles only among themselves.
3. No physical barrier was needed.
4. This is sympatric speciation by polyploidy.

## Exam Strategies

For a natural selection question, look for three things: variation, heritability, and different reproductive success. If any of the three is missing, it is not selection.

When selection favors one extreme, the trait shifts across generations. Under stabilizing selection, it stays about the same.

For a question on causes of evolution, ask what changed the frequency.

Chance in a small group points to drift. Movement of individuals or gametes between populations points to gene flow. A brand-new allele points to mutation.

Better survival and reproduction because of a heritable trait points to selection.

For a speciation question, find the reproductive isolation. A physical barrier suggests allopatric speciation. A new species without a barrier suggests sympatric speciation.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Crediting Darwin Alone

> Darwin and Wallace developed natural selection separately, and their papers were read together in $1858$.

Darwin's $1859$ book gave the idea its most influential early explanation.

### #2: Thinking Selection Is the Only Cause

> Mutation, drift and gene flow also change populations.

Not every change in allele frequency is an adaptation. Selection is the cause that produces adaptation.

### #3: Thinking Needs Create Traits

> Organisms do not grow a trait because they need it, and evolution has no goal.

Variants arise by chance. Whether selection makes a variant spread depends on the present environment.

### #4: Thinking Individuals Evolve

> An individual does not evolve a new inherited trait in its lifetime.

Individuals may change during life, but changes gained during life are not passed on. A mutation in the DNA that offspring receive is the exception. The population is what evolves.

### #5: Thinking Fitness Means Strength

> Fitness is about offspring, not muscles or age.

A weak but fertile animal can be fitter than a strong one that leaves no offspring.

### #6: Equating a Barrier With a New Species

> A barrier alone does not make a species.

Speciation needs reproductive isolation to evolve. Species concepts also vary, and some separate species can still hybridize, which means they interbreed and produce offspring.
