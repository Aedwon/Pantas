## Core Concept

The **cell** is the basic unit of life. In eukaryotic cells, membrane-bound structures called **organelles** carry out specialized functions.

### Organelles and Their Functions

The **nucleus** contains most of a eukaryotic cell's DNA and helps regulate gene expression and cell activities.

The **mitochondria** carry out major stages of aerobic cellular respiration and generate much of a eukaryotic cell's ATP.

**Ribosomes** build proteins by translating instructions carried by messenger RNA (mRNA).

The **endoplasmic reticulum (ER)** is a membrane network. The rough ER, which has ribosomes attached, helps synthesize and process many proteins destined for membranes, organelles, or secretion. The smooth ER has roles that include lipid synthesis, detoxification, and calcium storage, depending on cell type.

The **Golgi apparatus** modifies, sorts, and packages proteins and lipids for delivery to different cellular destinations.

### Typical Plant vs. Animal Cells

A typical land-plant cell has several structures that a typical animal cell lacks:

- A **cell wall** rich in cellulose outside the plasma membrane, providing support and shape.
- **Chloroplasts** in photosynthetic cells, where photosynthesis occurs.
- A large **central vacuole**, important for storage, breakdown of macromolecules, and maintaining turgor pressure.

These are useful plant-versus-animal clues, but they are not structures unique to the plant kingdom in all of life. For example, fungi and many prokaryotes also have cell walls, though made of different materials, and many algae contain chloroplasts.

Typical animal cells have a centrosome containing centrioles and commonly use **lysosomes** for intracellular digestion. Most plant cells lack the animal-type centrosome, and many lysosome-like digestive functions are carried out by the central vacuole.

### Cell Membrane and Cellular Transport

The **plasma membrane** is selectively permeable: it controls movement between the cell and its surroundings.

**Diffusion** is the net movement of particles down their concentration gradient, from higher to lower concentration, due to random molecular motion. Simple diffusion itself requires no cellular energy input.

**Osmosis** is the net diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane in response to differences in water availability/osmotic concentration. In the simple cases used here, water moves toward the side with the higher concentration of nonpenetrating solute.

**Active transport** moves a substance against its electrochemical or concentration gradient using energy. Some transporters use ATP directly; others use energy stored in an ion gradient that another process established.

**Tonicity** describes how an external solution changes cell volume through osmosis. It depends especially on solutes that cannot freely cross the membrane.

- **Hypotonic** to the cell: the external solution has a lower effective concentration of nonpenetrating solute, so water tends to enter.
- **Hypertonic** to the cell: the external solution has a higher effective concentration of nonpenetrating solute, so water tends to leave.
- **Isotonic** to the cell: there is no net change in cell volume from osmosis.

An animal cell can lyse in a sufficiently hypotonic solution because it lacks a cell wall. A plant cell instead develops **turgor pressure** as water enters and presses the membrane against the cell wall.

## Examples

### #1: Matching Organelles to Their Jobs

Match each organelle to its main function: (a) mitochondria, (b) ribosomes, (c) Golgi apparatus, (d) nucleus.

**Solution:**

1. Mitochondria: major site of aerobic ATP production in eukaryotic cells.
2. Ribosomes: protein synthesis.
3. Golgi apparatus: modification, sorting, and packaging of proteins and lipids.
4. Nucleus: contains most cellular DNA and participates in regulation of gene expression.

### #2: Plant Cell or Animal Cell?

A eukaryotic cell has a cellulose cell wall, a large central vacuole, and chloroplasts. Is it more consistent with a plant cell or an animal cell?

**Solution:**

1. A cellulose wall is characteristic of plant cells.
2. A large central vacuole is a typical plant-cell feature.
3. Chloroplasts indicate a photosynthetic eukaryotic cell and, together with the other clues, identify this as a plant cell.
4. The answer: plant cell.

### #3: Diffusion or Active Transport?

A cell brings in a nutrient that is already more concentrated inside than outside, using a membrane transporter and energy. Is this passive diffusion or active transport?

**Solution:**

1. The nutrient is moving against its concentration gradient.
2. The process requires an energy source.
3. That is active transport.

### #4: Hypertonic, Hypotonic, or Isotonic?

A red blood cell is placed in a salt solution and shrinks. What does that tell you about the solution's tonicity relative to the cell?

**Solution:**

1. Shrinking shows that water moved out of the cell.
2. The outside therefore had a higher effective concentration of nonpenetrating solute.
3. The external solution was hypertonic to the red blood cell.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For a typical plant-versus-animal cell question, the trio **cellulose wall + chloroplasts + large central vacuole** strongly indicates a plant cell. Do not turn that shortcut into the broader claim that no other organisms have walls, plastids, or vacuoles.

For osmosis questions, first identify which solutes can cross the membrane. In the simple textbook cases where the solute cannot cross, water moves toward the side with more effective solute.

For transport questions, ask whether movement is down or against the relevant gradient. Down-gradient transport can be passive; uphill transport requires an energy source.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Treating Cell Walls as Plant-Only Structures

> Plant cell walls contain cellulose, but cell walls also occur in fungi and most prokaryotes with different compositions.

The combination of several structures—not the mere presence of any wall—is what identifies a typical plant cell.

### #2: Mixing Up Hypertonic and Hypotonic

> Hypertonic outside: animal cells lose water. Hypotonic outside: animal cells gain water.

For plant cells, remember that the cell wall changes the outcome: water entry normally produces turgor instead of bursting the cell.

### #3: Saying Active Transport Always Uses ATP Directly

> Active transport always needs an energy source, but not every transporter hydrolyzes ATP itself.

Primary active transport can use ATP directly; secondary active transport can use an ion gradient established by another energy-consuming process.

### #4: Assuming Chloroplasts Occur Only in Plants

> Chloroplasts occur in plants and many algae; photosynthetic bacteria perform photosynthesis without chloroplasts.

Use chloroplasts as a plant-cell clue only within the typical plant-versus-animal comparison taught here.
