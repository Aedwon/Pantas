## Core Concept

Meiosis is the division that makes gametes in animals and spores in plants and fungi. It halves the number of chromosome sets. A haploid cell has $n$ chromosomes and a diploid cell has $2n$.

Meiosis can give each product a different mix of alleles.

This lesson needs the lesson Cellular Reproduction (Chromosomes, Cell Cycle, Mitosis). Read it first for chromosomes, sister chromatids, homologs, haploid and diploid, and for mitosis and cytokinesis.

### Words for Genes

A **gene** is a section of DNA that codes for a trait. An **allele** is one version of a gene.

For a gene on a non-sex chromosome, a diploid cell has two alleles, one on each homolog. The X and Y are the sex chromosomes. A male's X and Y carry mostly different genes, so he has one allele of most genes on the X.

The **genotype** is the alleles an organism has, written as letters, such as $Aa$. A pair of matching alleles, such as $BB$, is **homozygous**. A pair of different alleles, such as $Aa$, is **heterozygous**.

### Meiosis

**Meiosis** is a special nuclear division that halves the number of chromosome sets. A diploid cell makes haploid products. It has one round of DNA replication followed by two divisions, called meiosis I and meiosis II.

In animals, meiosis happens in **germ cells**, the cells in the ovaries and testes that make gametes. A **gamete** is a sex cell, such as a sperm or an egg.

In plants and fungi, meiosis often makes **spores** instead. A spore is a small cell that can grow into a new organism. So it is too narrow to say that meiosis always makes sperm and eggs.

**Meiosis I** separates the homologs.

- In prophase I, the homologs find each other and pair up side by side. **Crossing over** happens here: pieces of DNA are swapped between chromatids of the two homologs.
- In metaphase I, the homologous pairs line up at the middle, as pairs. Each pair faces the poles in a random way.
- In anaphase I, the homologs separate and move to opposite poles. The sister chromatids stay together, because the centromeres do not split.
- After cytokinesis, there are two cells. Each is haploid, with $n$ chromosomes. Each chromosome still has two sister chromatids.

The cell does not copy its DNA again between meiosis I and meiosis II.

**Meiosis II** separates the sister chromatids. It works like mitosis, but the cells start out haploid.

- In metaphase II, the chromosomes line up in single file.
- In anaphase II, the sister chromatids separate.
- After cytokinesis, there are four haploid cells in total. Each chromosome is now a single chromatid.

For a human germ cell, the numbers are:

- Before meiosis, after S phase: $46$ chromosomes and $92$ chromatids.
- After meiosis I: two cells, each with $23$ chromosomes and $46$ chromatids.
- After meiosis II: four cells, each with $23$ chromosomes and $23$ chromatids.

A typical meiosis gives four haploid cells. There are developmental exceptions.

In human **oogenesis**, the making of egg cells, the cytoplasm divides very unequally. One large egg cell is formed, along with one to three small cells called polar bodies. In human **spermatogenesis**, the making of sperm, one germ cell makes four sperm cells.

### How Meiosis Makes Variation

Meiosis can make gametes differ in their mix of alleles in two ways.

**Independent assortment.** At metaphase I, each homologous pair faces the poles in its own random way. This does not depend on how the other pairs face. So the maternal and paternal chromosomes (from the mother and from the father) are shuffled into the gametes.

One **arrangement** is the way all the pairs face the poles in one cell at metaphase I. A **combination** is the set of chromosomes that one gamete gets.

For $n$ pairs, a gamete can get $2^n$ different combinations. This counts whole chromosomes, so it ignores crossing over.

Without crossing over, a single meiosis uses one arrangement, so its four cells hold only two combinations. The other combinations come from meioses with other arrangements.

For humans, $n = 23$, so there are $2^{23} = 8{,}388{,}608$ combinations.

**Crossing over.** In prophase I, homologs swap pieces. This makes a chromosome that holds a mix of the mother's and father's alleles. It is new combinations on a single chromosome.

Independent assortment shuffles whole chromosomes. Crossing over shuffles pieces inside chromosomes.

**Fertilization** adds more variation. In fertilization, two haploid gametes fuse, and the cell they form is a **zygote**. Which sperm meets which egg is also a matter of chance.

### Why Meiosis Reduces the Number

In a life cycle with fertilization, two gametes fuse. Each brings one set of chromosomes.

If gametes were diploid, the number would double in every generation. Because meiosis halves the number, fertilization puts it back.

### Mitosis and Meiosis Compared

- **Replications of DNA**: mitosis has one. Meiosis has one.
- **Divisions**: mitosis has one. Meiosis has two.
- **Homologs pair up**: not in mitosis. Yes, in prophase I of meiosis.
- **What separates in anaphase**: in mitosis, sister chromatids. In meiosis, homologs in anaphase I and sister chromatids in anaphase II.
- **Products**: mitosis makes two cells with the parent's chromosome number. Meiosis usually makes four cells with half that number.
- **Genetic result**: mitosis makes cells that are almost identical to the parent cell. Meiosis makes cells that can differ from each other and from the parent, when the parent has heterozygous genes.

## Examples

### #1: Mitosis or Meiosis?

A skin cell lineage divides during repair, maintaining the same chromosome number in the daughter cells. Is this mitosis or meiosis?

**Solution:**

1. Repair and growth need new cells like the old ones. They do not need a reduced chromosome number.
2. The daughter nuclei keep the parent's chromosome number.
3. This is mitosis.

### #2: Counting Chromosomes After Human Meiosis

A diploid human germ cell with $46$ chromosomes completes meiosis. How many chromosomes does each final cell have, and how many chromatids does each of those chromosomes have?

**Solution:**

1. Human diploid cells have $46$ chromosomes, or $23$ homologous pairs.
2. Meiosis I separates the homologs, so each cell gets one chromosome from each pair. Meiosis II separates the sister chromatids, so no chromosome is lost.

$$\dfrac{46}{2} = 23$$

3. Each final cell has $23$ chromosomes.
4. After meiosis II, the sister chromatids have separated, so each chromosome has one chromatid.

### #3: Why Reduce the Chromosome Number?

Why does meiosis reduce the chromosome number before fertilization?

**Solution:**

1. In a diploid sexual life cycle, fertilization fuses two haploid gametes.
2. Each gamete brings one chromosome set.
3. The zygote is therefore diploid again. In humans, the sum is:

$$23 + 23 = 46$$

4. Without meiosis, the number would double with every generation.

### #4: Telling Metaphase of Mitosis, Meiosis I, and Meiosis II Apart

A diploid cell has $2n = 4$. Describe what the cell looks like at the middle of the cell in metaphase of mitosis, metaphase I, and metaphase II.

**Solution:**

1. Mitosis: all $4$ chromosomes line up in single file. Each has two chromatids.
2. Metaphase I: the chromosomes line up as $2$ homologous pairs. The pairs sit next to each other, with a homolog on each side of the middle.
3. Metaphase II: the cell is haploid, so there are only $2$ chromosomes. They line up in single file. Each has two chromatids.
4. Check by counting. Mitosis and metaphase I both have $4$ chromosomes, but only metaphase I has pairs. Metaphase II has half as many chromosomes as the others.

### #5: Counting Chromosome Combinations

A species has $2n = 6$. Ignoring crossing over, how many different combinations of maternal and paternal chromosomes can a gamete get?

**Solution:**

1. $2n = 6$ means there are $3$ homologous pairs.
2. For each pair, a gamete gets either the maternal or the paternal chromosome. That is $2$ choices.
3. The pairs sort independently, so the choices multiply.

$$2 \times 2 \times 2 = 2^3 = 8$$

4. A gamete can get $8$ different combinations.

### #6: Tracking Alleles on Different Chromosomes

A diploid cell has the genotype $AaBb$. The gene for $A$ is on one chromosome pair, and the gene for $B$ is on a different pair. Ignoring crossing over, which gametes can one meiosis produce, and which gametes can many such cells produce together?

**Solution:**

1. Each gamete is haploid. It gets one allele for each gene: $A$ or $a$, and $B$ or $b$.
2. The pairs sort independently, so each allele for the first gene can go with each allele for the second gene.
3. One meiosis has one arrangement of the two pairs. Its four cells are either $AB$, $AB$, $ab$, $ab$ or $Ab$, $Ab$, $aB$, $aB$, so it makes two combinations.
4. Other cells use the other arrangement. Together they can make all four combinations: $AB$, $Ab$, $aB$, and $ab$, in about equal numbers. This agrees with $2^2 = 4$.
5. This works only because the genes are on different pairs. Genes close together on the same chromosome tend to travel together, as the next example shows.
6. With crossing over between a gene and its centromere, one meiosis can make up to four combinations. They are still these same four.

### #7: Crossing Over and New Allele Combinations

Two genes are close together on the same chromosome. One homolog carries $A$ and $B$, and the other carries $a$ and $b$. What gamete types form with no crossing over, and what new ones can form with crossing over between the two genes?

**Solution:**

1. With no crossing over, each chromosome goes into a gamete as it is. The gametes are $AB$ and $ab$, two types.
2. Crossing over in prophase I swaps pieces between the two homologs, between the genes.
3. A chromatid can now carry $A$ with $b$, or $a$ with $B$.
4. Gametes $Ab$ and $aB$ can form too. These are new combinations that independent assortment could not make for genes this close together.

### #8: How Many Cells from One Germ Cell?

One human germ cell completes meiosis. How many functional gametes does it usually produce in spermatogenesis, and how many in oogenesis?

**Solution:**

1. Meiosis divides the nucleus into haploid nuclei. In spermatogenesis there are four.
2. In spermatogenesis, the cytoplasm divides equally. The result is $4$ sperm cells.
3. In oogenesis, the cytoplasm divides very unequally. Most of it goes to one cell.
4. The result is $1$ functional egg cell, plus one to three small polar bodies.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For a mitosis or meiosis question, look at the **chromosome number and chromosome behavior**, and not only at the number of daughter cells. Mitosis keeps the chromosome number, and meiosis halves it.

Mitosis gives two cells. In a haploid organism, those two cells are haploid.

Remember meiosis as **one copying, two divisions**. Homologs separate in meiosis I. Sister chromatids separate in meiosis II.

For variation, name two mechanisms: **crossing over** in prophase I and **independent assortment** at metaphase I.

$2^n$ counts chromosome combinations that many cells can make together, where $n$ is the number of pairs. If you count allele combinations instead, for genes on non-sex chromosomes that sit on different pairs, a homozygous gene adds no choice and each heterozygous gene doubles the number of choices. So $AaBB$ on two pairs has $4$ chromosome combinations but only $2$ allele combinations, $AB$ and $aB$.

A gene on a male's X is a special case: half his gametes carry the X, and half carry the Y.

For genes on different chromosome pairs, crossing over adds no new allele combinations. It matters for genes on the same chromosome, as in Example #7.

Without crossing over, one meiosis makes at most $2$ combinations.

To tell a metaphase, ask whether the chromosomes are in pairs. Pairs mean metaphase I.

In a diploid cell, single file with the full number of chromosomes means mitosis. Single file with half that number means metaphase II.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Saying Meiosis Always Makes Four Equal Gametes

> Meiosis usually makes four haploid cells, but gamete development can change the result.

Human spermatogenesis makes four sperm cells. Oogenesis normally makes one functional egg and one to three small polar bodies.

### #2: Confusing Homologs with Sister Chromatids

> Homologs separate in meiosis I. Sister chromatids separate in meiosis II.

Homologs are a pair from two parents. Sister chromatids are copies of one chromosome. This is the core reason meiosis halves the chromosome number.

### #3: Thinking the Cell Is Still Diploid After Meiosis I

> The number of chromosomes halves in meiosis I, not meiosis II.

After meiosis I, each cell is already haploid, because it holds one chromosome from each pair. Meiosis II only splits the chromatids, so the number of chromosomes stays $n$.
