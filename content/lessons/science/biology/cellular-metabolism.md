## Core Concept

Cells need energy for every job they do. They grow, move substances, repair damage and build new molecules. The energy comes in a form the cell can use at once: a molecule called **ATP** (adenosine triphosphate).

ATP has three phosphate groups (small units made of phosphorus and oxygen). When a cell breaks off the last phosphate, ATP becomes **ADP** (adenosine diphosphate) and releases energy for cell work. Other reactions add a phosphate back to ADP, so the same molecules are used again and again.

**Metabolism** means all the chemical reactions in a cell. This lesson covers enzymes, the main groups of biomolecules, and **photosynthesis**, which stores energy in sugar. The next lesson, Cellular Respiration and Fermentation, covers **cellular respiration**, which releases energy from sugar and other fuels.

### Words used in this lesson

An **organic molecule** is a carbon-based molecule that usually has carbon bonded to hydrogen, such as glucose ($C_6H_{12}O_6$, a sugar). Carbon dioxide and carbonates are carbon compounds, but they are not called organic.

An **organelle** is a small part inside a cell with its own job.

A **prokaryote** is a cell with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles. Bacteria are prokaryotes.

A **eukaryote** is a cell with a nucleus and organelles. Plants, animals, fungi and algae are eukaryotes.

An **electron** is a tiny charged particle. Moving electrons carry energy. An **electron carrier** is a molecule that picks up electrons in one place and drops them off in another.

### Enzymes: the helpers in many steps

An **enzyme** is a molecule that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up. Almost all enzymes are proteins. Each enzyme has an **active site**, a pocket that fits its own **substrate**, the molecule the enzyme works on. Some enzymes fit two or more substrates in the same reaction.

An enzyme works by making it easier for a reaction to start. It lowers the energy the reaction needs to get going. The reaction products leave, and the enzyme is ready for the next substrate.

Enzymes work best in a certain range of temperature and pH. The **pH** tells how acidic or basic a liquid is. Heat or a very wrong pH can change an enzyme's shape, so the active site no longer fits. This is called **denaturing**.

Many human enzymes work best near body temperature, about $37^\circ C$. Cold slows an enzyme but usually does not destroy its shape. Denaturing by heat is usually permanent.

Most steps in photosynthesis and respiration are each sped up by a specific enzyme. Some steps happen without one, such as chlorophyll absorbing light.

### Biomolecules: the fuels and the building blocks

Cells are built from four main groups of organic molecules, called **biomolecules**.

- **Carbohydrates** are sugars and the large carbohydrates built from them: starch and glycogen store sugar for fuel, and cellulose makes plant cell walls. Their small unit is a simple sugar such as glucose. Sugars are the usual fuel for cells.
- **Lipids** are a group that includes fats, oils, phospholipids and steroids. Fats and oils store a lot of energy. Phospholipids are the main molecules of cell membranes.
- **Proteins** are chains of amino acids, which are small building-block molecules. They form enzymes, build structures and carry out many jobs.
- **Nucleic acids** are DNA and RNA. They store and use genetic information.

Respiration usually burns glucose, but cells can also break down fats and proteins and feed the pieces into the same pathways. Fats give more energy per gram than carbohydrates do.

### Photosynthesis

**Photosynthesis** uses light energy to build sugar from carbon dioxide and water. In plants and algae it happens in **chloroplasts**. Cyanobacteria are prokaryotes that also carry out photosynthesis, without chloroplasts.

A simplified net equation for photosynthesis that releases oxygen is:

$$6CO_2 + 6H_2O + \text{light energy} \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2$$

The equation tracks the overall inputs and outputs. The real process has many steps. Not all photosynthetic bacteria release oxygen, but plants, algae and cyanobacteria do.

All of the oxygen gas comes from water, not from carbon dioxide. Check the atoms: $6O_2$ has $12$ oxygen atoms, but $6H_2O$ has only $6$. The full equation splits $12$ water molecules and makes $6$ new ones later, so the simplified equation shows the net change.

A chloroplast is wrapped in two membranes, an outer one and an inner one. Inside are flat sacs called **thylakoids**, stacked in piles. The fluid around them is the **stroma**.

Photosynthesis has two linked stages.

1. **Light reactions** happen in the thylakoid membranes of a chloroplast. The green pigment **chlorophyll** (a colored molecule) absorbs light, mostly red and blue light. The energy splits water, which releases oxygen gas. It also makes ATP and an electron carrier called **NADPH**.
2. **Calvin cycle** happens in the stroma of a chloroplast. It uses the ATP and NADPH to turn carbon dioxide into a three-carbon sugar. The cell then builds glucose and other molecules from this sugar. An enzyme called rubisco starts the step that attaches carbon dioxide.

The Calvin cycle does not use light directly. It still stops soon in the dark, because the light reactions stop making ATP and NADPH.

The places named above are for plants and algae. Cyanobacteria have no chloroplasts and no stroma, so they run both stages inside the cell.

## Examples

### #1: Matching Reactants and Products

Identify the missing product in this simplified equation and name the process:

$$6CO_2 + 6H_2O + \text{light energy} \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + \, ?$$

**Solution:**

1. The inputs are carbon dioxide, water and light, and one product is glucose. This is photosynthesis.
2. Count the oxygen atoms on the left: $6 \times 2 + 6 = 18$.
3. Glucose has $6$ oxygen atoms, so $12$ remain. They form $6O_2$.

The missing product is $6O_2$. The oxygen gas comes from water that is split in the light reactions, not from carbon dioxide.

### #2: Light Reactions and the Calvin Cycle

A plant in bright light is moved to darkness. After a short time, sugar making stops, even though the Calvin cycle does not use light directly. Explain why.

**Solution:**

1. The light reactions make ATP and NADPH in the thylakoid membranes.
2. The Calvin cycle in the stroma needs ATP and NADPH to turn carbon dioxide into sugar.
3. In darkness the light reactions stop.
4. The ATP and NADPH run out, so the Calvin cycle stops too.

### #3: Enzymes and Temperature

An enzyme in a human cell works fastest at $37^\circ C$. A sample is heated to $80^\circ C$ and then cooled back to $37^\circ C$. The enzyme no longer works. Explain.

**Solution:**

1. An enzyme works only when its active site fits its substrate.
2. High heat changes the enzyme's shape. This is denaturing.
3. The active site no longer fits the substrate.
4. Cooling the sample usually does not restore the shape, so the enzyme stays inactive.

If the sample had only been cooled to a low temperature, the enzyme would have slowed down. Its shape would not have been destroyed.

### #4: Biomolecules and Energy

Name the biomolecule group of each item: glucose, an enzyme, a fat, DNA. Which group do cells usually burn first for energy?

**Solution:**

1. Glucose is a carbohydrate.
2. Almost all enzymes are proteins.
3. A fat is a lipid.
4. DNA is a nucleic acid.
5. Cells usually use carbohydrates, mainly glucose, first. They can also break down fats and proteins when needed.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For the Calvin cycle, remember that it needs the ATP and NADPH from the light reactions. That is why it stops soon after the light goes out.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Assuming Photosynthesis Needs Chloroplasts in Every Organism

> Plants and algae use chloroplasts. Photosynthetic bacteria do not have them.

A chloroplast is an organelle of eukaryotes. Photosynthesis in cyanobacteria happens without one.

### #2: Saying the Oxygen Gas Comes From Carbon Dioxide

> The oxygen released in photosynthesis comes from water.

Carbon dioxide gives its carbon to sugar. The water is split in the light reactions, and its oxygen is released as $O_2$.

### #3: Thinking an Enzyme Is Used Up

> An enzyme speeds up a reaction and comes out unchanged.

The substrate is changed into products. The enzyme is ready to work again, unless heat or a wrong pH has denatured it.
