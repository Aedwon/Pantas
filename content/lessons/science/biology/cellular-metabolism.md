## Core Concept

Cells need energy for every job they do. They grow, move substances, repair damage and build new molecules. The energy comes in a form the cell can use at once: a molecule called **ATP** (adenosine triphosphate).

ATP has three phosphate groups (small units made of phosphorus and oxygen). When a cell breaks off the last phosphate, ATP becomes **ADP** (adenosine diphosphate) and releases energy for cell work. Other reactions add a phosphate back to ADP, so the same molecules are used again and again.

**Metabolism** means all the chemical reactions in a cell. This lesson covers the two big energy pathways: **photosynthesis**, which stores energy in sugar, and **cellular respiration**, which releases energy from sugar and other fuels.

### Words used in this lesson

An **organic molecule** is a carbon-based molecule made by living things, such as glucose ($C_6H_{12}O_6$, a sugar). A **prokaryote** is a cell with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles. Bacteria are prokaryotes. A **eukaryote** is a cell with a nucleus and organelles. Plants, animals, fungi and algae are eukaryotes.

An **organelle** is a small part inside a cell with its own job. The **cytosol** is the watery fluid in a cell, outside the organelles. **Catabolic** means breaking big molecules down into smaller ones. **Anabolic** means building small molecules up into bigger ones.

An **electron** is a tiny charged particle. Moving electrons carry energy. A molecule that **gains** electrons is **reduced**. A molecule that **loses** electrons is **oxidized**. An **electron carrier** is a molecule that picks up electrons in one place and drops them off in another.

### Enzymes: the helpers in every step

An **enzyme** is a molecule that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up. Almost all enzymes are proteins. Each enzyme has an **active site**, a pocket that fits one kind of **substrate**, the molecule the enzyme works on.

An enzyme works by making it easier for a reaction to start. It lowers the energy the reaction needs to get going. The reaction products leave, and the enzyme is ready for the next substrate.

Enzymes work best in a certain range of temperature and pH. Heat or a very wrong pH can change an enzyme's shape, so the active site no longer fits. This is called **denaturing**. Many human enzymes work best near body temperature, about $37^\circ C$.

Cold slows an enzyme but usually does not destroy its shape. Denaturing by heat is usually permanent. Every step of photosynthesis and respiration has its own enzyme.

### Biomolecules: the fuels and the building blocks

Cells are built from four main groups of organic molecules, called **biomolecules**.

- **Carbohydrates** are sugars and starches. Their small unit is a simple sugar such as glucose. They are the usual fuel for cells.
- **Lipids** are fats and oils. They store a lot of energy and form cell membranes.
- **Proteins** are chains of amino acids. They form enzymes, build structures and carry out many jobs.
- **Nucleic acids** are DNA and RNA. They store and use genetic information.

Respiration usually burns glucose, but cells can also break down fats and proteins and feed the pieces into the same pathways. Fats give more energy per gram than carbohydrates do.

### Photosynthesis

**Photosynthesis** uses light energy to build sugar from carbon dioxide and water. In plants and algae it happens in **chloroplasts**. Cyanobacteria are prokaryotes that also carry out photosynthesis, without chloroplasts.

A simplified net equation for photosynthesis that releases oxygen is:

$$6CO_2 + 6H_2O + \text{light energy} \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2$$

The equation tracks the overall inputs and outputs. The real process has many steps. Not all photosynthetic bacteria release oxygen, but plants, algae and cyanobacteria do.

All of the oxygen gas comes from water, not from carbon dioxide. Check the atoms: $6O_2$ has $12$ oxygen atoms, but $6H_2O$ has only $6$. The full equation splits $12$ water molecules and makes $6$ new ones later, so the simplified equation shows the net change.

A chloroplast has two outer membranes. Inside are flat sacs called **thylakoids**, stacked in piles. The fluid around them is the **stroma**.

Photosynthesis has two linked stages.

1. **Light reactions** happen in the thylakoid membranes. The green pigment **chlorophyll** absorbs light, mostly red and blue light. The energy splits water, which releases oxygen gas. It also makes ATP and an electron carrier called **NADPH**.
2. **Calvin cycle** happens in the stroma. It uses the ATP and NADPH to turn carbon dioxide into a three-carbon sugar. The cell then builds glucose and other molecules from this sugar. An enzyme called rubisco starts the step that attaches carbon dioxide.

The Calvin cycle does not use light directly. It still stops soon in the dark, because the light reactions stop making ATP and NADPH.

### Cellular Respiration

**Cellular respiration** is a set of catabolic pathways that move energy from organic molecules into ATP. Some of the energy is lost as heat.

In **aerobic respiration** (aerobic means with oxygen), oxygen is the final **electron acceptor**, the last molecule to receive electrons. A simplified overall equation is:

$$C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2 \rightarrow 6CO_2 + 6H_2O + \text{ATP and heat}$$

The carbon in the carbon dioxide comes from glucose. The oxygen gas ends up in water.

Aerobic respiration in a eukaryote has four stages:

1. **Glycolysis** (in the cytosol) splits one glucose, which has six carbons, into two **pyruvate** molecules, which have three carbons each. It gives a net gain of $2$ ATP and makes $2$ NADH. NADH is an electron carrier made when **NAD+** gains electrons. Glycolysis needs no oxygen.
2. **Pyruvate oxidation** (in the mitochondrial matrix) turns each pyruvate into a two-carbon unit called acetyl CoA. One carbon leaves each time as carbon dioxide. More NADH forms.
3. The **citric acid cycle** (in the matrix) breaks acetyl CoA down further. It releases the rest of the carbon as carbon dioxide and makes a little ATP. It also fills NADH and a second carrier, FADH$_2$, with electrons.
4. **Oxidative phosphorylation** (in the inner mitochondrial membrane) is where NADH and FADH$_2$ drop off their electrons. The electrons pass along a chain of proteins, the **electron transport chain**. The energy pumps hydrogen ions (H$^+$) across the membrane.

The hydrogen ions then flow back through an enzyme called **ATP synthase**, which makes most of the ATP. Think of water that builds up behind a dam and turns a turbine as it flows through. At the end of the chain, oxygen takes the electrons and combines with H$^+$ to form water.

A mitochondrion has an outer membrane and a folded inner membrane. The folds are called **cristae**. The space inside the inner membrane is the **matrix**.

One glucose gives only $2$ ATP in glycolysis and a few more in the citric acid cycle. Most ATP comes from oxidative phosphorylation. Current textbooks estimate about $30$ to $32$ ATP per glucose. Older books say $36$ to $38$. The real number varies with the cell type and how the cell moves NADH into the mitochondrion.

Prokaryotes have no mitochondria. They run the later stages of respiration in the cytosol and across the cell's plasma membrane. So the location split above belongs to eukaryotes.

### Respiration without oxygen

Some cells have no oxygen, or cannot use it. They still need ATP. There are two main ways to make it.

**Anaerobic respiration** is used by some prokaryotes. It has an electron transport chain, like aerobic respiration, but the final electron acceptor is not oxygen. It may be a nitrate or a sulfate compound. It usually makes less ATP than aerobic respiration, but more than fermentation.

**Fermentation** has no electron transport chain. Glycolysis alone makes the ATP, a net of $2$ per glucose. The problem is that glycolysis turns NAD+ into NADH, and the cell runs out of NAD+. Fermentation fixes this: NADH gives its electrons to pyruvate or to a molecule made from pyruvate. That turns NADH back into NAD+, so glycolysis can continue.

There are two common kinds:

- **Lactic acid fermentation** turns pyruvate into lactate (lactic acid). It happens in some bacteria and in animal muscle cells working hard. It releases no carbon dioxide.
- **Alcohol fermentation** turns pyruvate into ethanol and carbon dioxide. Yeast does this. The carbon dioxide makes bread dough rise.

Many cells, including yeast and human muscle, use aerobic respiration when oxygen is present and ferment when it is not. Some bacteria are **obligate anaerobes**, which means oxygen harms them.

### How the two processes connect

Photosynthesis and aerobic respiration have complementary overall inputs and outputs. They are not the same pathway run backward. They use different enzymes, steps, membranes and ways of making ATP.

Matter cycles: carbon, hydrogen and oxygen move between sugar, carbon dioxide, water and oxygen gas. Energy does not cycle. It enters mostly as light and leaves as heat.

Plants do both processes. Respiration goes on in all living plant cells day and night, including roots, which need oxygen from the soil. Photosynthesis happens only in cells with chloroplasts, and only when there is light. In bright light a leaf makes more sugar and oxygen than it uses. In the dark it takes in oxygen and gives off carbon dioxide, like an animal cell.

## Examples

### #1: Matching Reactants and Products

Identify the missing product in this simplified equation and name the process:

$$6CO_2 + 6H_2O + \text{light energy} \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + \, ?$$

**Solution:**

1. The inputs are carbon dioxide, water and light, and one product is glucose. This is photosynthesis.
2. Count the oxygen atoms on the left: $6 \times 2 + 6 = 18$.
3. Glucose has $6$ oxygen atoms, so $12$ remain. They form $6O_2$.

The missing product is $6O_2$. The oxygen gas comes from water that is split in the light reactions, not from carbon dioxide.

### #2: Where Does Each Process Happen?

In a plant cell, name the organelle where photosynthesis takes place. Then say where each stage of aerobic respiration takes place.

**Solution:**

1. Photosynthesis takes place in chloroplasts.
2. Glycolysis takes place in the cytosol, outside the mitochondrion.
3. Pyruvate oxidation and the citric acid cycle take place in the mitochondrial matrix.
4. The electron transport chain and ATP synthase are in the inner mitochondrial membrane.
5. So "chloroplast means photosynthesis" is a good rule. "All respiration happens in mitochondria" is too broad.

### #3: The Connection Between the Two Processes

Explain how photosynthesis and aerobic respiration are connected in an ecosystem.

**Solution:**

1. Photosynthesis builds glucose and releases oxygen.
2. Aerobic respiration uses glucose and oxygen. It releases carbon dioxide and water and moves energy into ATP.
3. The carbon dioxide and water can be used again in photosynthesis.
4. Matter cycles through both processes. Energy enters as light and leaves as heat.

### #4: Do Plants Perform Cellular Respiration Too?

A student says, "Plants only perform photosynthesis, and only animals perform cellular respiration." Explain the error.

**Solution:**

1. Plant cells need ATP for growth, moving substances, building molecules and repair.
2. Plants make this ATP by cellular respiration, as animals do.
3. Respiration is also done by fungi, bacteria and other organisms.
4. Photosynthesis and respiration are pathways. They are not labels that sort organisms into two groups.

### #5: A Leaf in Light and in Darkness

A sealed jar holds a green leaf. In bright light, the carbon dioxide in the jar goes down. In darkness, the carbon dioxide goes up. Explain both results.

**Solution:**

1. In bright light, photosynthesis in the leaf uses carbon dioxide faster than respiration releases it.
2. The carbon dioxide in the jar goes down.
3. In darkness, the light reactions stop, so there is no photosynthesis.
4. Respiration goes on, so the leaf releases carbon dioxide and the amount in the jar goes up.

At one light level between these two, photosynthesis and respiration run at equal rates. The carbon dioxide in the jar then does not change.

### #6: Light Reactions and the Calvin Cycle

A plant in bright light is moved to darkness. After a short time, sugar making stops, even though the Calvin cycle does not use light directly. Explain why.

**Solution:**

1. The light reactions make ATP and NADPH in the thylakoid membranes.
2. The Calvin cycle in the stroma needs ATP and NADPH to turn carbon dioxide into sugar.
3. In darkness the light reactions stop.
4. The ATP and NADPH run out, so the Calvin cycle stops too.

### #7: Following One Glucose Through Aerobic Respiration

In aerobic respiration of one glucose, at which stages is carbon dioxide released, and where is oxygen used?

**Solution:**

1. Glucose has six carbons. Glycolysis splits it into two pyruvate molecules of three carbons each. No carbon is lost here.
2. Pyruvate oxidation removes one carbon from each pyruvate as carbon dioxide. That gives $2$ carbon dioxide.
3. The citric acid cycle releases the remaining four carbons as $4$ carbon dioxide. The total is $6$.
4. Oxygen is used at the end of the electron transport chain. It accepts electrons and forms water.

Carbon dioxide is released in pyruvate oxidation and the citric acid cycle. Oxygen is used in oxidative phosphorylation.

### #8: Fermentation and Anaerobic Respiration

Yeast cells are sealed in a flask of sugar solution with no oxygen. Bubbles form. Name the process and its products. Explain why it keeps going and compare its ATP yield with aerobic respiration.

**Solution:**

1. Without oxygen, the yeast carries out alcohol fermentation. The bubbles are carbon dioxide. The other product is ethanol.
2. Glycolysis makes NADH from NAD+. Fermentation passes the electrons from NADH to a molecule made from pyruvate, so NAD+ forms again.
3. With NAD+ back, glycolysis can run again and keep making ATP.
4. Glycolysis alone nets $2$ ATP per glucose. Aerobic respiration gives about $30$ to $32$ ATP, so fermentation gives far less.

In a hard-working muscle cell short of oxygen, the same logic applies, but the product is lactate and no carbon dioxide forms. Anaerobic respiration is different: it still has an electron transport chain, with an acceptor other than oxygen.

### #9: Enzymes and Temperature

An enzyme in a human cell works fastest at $37^\circ C$. A sample is heated to $80^\circ C$ and then cooled back to $37^\circ C$. The enzyme no longer works. Explain.

**Solution:**

1. An enzyme works only when its active site fits its substrate.
2. High heat changes the enzyme's shape. This is denaturing.
3. The active site no longer fits the substrate.
4. Cooling the sample does not restore the shape, so the enzyme stays inactive.

If the sample had only been cooled to a low temperature, the enzyme would have slowed down. Its shape would not have been destroyed.

### #10: Biomolecules and Energy

Name the biomolecule group of each item: glucose, an enzyme, a fat, DNA. Which group do cells usually burn first for energy?

**Solution:**

1. Glucose is a carbohydrate.
2. An enzyme is a protein.
3. A fat is a lipid.
4. DNA is a nucleic acid.
5. Cells usually use carbohydrates, mainly glucose, first. They can also break down fats and proteins when needed.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Use the simplified equations to track overall matter. They are not one-step reactions, and they are not exact reverses of each other.

Remember the location rule for aerobic respiration in eukaryotes: **glycolysis in the cytosol, the later stages in the mitochondrion**. It does not hold for prokaryotes, which have no mitochondria.

If a question says oxygen is needed, it describes an **aerobic** pathway. Anaerobic respiration and fermentation make ATP without oxygen.

To tell the two oxygen-free pathways apart, ask whether there is an electron transport chain. Anaerobic respiration has one, with a final acceptor that is not oxygen. Fermentation has none.

For the Calvin cycle, remember that it needs the ATP and NADPH from the light reactions. That is why it stops soon after the light goes out.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Saying All Cellular Respiration Needs Oxygen

> Aerobic respiration uses oxygen, but anaerobic respiration and fermentation do not.

Some prokaryotes use other final electron acceptors. Fermentation lets cells keep glycolysis running without oxygen.

### #2: Saying Cellular Respiration Happens Entirely in Mitochondria

> Glycolysis happens in the cytosol of prokaryotes and eukaryotes alike.

In eukaryotes the mitochondria carry out the later stages of aerobic respiration. They do not carry out every step of glucose breakdown.

### #3: Treating Photosynthesis and Respiration as Exact Reverses

> The simplified equations look like mirror images, but the pathways are different.

They use different enzymes, steps and membranes. Only the overall inputs and outputs are complementary.

### #4: Assuming Photosynthesis Needs Chloroplasts in Every Organism

> Plants and algae use chloroplasts. Photosynthetic bacteria do not have them.

A chloroplast is an organelle of eukaryotes. Photosynthesis in cyanobacteria happens without one.

### #5: Saying the Oxygen Gas Comes From Carbon Dioxide

> The oxygen released in photosynthesis comes from water.

Carbon dioxide gives its carbon to sugar. The water is split in the light reactions, and its oxygen is released as $O_2$.

### #6: Saying Plants Take In Carbon Dioxide and Give Out Oxygen All the Time

> This is true only while photosynthesis is faster than respiration.

In the dark, or in cells without chloroplasts, a plant takes in oxygen and gives off carbon dioxide.

### #7: Thinking an Enzyme Is Used Up

> An enzyme speeds up a reaction and comes out unchanged.

The substrate is changed into products. The enzyme is ready to work again, unless heat or a wrong pH has denatured it.
