## Core Concept

**Botany** is the study of plants. This lesson covers flowering and other vascular plants. A **vascular plant** has tubes inside it that carry water and food.

The lesson has three parts: the tissues that make up a plant, the way plants grow toward or away from things, and the way flowering plants make seeds.

### Plant Tissue Types

A **tissue** is a group of similar cells that work together. A plant body has three tissue systems.

**Dermal tissue** is the outer covering of the plant. It protects the plant and limits water loss. Leaves also have tiny openings called **stomata** (one opening is a **stoma**). Gases such as carbon dioxide and oxygen pass through them.

**Vascular tissue** is the transport system. It has two parts: **xylem** and **phloem**.

**Xylem** carries water and dissolved minerals from the roots up through the plant. The pull comes mostly from **transpiration**, which is water evaporating from the leaves. So the main xylem stream moves toward the leaves.

**Phloem** carries sugars and other organic compounds, the substances made from carbon that the plant builds. A **source** is a place that makes sugar or releases it from storage, such as a mature leaf. A **sink** is a place that uses or stores sugar, such as a growing root, a young shoot, or a fruit.

Phloem moves sugar from sources to sinks. Because sources and sinks sit in different places, different phloem tubes can carry sugar upward or downward.

Xylem and phloem are made of different cells. The water-carrying xylem cells are dead when mature. They are hollow tubes.

All vascular plants have **tracheids**. Flowering plants also have wider tubes called **vessel elements**. Xylem also has living cells, called parenchyma cells.

The sugar-carrying cells of phloem, called **sieve elements**, are alive when mature. In flowering plants, each sieve element works beside a living **companion cell**. Conifers and ferns have a different kind of sieve element and different helper cells, not companion cells.

**Ground tissue** is everything else. It fills the space between the dermal tissue and the vascular tissue. Depending on the organ, it does photosynthesis (making sugar from light, water and carbon dioxide), stores food, or gives support.

### Tropisms

A **tropism** is growth of a plant part in a direction set by a stimulus. A **stimulus** is something in the surroundings that a plant can sense, such as light. Growth toward the stimulus is **positive**. Growth away from it is **negative**.

A tropism is slow growth. The part bends because cells on one side grow longer than cells on the other side. It is different from a quick movement.

- **Phototropism** is growth in response to light. Shoots commonly grow toward light, which is positive phototropism. Roots do not follow one rule here: their response to light differs among species and conditions.
- **Gravitropism** is growth in response to gravity. Roots typically grow down, toward gravity: positive. Shoots typically grow up, away from gravity: negative.
- **Thigmotropism** is growth in response to touch or contact. A climbing vine's tendril curls around a support it touches.

One plant can show more than one tropism at the same time. A shoot that grows up (negative gravitropism) can also bend toward a window (positive phototropism).

### Flowers and Their Parts

Flowering plants reproduce sexually. **Sexual reproduction** joins a male and a female **gamete**, a sex cell, into one new cell. Flowers are the parts where this happens. Each part below has a job.

The **anther** is the part that makes **pollen**. Pollen is a powdery grain. Each pollen grain holds the cells that will make sperm, the male gametes.

The anther sits on a thin stalk. Anther and stalk together are the **stamen**, the male part.

The female part is the **pistil**. It has three regions. The **stigma** is the sticky top that catches pollen. The **style** is the tube-like stalk below the stigma. The **ovary** is the swollen base.

Inside the ovary are one or more **ovules**. An ovule holds the female gamete, the **egg cell**. Many flowers also have petals, which can attract animals, and sepals, which protect the bud.

### Pollination, Fertilization, Seed and Fruit

Reproduction in a flowering plant is a chain of steps. Each step has its own name.

**Pollination** is the arrival of pollen on a stigma of the same species. The pollen can come from the same flower, another flower on the same plant, or a flower on a different plant. Wind, water, insects and other animals can carry it.

Pollination only moves pollen. It does not join any gametes.

After pollination, the pollen grain grows a thin tube, the **pollen tube**. The tube grows down through the style to an ovule. It carries two sperm cells.

**Fertilization** is the fusion of gametes. In flowering plants it happens twice, which is called **double fertilization**:

1. One sperm fuses with the egg cell. The result is a **zygote**, the first cell of a new plant. The zygote grows into the **embryo**, the young plant.
2. The other sperm fuses with a cell in the ovule that has two nuclei, called the central cell. In most flowering plants this makes **endosperm**, a tissue that feeds the embryo.

After fertilization, the ovule becomes the **seed**. The ovary usually becomes the **fruit**. Some fruits, such as apples, also use other flower parts.

So the order is: pollination, pollen tube growth, double fertilization, then seed and fruit. Fertilization needs successful pollination first, but pollination does not guarantee fertilization. For example, the pollen tube may stop growing before it reaches an ovule.

## Examples

### #1: Matching Tissue to Function

A gardener notices a plant's stem transporting water from its roots toward its leaves, while sugars produced in mature leaves are transported to growing roots and shoots. Name the tissues responsible.

**Solution:**
Match each material to the vascular tissue that carries it.

1. Water and dissolved minerals moving from the roots through the plant are carried by xylem.
2. Sugars moving from a source, a mature leaf, to sinks, growing roots and shoots, are carried by phloem.
3. Both are parts of the vascular tissue system.

### #2: Sugar Moving in Different Directions

In a flowering plant, a mature leaf makes sugar. A young fruit grows on a branch above that leaf, and the roots are below it. Which directions can phloem carry sugar from the mature leaf?

**Solution:**
Phloem follows sources and sinks, not up or down.

1. The mature leaf is the source.
2. The young fruit and the roots are both sinks.
3. The fruit is above the leaf and the roots are below it. So sugar can move up to the fruit and down to the roots, in different phloem tubes.

### #3: Identifying a Tropism

A seedling is planted sideways in a pot and left in the dark. After several days, its root curves downward and its shoot curves upward. What tropism explains this, and is it positive or negative for each part?

**Solution:**
Identify the stimulus and the direction of growth.

1. The stimulus is gravity, so this is gravitropism.
2. The root grows in the direction of gravity: positive gravitropism.
3. The shoot grows against the direction of gravity: negative gravitropism.
4. The pot is in the dark, so light cannot explain the curving.

### #4: Light and Touch Together

A young pea plant is kept near a window. Its shoot bends toward the glass. Its tendrils wrap around a stick. Name the two tropisms.

**Solution:**
Take each observation by itself.

1. The shoot bends toward light, so it shows positive phototropism.
2. The tendrils respond to contact with the stick, so they show thigmotropism.
3. These are two separate stimuli acting on the same plant.

### #5: Naming Flower Parts

A student looks at a flower. She sees a sticky tip that holds pollen, and she sees that pollen forms at the top of thin stalks. Which part catches pollen, and which part makes it?

**Solution:**
Match each job to the part.

1. The stigma is the sticky top of the pistil, so it catches pollen.
2. The anther is on the stalk and makes the pollen.
3. The anther is male, and the stigma is part of the female pistil.

### #6: Pollination vs. Fertilization

A bee carries pollen from one flower and leaves it on the stigma of another flower of the same species. Later, a sperm cell delivered through the pollen tube joins with an egg cell to form a zygote. Which event is pollination and which is fertilization?

**Solution:**
Separate the transfer step from the gamete-fusion step.

1. Leaving pollen on the stigma is pollination.
2. The sperm joining the egg is fertilization.
3. Pollination comes first, then the pollen tube grows, then fertilization.

### #7: Tracing Double Fertilization

A pollen tube has reached an ovule inside an ovary. It releases two sperm cells. What does each sperm form, and what do the ovule and ovary become?

**Solution:**
Follow each sperm and then the flower parts.

1. The first sperm fuses with the egg. This forms a zygote, which grows into the embryo.
2. The second sperm fuses with the central cell. In most flowering plants this forms endosperm, which feeds the embryo.
3. The ovule becomes the seed.
4. The ovary usually becomes the fruit.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For transport, start with the material. Water and minerals go with xylem. Sugars and other organic compounds go with phloem. For phloem, find the source and the sink, then follow the sugar from one to the other.

Do not use "phloem goes down" as a rule, because it fails when the sink is above the source.


For tropisms, name the stimulus first, then decide whether the part grows toward it (positive) or away (negative). Roots are typically positive for gravity and shoots negative.

Shoots commonly grow toward light, but do not use a fixed rule for root light responses.

For reproduction, keep the two names apart. Pollination is pollen transfer. Fertilization is gamete fusion.

For flower parts, follow the pollen and then the tube. Pollen forms in the anther and lands on the stigma. Then the pollen tube grows down the style to an ovule in the ovary, and the sperm travel inside the tube.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Calling All Xylem Cells Dead

> The main water-carrying cells of xylem are dead when mature, but xylem tissue also holds living cells.

Tracheids and vessel elements are the water-carrying pathways (vessel elements are found in flowering plants). Saying "all xylem cells are dead" is too broad.

### #2: Assuming Phloem Only Moves Sugar Downward

> Phloem moves materials from sources to sinks, not by a fixed up or down rule.

A mature leaf may send sugar to roots below it or to a growing shoot above it. The direction depends on where the source and sink are.

### #3: Treating Pollination and Fertilization as the Same Event

> Pollination puts pollen on the stigma. Fertilization is the later fusion of gametes.

A question that describes only pollen landing on a stigma describes pollination, not fertilization.

### #4: Treating Pollen as Sperm

> A pollen grain is not a sperm cell. It holds the cells that make sperm.

The sperm cells travel down the pollen tube. Pollen is what lands on the stigma.
