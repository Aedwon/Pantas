## Core Concept

**Taxonomy** is the work of naming, describing, and sorting living things into groups. **Systematics** is the wider study of how living things are related by ancestry. A modern classification is a hypothesis, which means a best explanation that new evidence can change.

Two kinds of evidence are used. **Morphology** means the form and structure of the body. **Molecular data** means DNA and protein sequences, which scientists compare between organisms.

### The Traditional Rank Hierarchy

A **rank** is a level in the classification. A group of organisms at any rank is called a **taxon** (plural: taxa). The traditional ranks run from the broadest group to the narrowest:

**Domain → Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species**

Each rank sits inside the one above it. A genus holds one or more species. A family holds one or more genera (the plural of genus). The ranks are nested like boxes inside boxes.

These $8$ ranks are a classroom simplification. Scientists also use ranks such as subphylum, subclass, and subspecies. Many groups are also described without any rank at all.

For example, the lion is classified like this:

**Eukarya → Animalia → Chordata → Mammalia → Carnivora → Felidae → *Panthera* → *Panthera leo***

### Domains and Kingdoms

The **three-domain system** has $3$ domains: Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya. Bacteria and Archaea are **prokaryotes**. Their cells have no nucleus, which is a membrane-bound compartment that holds the DNA.

Eukarya are **eukaryotes**. Their cells have a nucleus and other membrane-bound parts. A few specialized cells lose the nucleus later, such as mature human red blood cells, but the organism's other cells keep it.

Kingdoms are the next rank down, and textbooks do not all agree on them. Some books use $5$ kingdoms, with one kingdom called Monera for all prokaryotes. Some use $6$ kingdoms, which split the prokaryotes into two. This lesson uses the three domains and, inside Eukarya, the $4$ traditional kingdoms: Animalia, Plantae, Fungi, and Protista. If your textbook differs, learn its scheme too.

Here are the usual traits of the $4$ eukaryote kingdoms:

- **Animalia:** many cells, no cell walls, and the organism takes in food by eating.
- **Plantae:** many cells, cell walls made of cellulose, and the organism makes its own food by photosynthesis.
- **Fungi:** cell walls made of chitin, and the organism absorbs food from its surroundings. Most are many-celled, but yeasts have one cell.
- **Protista:** a mixed group of eukaryotes that are not animals, plants, or fungi. Most have one cell, but some, such as kelp, have many.

These are usual traits, not perfect rules. A dodder is a plant that has lost most of its ability to photosynthesize. A yeast is a fungus with only one cell. So decide with several traits together, never one trait alone.

### Classifying an Unfamiliar Organism

To classify an organism, list its traits, then move from the broad rank to the narrow rank. First ask if the cells have a nucleus. Then ask about the number of cells, the cell wall, and how it gets food.

A **dichotomous key** is a tool that does this in steps. Each step gives two choices. You pick the one that fits, and it sends you to the next step or to a name. A key only works for the group of organisms it was written for.

### Evolutionary Relationships

Two organisms share a **common ancestor** when both descend from the same earlier organism. A **lineage** is one line of descent through time.

A **clade** is a group made of one ancestor and all of its descendants. A clade is also called a **monophyletic** group. A group that leaves out some descendants of its ancestor, or that mixes in organisms from other lineages, is not monophyletic.

Scientists show ancestry in a branching tree. Each branch point stands for a common ancestor. Two groups that meet at a branch point more recently are more closely related than two groups that meet further back.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: a branching tree with four tips labeled A, B, C, D from left to right; A and B join at node X, C joins the A-B branch at node Y, D joins at the root node Z; nodes X, Y, Z are labeled -->

Similar looks do not always mean close ancestry. In **convergent evolution**, unrelated lineages end up with similar traits because they live in similar ways. The wings of a bat and a bird are an example. Both fly, but the two lineages gained wings separately.

This is why a classification can change. If molecular data show that a group is not one clade, taxonomists may split it, merge it, or redefine it. The old catch-all Kingdom Protista is an example. Its members do not all come from one ancestor, so "protist" is now only an informal word for eukaryotes that are not animals, land plants, or fungi.

### Binomial Nomenclature

**Binomial nomenclature** means a two-part scientific name for a species. The first part is the **genus**, written with a capital letter. The second part is the **specific epithet**, written in lowercase.

Together they form the species name. In *Homo sapiens*, *Homo* is the genus and *sapiens* is the specific epithet. The species name is both words together.

Type the name in italics. When you write it by hand, underline it. After the full name has been given once, the genus may be shortened to its first letter, as in *H. sapiens*.

The epithet alone does not name a species, because the same epithet can appear in different genera. *Escherichia coli* is a bacterium. *Entamoeba coli* is a different organism, a protist. A genus name can also be used alone, such as *Panthera*, to mean the whole genus.

Naming follows international codes of nomenclature. Animals, plants with algae and fungi, and bacteria each have their own code. Names can change when new evidence shows a species belongs in a different genus.

Carl Linnaeus lived in the eighteenth century. He did not invent every idea behind two-part names. His work standardized and spread the system and the nested ranks.

## Mnemonics & Memory Aids

A well-known sentence for the rank order is "Dear King Philip Came Over For Good Soup." It stands for Domain, Kingdom, Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus, Species.

## Examples

### #1: Ordering the Traditional Ranks

Arrange Family, Kingdom, Species, Phylum, Domain, Genus, Class, and Order from broadest to most specific in the traditional hierarchy.

**Solution:**

1. The broadest rank is the Domain, and the next is the Kingdom.
2. Then come Phylum, Class, Order, and Family.
3. The narrowest ranks are Genus and then Species.

The order is Domain, Kingdom, Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus, Species.

### #2: Reading Nested Ranks

The lion is *Panthera leo*, the tiger is *Panthera tigris*, and the domestic cat is *Felis catus*. All three belong to the family Felidae. Which pair shares the narrowest rank, and what is that rank?

**Solution:**

1. All three share the family Felidae, so family is not the answer.
2. The lion and the tiger both have the genus *Panthera*.
3. The cat has the genus *Felis*, so it shares the genus with neither.

The lion and the tiger share the genus. That is a narrower rank than the family.

### #3: Identifying Parts of a Scientific Name

In *Panthera leo*, identify the genus, the specific epithet, and the full species name.

**Solution:**

1. The genus is *Panthera*, the first word with a capital letter.
2. The specific epithet is *leo*, the second word in lowercase.
3. The species name is both words: *Panthera leo*.

Calling *leo* alone "the species name" is wrong. *Leo* does not identify the species without its genus.

### #4: Why Scientific Names?

Why are standardized scientific names better than common names in research?

**Solution:**

1. Common names change between languages and regions. One common name can also point to more than one organism.
2. A scientific name is set by an international code, so one species has one accepted name.
3. Scientists can look up an older name in the literature and find the current one.

Scientific names reduce confusion between researchers in different places.

### #5: Placing an Organism from Its Traits

An organism grows on a fallen log. Its cells have a nucleus. It is made of many cells with cell walls of chitin. It has no chlorophyll and absorbs food from the log. Which domain and kingdom does it belong to?

**Solution:**

1. The cells have a nucleus, so the domain is Eukarya.
2. Many cells and no chlorophyll rule out Plantae, which makes its own food.
3. Cell walls of chitin and absorbing food match Fungi.
4. An animal has no cell walls and eats food, so it does not fit.

The organism is in domain Eukarya and kingdom Fungi.

### #6: When One Trait Misleads

Baker's yeast has a nucleus, one cell, and a cell wall of chitin. It absorbs sugars from its surroundings. A student says it must be a protist because it has one cell. Is the student right?

**Solution:**

1. Having one cell is usual for protists, but it does not decide the kingdom.
2. A cell wall of chitin and absorbing food are traits of Fungi.
3. Two traits point to Fungi and only one points to Protista.

Yeast is a fungus. One trait alone can mislead, so use several traits together.

### #7: Using a Dichotomous Key

Use this key written for a bacterium, a mango tree, a mushroom, and a goat.

1. Cells have a nucleus: go to step 2. Cells have no nucleus: Bacteria.
2. Makes its own food by photosynthesis: Plantae. Does not: go to step 3.
3. Cell walls of chitin and absorbs food: Fungi. No cell walls and eats food: Animalia.

Where does the mushroom go? Would kelp, a many-celled organism that photosynthesizes, be placed correctly?

**Solution:**

1. The mushroom has a nucleus, so go to step 2.
2. It does not photosynthesize, so go to step 3.
3. It has chitin walls and absorbs food, so it is Fungi.
4. Kelp has a nucleus and photosynthesizes, so this key stops at Plantae.

Traditionally kelp is placed among the protists, so the key gives the wrong answer for it. A key is only correct for the organisms it was written for.

### #8: Reading a Branching Tree

In a tree, A and B meet at node X. That group meets C at node Y, which is further back in time. D meets all of them at node Z, which is the furthest back. Which organism is most closely related to A, and which is the most distant?

**Solution:**

1. A and B meet at node X, the most recent branch point. B is the closest relative of A.
2. C joins at node Y, which is further back, so C is more distant than B.
3. D joins at node Z, the furthest back, so D is the most distant.

B is closest to A, and D is the most distant. The order of branching tells relatedness.

### #9: Why Can Classification Change?

A molecular study finds that some organisms, grouped together because they look alike, do not all come from one ancestor. Why might taxonomists revise the group?

**Solution:**

1. Similar looks can come from convergent evolution, not from shared ancestry.
2. Modern systematics tries to show ancestry, so it prefers groups that are clades.
3. The study shows the old group is not a clade.
4. Taxonomists may split the group, merge it with others, or redefine it.

The old Kingdom Protista changed in this way.

## Exam Strategies

Match the question to the type. If it gives traits, check the nucleus first, then cell number, cell wall, and food source. If it gives names, look for the capital genus and the lowercase epithet.

For relatedness, think of the **more recent common ancestor**, not the number of shared rank labels. Ranks are useful groups, but they are not a ruler of evolutionary distance. Two species in the same genus are usually closer relatives than two species that share only a family, but only the tree shows how close.

Always check which kingdom scheme the question uses. If it names a kingdom list, such as Monera, use that list.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Calling the Epithet the Whole Species Name

> In *Homo sapiens*, *sapiens* is the specific epithet, and *Homo sapiens* is the species name.

The second word alone does not identify a species. It needs its genus.

### #2: Treating Protista as a Settled Kingdom

> "Protist" is a useful informal word, but Kingdom Protista is not one clade.

The Protista are many different lineages. Classification changes as evidence improves.

### #3: Saying Linnaeus Created Binomial Naming

> Linnaeus standardized and spread the system. He did not invent every idea in it.

Scientific naming grew over time. International codes now govern it.

### #4: Treating Rank Count as Evolutionary Distance

> Sharing a genus usually means closer ancestry than sharing only a family, but ranks are not equal steps.

Ranks are labels, not units of time or DNA difference. Different groups use ranks differently, so only the tree shows how close two lineages are.

### #5: Classifying by One Trait

> One trait can point to the wrong kingdom.

Yeast has one cell but is a fungus. Dodder is a plant that barely photosynthesizes. Use several traits together.

### #6: Assuming All Textbooks Use the Same Kingdoms

> Some books use $5$ kingdoms, some use $6$, and some use domains first.

Always read which scheme the question uses before you answer.
