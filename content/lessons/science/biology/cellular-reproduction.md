## Core Concept

### Chromosomes, Chromatids, and Sets

A **eukaryotic cell** is a cell with a nucleus. The nucleus holds the cell's DNA. DNA is packaged into **chromosomes**, and each chromosome carries many **genes**.

A gene is a stretch of DNA that carries instructions for a trait. Different versions of the same gene are called **alleles**.

In most animals, a body cell holds chromosomes in pairs. Each pair is a **homologous pair**.

The two chromosomes in the pair are called **homologs**. One came from the mother and one from the father.

Homologs carry the same genes in the same order. They are not copies of each other. At a given gene they may carry the same allele or different alleles.

The sex chromosomes are the exception. In human males, X and Y pair up in meiosis, but they are not fully homologous. They share only a small matching region and carry mostly different genes.

A cell with two of each kind of chromosome is **diploid**. We write this as $2n$. A cell with one of each kind is **haploid**, written $n$. In humans, $n = 23$ and $2n = 46$.

A haploid cell does not hold a random half of the chromosomes. It holds exactly one chromosome of each kind, so one from every homologous pair.

Before a cell divides, it copies its DNA. This copying is called **replication**.

Each chromosome becomes two identical DNA copies that stay stuck together, joined at a spot called the **centromere**. Each copy is a **sister chromatid**.

Two sister chromatids joined at one centromere still count as one chromosome. To count chromosomes, count centromeres. When the sister chromatids pull apart, each one is then counted as its own chromosome.

Keep these two pairs of words apart:

- **Sister chromatids** are the two copies of one chromosome, made by replication.
- **Homologs** are the two chromosomes of a pair, one from each parent.

Sister chromatids are identical copies, apart from rare copying errors. One exception is meiosis, where crossing over can swap pieces between a chromatid and a chromatid of its homolog. After that swap, sister chromatids are no longer identical.

### The Cell Cycle

The **cell cycle** is the ordered series of steps through which many eukaryotic cells grow, copy their DNA, and divide. Not every cell is always cycling. Many specialized cells rest in a non-dividing state called $G_0$.

Most of the cycle of a dividing cell is **interphase**. It has three parts.

- $G_1$: the cell grows and does its normal work. Each chromosome is a single DNA molecule.
- **S phase**: the DNA is replicated. Each chromosome now has two sister chromatids.
- $G_2$: the cell keeps growing and gets ready to divide.

After interphase comes the dividing stage. It has two jobs: **mitosis** (or meiosis), which divides the nucleus, and **cytokinesis**, which divides the cytoplasm. The cytoplasm is everything in the cell outside the nucleus.

### Mitosis

**Mitosis** divides the nucleus so that each new nucleus gets the same chromosomes as the parent nucleus. The cell has copied its DNA once and divides once. Cytokinesis usually follows, and the result is two **daughter cells**.

In ordinary growth and tissue repair, the two daughter cells are genetically very close to the parent cell. Only mutations or chromosome-sorting errors make them differ. A **mutation** is a change in DNA.

Mitosis keeps the chromosome number the same. It does not matter whether the parent cell is diploid or haploid. A diploid cell makes two diploid cells, and a haploid cell makes two haploid cells.

The main phases are:

- **Prophase and prometaphase**: the chromosomes coil up and become short and thick. The **spindle** forms. The spindle is a set of fibers made of microtubules that move chromosomes. The nuclear envelope, the membrane around the nucleus, breaks down, and spindle fibers attach to the centromeres.
- **Metaphase**: the chromosomes line up in single file across the middle of the cell, called the metaphase plate. The sister chromatids of each chromosome face opposite poles of the cell.
- **Anaphase**: the sister chromatids separate and move toward opposite poles. Each separated chromatid is now a chromosome.
- **Telophase**: the chromosomes reach the poles and new nuclear envelopes form around them.

**Cytokinesis** overlaps with late mitosis, but it is a separate process. In animal cells, the membrane pinches inward in the middle and splits the cell in two. In plant cells, a new cell wall called the cell plate forms across the middle.

Here is how the numbers change for a human body cell, with $2n = 46$:

- $G_1$: $46$ chromosomes, each with one DNA molecule.
- After S phase, through metaphase: still $46$ chromosomes, but now $92$ chromatids.
- Anaphase and telophase: the chromatids separate, so the cell holds $92$ chromosomes for a short time, in one cell.
- After cytokinesis: each daughter cell has $46$ chromosomes.

### Meiosis

**Meiosis** is a special nuclear division that halves the number of chromosome sets. A diploid cell makes haploid products. It has one round of DNA replication followed by two divisions, called meiosis I and meiosis II.

In animals, meiosis happens in **germ cells**, the cells in the ovaries and testes that make gametes. A **gamete** is a sex cell, such as a sperm or an egg.

In plants and fungi, meiosis often makes **spores** instead. A spore is a small cell that can grow into a new organism. So it is too narrow to say that meiosis always makes sperm and eggs.

**Meiosis I** separates the homologs.

- In prophase I, the homologs find each other and pair up side by side. **Crossing over** happens here: pieces of DNA are swapped between chromatids of the two homologs.
- In metaphase I, the homologous pairs line up at the middle, as pairs. Each pair faces the poles in a random way.
- In anaphase I, the homologs separate and move to opposite poles. The sister chromatids stay together, because the centromeres do not split.
- After cytokinesis, there are two cells. Each is haploid, with $n$ chromosomes. Each chromosome still has two sister chromatids.

The cell does not copy its DNA again between meiosis I and meiosis II.

**Meiosis II** separates the sister chromatids. It works like mitosis, but the cells start out haploid.

- In metaphase II, the chromosomes line up in single file.
- In anaphase II, the sister chromatids separate.
- After cytokinesis, there are four haploid cells in total. Each chromosome is now a single chromatid.

For a human germ cell, the numbers are:

- Before meiosis, after S phase: $46$ chromosomes and $92$ chromatids.
- After meiosis I: two cells, each with $23$ chromosomes and $46$ chromatids.
- After meiosis II: four cells, each with $23$ chromosomes and $23$ chromatids.

A typical meiosis gives four haploid cells. There are developmental exceptions.

In human **oogenesis**, the making of egg cells, the cytoplasm divides very unequally. One large egg cell is formed, along with one to three small cells called polar bodies. In human **spermatogenesis**, the making of sperm, one germ cell makes four sperm cells.

### How Meiosis Makes Variation

Meiosis gives each gamete a different mix of alleles in two ways.

**Independent assortment.** At metaphase I, each homologous pair faces the poles in its own random way. This does not depend on how the other pairs face. So the maternal and paternal chromosomes are shuffled into the gametes.

For $n$ pairs, there are $2^n$ ways to sort them. This count does not include crossing over.

For humans, $n = 23$, so there are $2^{23} = 8{,}388{,}608$ ways.

**Crossing over.** In prophase I, homologs swap pieces. This makes a chromosome that holds a mix of the mother's and father's alleles. It is new combinations on a single chromosome.

Independent assortment shuffles whole chromosomes. Crossing over shuffles pieces inside chromosomes.

**Fertilization** adds more variation. In fertilization, two haploid gametes fuse, and the cell they form is a **zygote**. Which sperm meets which egg is also a matter of chance.

### Why Meiosis Reduces the Number

In a life cycle with fertilization, two gametes fuse. Each brings one set of chromosomes.

If gametes were diploid, the number would double in every generation. Because meiosis halves the number, fertilization puts it back.

### Mitosis and Meiosis Compared

- **Replications of DNA**: mitosis has one. Meiosis has one.
- **Divisions**: mitosis has one. Meiosis has two.
- **Homologs pair up**: not in mitosis. Yes, in prophase I of meiosis.
- **What separates in anaphase**: in mitosis, sister chromatids. In meiosis, homologs in anaphase I and sister chromatids in anaphase II.
- **Products**: mitosis makes two cells with the parent's chromosome number. Meiosis usually makes four cells with half that number.
- **Genetic result**: mitosis makes cells that are almost identical to the parent cell. Meiosis makes cells that differ from each other and from the parent.

## Examples

### #1: Mitosis or Meiosis?

A skin cell lineage divides during repair, maintaining the same chromosome number in the daughter cells. Is this mitosis or meiosis?

**Solution:**

1. Repair and growth need new cells like the old ones. They do not need a reduced chromosome number.
2. The daughter nuclei keep the parent's chromosome number.
3. This is mitosis.

### #2: Counting Chromosomes and Chromatids in Mitosis

A human skin cell in $G_1$ has $46$ chromosomes. How many chromosomes and how many chromatids does it have at metaphase? How many chromosomes does one daughter cell have at the end?

**Solution:**

1. In S phase, each of the $46$ chromosomes is copied. The copies stay joined at the centromere.
2. Centromeres do not split until anaphase. So at metaphase there are still $46$ chromosomes.
3. Each chromosome has two chromatids.

$$46 \times 2 = 92 \text{ chromatids}$$

4. In anaphase the chromatids separate. The cell then divides, and the $92$ chromosomes share out equally between the two daughter cells.

$$\dfrac{92}{2} = 46$$

5. At metaphase: $46$ chromosomes and $92$ chromatids. Each daughter cell has $46$ chromosomes.

### #3: Counting Chromosomes After Human Meiosis

A diploid human germ cell with $46$ chromosomes completes meiosis. How many chromosomes does each final cell have, and how many chromatids does each of those chromosomes have?

**Solution:**

1. Human diploid cells have $46$ chromosomes, or $23$ homologous pairs.
2. Meiosis I separates the homologs, so each cell gets one chromosome from each pair. Meiosis II separates the sister chromatids, so no chromosome is lost.

$$\dfrac{46}{2} = 23$$

3. Each final cell has $23$ chromosomes.
4. After meiosis II, the sister chromatids have separated, so each chromosome has one chromatid.

### #4: Why Reduce the Chromosome Number?

Why does meiosis reduce the chromosome number before fertilization?

**Solution:**

1. In a diploid sexual life cycle, fertilization fuses two haploid gametes.
2. Each gamete brings one chromosome set.
3. The zygote is therefore diploid again. In humans, the sum is:

$$23 + 23 = 46$$

4. Without meiosis, the number would double with every generation.

### #5: Identifying a Mitotic Phase

A cell is observed with duplicated chromosomes lined up in single file at the middle of the spindle. The sister chromatids of each chromosome are attached to opposite poles. Which phase is this?

**Solution:**

1. Chromosomes lined up at the middle is the clue for metaphase.
2. Sister chromatids face opposite poles, ready to separate in anaphase.
3. The phase is **metaphase**.

### #6: Telling Metaphase of Mitosis, Meiosis I, and Meiosis II Apart

A diploid cell has $2n = 4$. Describe what the cell looks like at the middle of the cell in metaphase of mitosis, metaphase I, and metaphase II.

**Solution:**

1. Mitosis: all $4$ chromosomes line up in single file. Each has two chromatids.
2. Metaphase I: the chromosomes line up as $2$ homologous pairs. The pairs sit next to each other, with a homolog on each side of the middle.
3. Metaphase II: the cell is haploid, so there are only $2$ chromosomes. They line up in single file. Each has two chromatids.
4. Check by counting. Mitosis and metaphase I both have $4$ chromosomes, but only metaphase I has pairs. Metaphase II has half as many chromosomes as the others.

### #7: Counting Chromosome Combinations

A species has $2n = 6$. Ignoring crossing over, how many different combinations of maternal and paternal chromosomes can a gamete get?

**Solution:**

1. $2n = 6$ means there are $3$ homologous pairs.
2. For each pair, a gamete gets either the maternal or the paternal chromosome. That is $2$ choices.
3. The pairs sort independently, so the choices multiply.

$$2 \times 2 \times 2 = 2^3 = 8$$

4. A gamete can get $8$ different combinations.

### #8: Tracking Alleles on Different Chromosomes

A diploid cell has the genotype $AaBb$. The gene for $A$ is on one chromosome pair, and the gene for $B$ is on a different pair. Ignoring crossing over, which gametes can meiosis produce?

**Solution:**

1. Each gamete is haploid. It gets one allele for each gene: $A$ or $a$, and $B$ or $b$.
2. The pairs sort independently, so each allele for the first gene can go with each allele for the second gene.
3. The four gametes are $AB$, $Ab$, $aB$, and $ab$.
4. This works only because the genes are on different pairs. Genes close together on the same chromosome tend to travel together, as the next example shows.

### #9: Crossing Over and New Allele Combinations

Two genes are close together on the same chromosome. One homolog carries $A$ and $B$, and the other carries $a$ and $b$. What gametes form with no crossing over, and what new ones can form with crossing over between the two genes?

**Solution:**

1. With no crossing over, each chromosome goes into a gamete as it is. The gametes are $AB$ and $ab$.
2. Crossing over in prophase I swaps pieces between the two homologs, between the genes.
3. A chromatid can now carry $A$ with $b$, or $a$ with $B$.
4. Gametes $Ab$ and $aB$ can form too. These are new combinations that independent assortment could not make for genes this close together.

### #10: How Many Cells from One Germ Cell?

One human germ cell completes meiosis. How many functional gametes does it usually produce in spermatogenesis, and how many in oogenesis?

**Solution:**

1. Meiosis divides the nucleus into haploid nuclei. In spermatogenesis there are four.
2. In spermatogenesis, the cytoplasm divides equally. The result is $4$ sperm cells.
3. In oogenesis, the cytoplasm divides very unequally. Most of it goes to one cell.
4. The result is $1$ functional egg cell, plus one to three small polar bodies.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For a mitosis or meiosis question, look at the **chromosome number and chromosome behavior**, and not only at the number of daughter cells. Mitosis keeps the chromosome number. Meiosis halves it. Mitosis can give two cells, and in a haploid organism it gives two haploid cells.

Remember meiosis as **one copying, two divisions**. Homologs separate in meiosis I. Sister chromatids separate in meiosis II.

To count chromosomes, count centromeres, not rods. A chromosome of two joined chromatids counts as one. Chromatids are counted as chromosomes only after they separate in anaphase.

For variation, name two mechanisms: **crossing over** in prophase I and **independent assortment** at metaphase I. $2^n$ counts gamete types only when $n$ is the number of gene pairs that are heterozygous (such as $Aa$) and sit on different chromosomes. A homozygous pair (such as $BB$) adds no choice, so $AaBB$ gives $2$ types, not $4$. Ignore crossing over when you use it.

To tell a metaphase, ask whether the chromosomes are in pairs. Pairs mean metaphase I. Single file with a full set means mitosis. Single file with half the set means metaphase II.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Saying Meiosis Always Makes Four Equal Gametes

> Meiosis usually makes four haploid cells, but gamete development can change the result.

Human spermatogenesis makes four sperm cells. Oogenesis normally makes one functional egg and one to three small polar bodies.

### #2: Calling Cytokinesis a Phase of Mitosis

> Mitosis divides the nucleus. Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm.

They overlap in time, but they are different processes. If cytokinesis does not follow mitosis, one cell can end up with two nuclei.

### #3: Saying Mitotic Daughter Cells Are Absolutely Identical

> Mitosis copies DNA with high accuracy, but mutations and chromosome-sorting errors can occur.

For ordinary exam problems, "genetically identical" is a useful model, not a law without exceptions.

### #4: Confusing Homologs with Sister Chromatids

> Homologs separate in meiosis I. Sister chromatids separate in meiosis II.

Homologs are a pair from two parents. Sister chromatids are copies of one chromosome. This is the core reason meiosis halves the chromosome number.

### #5: Counting Chromatids as Chromosomes

> Count chromosomes by centromeres.

From S phase until anaphase, a cell with $46$ chromosomes has $92$ chromatids. A cell with $92$ chromatids does not have $92$ chromosomes then. After the chromatids separate, each counts as a chromosome.

### #6: Thinking the Cell Is Still Diploid After Meiosis I

> The number of chromosomes halves in meiosis I, not meiosis II.

After meiosis I, each cell is already haploid, because it holds one chromosome from each pair. Meiosis II only splits the chromatids, so the number of chromosomes stays $n$.
