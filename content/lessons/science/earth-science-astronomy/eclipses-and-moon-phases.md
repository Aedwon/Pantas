## Core Concept

### Phases of the Moon

The Moon does not make its own light. We only see the half of the Moon that sunlight is hitting. As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different amounts of its sunlit half, and this creates the **phases of the Moon**.

One full cycle of phases, from one new moon to the next, takes about $29.5$ days (called a **synodic month**). The main phases in order: **new moon** (the sunlit side faces away from Earth, so the Moon looks dark), **waxing crescent**, **first quarter** (half lit), **waxing gibbous**, **full moon** (the whole sunlit side faces Earth), **waning gibbous**, **last quarter** (half lit), and **waning crescent**, before returning to new moon. "Waxing" means growing bigger; "waning" means shrinking smaller.

### Eclipses

An eclipse happens when the Sun, Earth, and Moon line up almost perfectly in a straight line, so one body's shadow falls on another.

A **solar eclipse** happens at new moon, when the Moon passes directly between the Sun and Earth, blocking the Sun's light from a small part of Earth. A **lunar eclipse** happens at full moon, when Earth passes directly between the Sun and the Moon, so Earth's shadow falls on the Moon.

Each type of eclipse has different versions, based on how completely the shadow covers the object:

- **Total solar eclipse**: the Moon completely covers the Sun, for viewers standing in the Moon's darkest shadow (the **umbra**).
- **Partial solar eclipse**: viewers only in the lighter outer shadow (the **penumbra**) see the Moon cover just part of the Sun.
- **Annular solar eclipse**: the Moon is a little too far from Earth to fully cover the Sun, leaving a bright ring ("ring of fire") visible around the edge.
- **Total lunar eclipse**: the whole Moon passes into Earth's umbra and can turn a reddish color, sometimes called a "blood moon."
- **Partial lunar eclipse**: only part of the Moon passes into Earth's umbra.

Eclipses do not happen every single month, even though new moons and full moons happen every month. This is because the Moon's orbit is slightly tilted compared to Earth's orbit around the Sun, so the three bodies usually miss lining up exactly.

## Examples

### #1: Naming a Moon Phase

A student observes that exactly half of the Moon's visible face is lit, and the lit half is growing bigger each night. What phase is this?

**Solution:**
Match the description to the correct term.

1. "Growing bigger" means the phase is waxing, not waning.
2. Exactly half lit, while growing, is the first quarter phase (on the way toward full moon).
3. The answer: first quarter (waxing).

### #2: Solar Eclipse or Lunar Eclipse?

During a certain event, the Moon passes directly between the Sun and Earth, and its shadow falls on part of Earth's surface. What phase of the Moon must this be happening during, and what type of eclipse is this?

**Solution:**
Work out the required alignment first.

1. The Moon sits between the Sun and Earth, so its shadow falls on Earth. This alignment can only happen at new moon, when the Moon's dark side faces us.
2. Since the Moon's shadow is falling on Earth, this is a solar eclipse.
3. Answer: new moon, solar eclipse.

### #3: Why No Eclipse Every Month?

New moons and full moons both happen every month, yet eclipses do not happen every month. Explain why.

**Solution:**
Consider the shape of the Moon's orbit relative to Earth's orbit.

1. For an eclipse, the Sun, Earth, and Moon must line up in an almost perfectly straight line.
2. The Moon's orbital path around Earth is tilted slightly compared to the plane of Earth's orbit around the Sun.
3. Because of this tilt, at most new moons and full moons, the Moon actually passes slightly above or below the perfect alignment line, so no shadow falls where it needs to.
4. Only when a new moon or full moon happens to occur very close to where the two tilted orbital planes cross does a true eclipse occur.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Two clean rules replace a lot of memorization: solar eclipse happens only at new moon (Moon between Sun and Earth); lunar eclipse happens only at full moon (Earth between Sun and Moon). If a question's timing does not match, the described eclipse type is impossible.

For phase names, "waxing" and "waning" tell you the direction (growing or shrinking), while "crescent," "quarter," and "gibbous" tell you the amount lit. Combine the two words and you can name any phase without memorizing a picture.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Mixing Up Which Eclipse Happens at Which Phase

> Solar eclipse: New moon. Lunar eclipse: Full moon.

It is easy to swap these, especially since "solar" might make students think of the Moon glowing brightly (which actually matches full moon, but for the wrong eclipse type). Anchor it by remembering who blocks whom: the Moon blocks the Sun during a solar eclipse, which needs new moon.

### #2: Assuming Eclipses Happen Every Month

> Eclipses need the Moon's orbit to also line up with Earth's orbital plane, which does not happen every month.

A choice may state "a lunar eclipse happens at every full moon." This is false: most full moons pass just above or below Earth's shadow because of the Moon's tilted orbit, so only some full moons produce an actual eclipse.

### #3: Confusing the Umbra and Penumbra

> The umbra is the dark, complete shadow. The penumbra is the lighter, partial shadow.

Viewers in the umbra see a total eclipse; viewers in the penumbra see only a partial eclipse. A question describing "the Sun completely blocked" points to the umbra, while "the Sun partly covered" points to the penumbra.
