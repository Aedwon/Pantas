## Core Concept

### Phases of the Moon

The Moon makes no light of its own. It shines because sunlight hits it. Sunlight always lights one half of the Moon, the half that faces the Sun.

The Moon orbits Earth, so our view of that lit half keeps changing. Sometimes we see all of it, and sometimes none. These changing shapes are the **phases of the Moon**.

One cycle, from one new moon to the next, takes about $29.5$ days. This time is called a **synodic month**.

Four words describe the shapes. **Waxing** means the lit part is growing. **Waning** means it is shrinking.

**Crescent** means less than half of the face is lit. **Gibbous** means more than half is lit.

The order of the phases is below. Read it as one cycle that repeats.

1. **New moon**: the lit half faces away from Earth, so the Moon looks dark.
2. Waxing crescent.
3. **First quarter**: half of the face is lit.
4. Waxing gibbous.
5. **Full moon**: the whole face is lit.
6. Waning gibbous.
7. **Last quarter**: half of the face is lit.
8. Waning crescent, and then new moon again.

A "quarter" moon is not one quarter lit. Half of its face is lit. The name counts the trip around the orbit: at first quarter the Moon is one quarter of the way around from new moon, and at last quarter it is three quarters of the way around. Earth's shadow does not cause the phases.

The phase also tells you when the Moon is in the sky. A waxing Moon shows in the evening sky. A waning Moon shows in the morning sky, before sunrise.

A full moon rises near sunset and sets near sunrise.

### Eclipses

An **eclipse** happens when the Sun, Earth, and Moon line up in an almost straight line. Then one body's shadow falls on another body.

A shadow has two parts. The **umbra** is the dark center, where light is fully blocked. The **penumbra** is the lighter outer part, where light is partly blocked.

A **solar eclipse** happens at new moon. The Moon is between the Sun and Earth. The Moon's shadow falls on a small part of Earth, so only people in that part see the eclipse.

A **lunar eclipse** happens at full moon. Earth is between the Sun and the Moon, and Earth's shadow falls on the Moon.

Anyone on the night side of Earth, where the Moon is above the horizon, can see it.

The kind of eclipse depends on how much of the object the shadow covers:

- **Total solar eclipse**: the Moon completely covers the Sun for viewers in the Moon's umbra.
- **Partial solar eclipse**: viewers in the Moon's penumbra see the Moon cover only part of the Sun.
- **Annular solar eclipse**: the Moon looks too small to cover the Sun, so a bright ring shows around the Moon. This happens when the Moon is far from Earth in its orbit, which is not a perfect circle.
- **Total lunar eclipse**: the whole Moon moves into Earth's umbra. It often turns reddish, because Earth's air bends some red sunlight onto the Moon.
- **Partial lunar eclipse**: only part of the Moon moves into Earth's umbra.
- **Penumbral lunar eclipse**: the Moon enters only Earth's penumbra and dims slightly.

Eclipses do not happen every month, even though a new moon and a full moon do. The Moon's orbit is tilted about $5$ degrees from Earth's orbit around the Sun. The two orbits cross at only two points.

So the Moon usually passes above or below the line from the Sun through Earth. An eclipse needs a new moon or full moon near a crossing point.

## Examples

### #1: Naming a Moon Phase

A student observes that exactly half of the Moon's visible face is lit, and the lit part grows bigger each night. Which phase is this?

**Solution:**
Match the description to the correct term.

1. "Growing bigger" means waxing, not waning.
2. Half of the face lit is a quarter phase.
3. A waxing quarter is the first quarter.

The answer is first quarter.

### #2: Naming a Phase From the Sky

Before sunrise, a student sees a thin, curved sliver of the Moon low in the east. The lit part gets thinner each morning. Which phase is this?

**Solution:**
Use the shape, the change, and the time of day.

1. A thin sliver, less than half lit, is a crescent.
2. A lit part that gets thinner is waning, and a waning Moon shows in the morning sky.

The answer is waning crescent.

### #3: Counting Days in the Cycle

A full moon is seen on March $1$. About how many days later is the next new moon?

**Solution:**
A full moon is halfway through the cycle. Half of $29.5$ days is the time to the next new moon.

$$29.5 \div 2 = 14.75$$

The next new moon comes about $15$ days later.

### #4: Solar or Lunar Eclipse?

The Moon passes directly between the Sun and Earth, and its shadow falls on part of Earth. Which Moon phase must it be, and what kind of eclipse is this?

**Solution:**
Work out the alignment first.

1. The Moon is between the Sun and Earth, so only new moon fits.
2. The Moon's shadow on Earth makes a solar eclipse.

The answer is new moon, solar eclipse.

### #5: Lunar Eclipse Alignment

Earth lies between the Sun and the Moon. The whole Moon moves into Earth's umbra. Name the Moon phase, the eclipse, and what the Moon may look like.

**Solution:**
Find whose shadow falls on whom.

1. Earth is in the middle, so Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. This is a lunar eclipse at full moon.
2. The whole Moon is inside the umbra, so the eclipse is total.
3. Some red sunlight bends through Earth's air onto the Moon.

The answer is full moon, total lunar eclipse, and the Moon may look reddish.

### #6: Total or Partial?

During a solar eclipse, the Moon's umbra falls on town P. Town Q lies in the Moon's penumbra. What does a person in each town see?

**Solution:**
Match each shadow to how much of the Sun is blocked.

1. In the umbra, the light is fully blocked. Town P sees the Moon cover the whole Sun: a total solar eclipse.
2. In the penumbra, the light is partly blocked. Town Q sees the Moon cover only part of the Sun: a partial solar eclipse.

### #7: A Ring Around the Moon

During a solar eclipse, the Moon is at the part of its orbit farthest from Earth. A person at the center of the eclipse path sees a bright ring around the dark Moon. What kind of eclipse is this, and why?

**Solution:**
Think about how big the Moon looks.

1. A farther Moon looks smaller in the sky.
2. It is too small to cover the whole Sun, so the Sun's edge shows as a bright ring.

The answer is an annular solar eclipse.

### #8: Why No Eclipse Every Month?

New moons and full moons happen every month, yet eclipses do not. Explain why.

**Solution:**
Compare the Moon's orbit with Earth's orbit.

1. For an eclipse, the Sun, Earth, and Moon must line up almost exactly.
2. The Moon's orbit is tilted about $5$ degrees from Earth's orbit.
3. At most new moons and full moons, the Moon is a little above or below the line, so no shadow falls where it is needed.
4. An eclipse happens only when the new moon or full moon falls near a point where the two orbits cross.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Ask who is in the middle. If the Moon is in the middle, it is a solar eclipse at new moon. If Earth is in the middle, it is a lunar eclipse at full moon.

If the timing does not match, that eclipse is impossible.

Name a phase between new moon and full moon with two words: "waxing" or "waning" for direction, and "crescent," "quarter," or "gibbous" for the amount lit. For example, a lit part that is growing and less than half is a waxing crescent.

This shortcut has limits. New moon and full moon have no direction word. For the quarter phases, the usual names are first quarter (waxing) and last quarter (waning), not "waxing quarter" or "waning quarter".

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Mixing Up Which Eclipse Happens at Which Phase

> Solar eclipse: new moon. Lunar eclipse: full moon.

Students often swap these. Remember who blocks whom. The Moon blocks the Sun in a solar eclipse, and that needs a new moon.

### #2: Assuming Eclipses Happen Every Month

> Eclipses also need the Moon to be near the crossing of the two orbits.

A choice may say that a lunar eclipse happens at every full moon. This is false. Most full moons pass just above or below Earth's shadow.

### #3: Confusing the Umbra and Penumbra

> The umbra is the dark, complete shadow. The penumbra is the lighter, partial shadow.

For a solar eclipse, viewers in the Moon's umbra see a total eclipse. Viewers in the Moon's penumbra see a partial one. "The Sun is completely blocked" points to the umbra, and "partly covered" points to the penumbra.

This rule does not carry over to a lunar eclipse. For the Moon, the umbra gives a total eclipse when the whole Moon is inside it, and a partial eclipse when only part of the Moon is. A Moon that enters only the penumbra gives a penumbral eclipse, not a partial one.

### #4: Blaming Earth's Shadow for the Phases

> Phases are the lit half seen from different angles, not shadow.

A choice may say that Earth's shadow covers part of the Moon to make a crescent. That describes a lunar eclipse. Sunlight always lights half of the Moon, and we see more or less of that half.
