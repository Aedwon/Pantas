## Core Concept

The **water cycle** is the movement of water between the oceans, the air, and the land. Water changes between liquid, gas, and solid as it moves. The cycle has no fixed starting point and no fixed order, so a drop of water can take many different paths.

Sunlight supplies the heat that powers the upward steps. Gravity pulls water down in the downward steps.

### The stages

**Evaporation**: heat turns liquid water into **water vapor**, which is water as an invisible gas. The vapor rises into the air. Evaporation happens from oceans, lakes, rivers, wet soil, and wet leaves. The oceans give the most vapor because they have the most exposed water.

**Transpiration**: plants take in water through their roots. The water travels up the plant, and the leaves let some of it out as vapor through tiny pores. Transpiration is usually faster in warm, dry, windy air. Evaporation and transpiration together are called **evapotranspiration**.

**Sublimation**: ice or snow can turn straight into vapor without becoming liquid first. This is slower than evaporation, but it adds some vapor to the air over glaciers and snowfields.

**Condensation**: air that rises usually gets cooler. Cool air cannot hold as much vapor, so the vapor turns back into tiny liquid droplets. The droplets gather on tiny bits of dust or salt in the air, and together they form clouds. Condensation is the opposite of evaporation.

**Precipitation**: cloud droplets are too small to fall. They bump into each other and join until they are heavy enough to drop. Precipitation is any water that falls from clouds.

- Rain is liquid drops.
- Snow is ice crystals.
- Sleet is small pellets of ice made when raindrops freeze as they fall.
- Hail is lumps of ice that grow inside strong storm clouds.

**Infiltration and runoff**: when precipitation reaches the ground, it has two main paths. Water that soaks into the soil is **infiltration**. Water that flows over the ground surface into streams and rivers is **runoff**. Runoff often ends in the ocean, but it can also end in a lake or in dry land inland.

Soaked-in water can sink deeper and become **groundwater**, which is water held in the spaces of soil and rock underground. Groundwater usually moves slowly and can come out again in springs, rivers, or the ocean. Plant roots can also take it up for transpiration.

### What decides infiltration or runoff

The same rain can split between the two paths. More water soaks in when the soil is sandy or loose, dry, flat, or covered with plants. More water runs off when the ground is steep, already soaked, packed hard, or paved. Very heavy rain also runs off, because the soil cannot take in water as fast as it arrives.

### Where Earth's water is stored

A **reservoir** is a place where water is stored. The ocean is the largest reservoir. It holds about $96.5\%$ of Earth's water, according to the US Geological Survey.

About $2.5\%$ of Earth's water is fresh water, according to the US Geological Survey. The rest is salty, such as salty groundwater. Most fresh water is frozen in glaciers and ice caps, and most of the remainder is groundwater. Lakes and rivers hold only a tiny share. Water stays in a reservoir for a short time in some places, such as the air, and for a very long time in others, such as deep groundwater and ice.

### Ocean water and its layers

Ocean water is **saline**, which means salty. Seawater has about $35$ grams of dissolved salt in every $1000$ grams of water. The saltiness changes a little from place to place.

**Density** is how much mass fits in a given space. A denser liquid sinks below a less dense one. For seawater, cold water is denser than warm water. Fresh water is different: it is densest at about $4\,^\circ\text{C}$ and gets slightly less dense as it cools toward freezing. Ice floats for a separate reason: solid ice is less dense than liquid water. Salty water is denser than less salty water.

The ocean forms layers because of density and sunlight. The top layer is warm and lit by the Sun. Sunlight reaches down to about $200$ meters. Below it, in most of the open ocean, the temperature falls fast with depth in a layer called the **thermocline**. Under that, the deep water is cold, dense, and dark.

### Ocean currents

A **current** is a steady flow of ocean water. There are two main kinds.

**Surface currents** are driven mainly by wind. Their paths are bent by the shape of the continents and by the **Coriolis effect**. This is the turning of moving water and air caused by Earth's spin. It bends them to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. The result is huge loops of current called **gyres**. Gyres turn clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and counterclockwise in the Southern Hemisphere.

**Deep currents** are driven by differences in density. Near the poles, surface water gets very cold, and sea ice formation leaves extra salt in the water below. This cold, salty water is dense and sinks. It then spreads slowly along the ocean floor, and other water flows in to replace it.

Currents carry heat. A warm current, such as the Kuroshio that flows north past Taiwan and Japan, carries heat from the tropics toward higher latitudes. A cold current, such as the Humboldt Current off western South America, brings cool water toward the tropics.

### Tides

A **tide** is the regular rise and fall of the ocean surface. It is caused by the gravity of the Moon and the Sun pulling on Earth's water. The Moon has the larger effect because it is so much closer. The Sun's effect is smaller but still real.

The Moon pulls hardest on the water facing it. Water on the far side is pulled least. Earth as a whole is pulled toward the Moon more than that far water is, so the far water is left behind and bulges away. This makes two bulges of water, one facing the Moon and one on the opposite side of Earth. The bulges are **high tide**. The places between them have **low tide**.

Earth spins under the bulges. In most places, each spot passes through two high tides and two low tides a day. Some places get only one of each a day, because the shape of the coast and the sea changes the pattern. The **tidal range** is the difference in water height between high tide and low tide.

The Moon also moves around Earth in the same direction as Earth spins. So a given spot reaches the Moon again after about $24$ hours and $50$ minutes. Two high tides are therefore about $12$ hours and $25$ minutes apart, and each day's tides come about $50$ minutes later than the day before.

### Spring tides and neap tides

The Moon's and Sun's pulls add up or partly cancel, depending on where they are.

At **new moon** and **full moon**, the Sun, Earth, and Moon lie nearly in a straight line. The two pulls work together. This gives **spring tides**, with the highest high tides and the lowest low tides, so the tidal range is largest.

At **first quarter** and **third quarter**, the Moon and Sun pull from directions at a right angle to each other. The pulls partly cancel. This gives **neap tides**, with a smaller tidal range.

Spring tides have nothing to do with the spring season. Each type happens about twice a month.

## Examples

### #1: Naming the Water Cycle Stage

Water vapor rises from the ocean surface, cools as it rises higher in the air, and turns into tiny liquid droplets that form a cloud. Which stage of the water cycle does the change from vapor to droplets describe?

**Solution:**
Match the change of state to its stage name.

1. Water vapor is a gas, and droplets are liquid.
2. A gas turning into a liquid is the opposite of evaporation.
3. This gas-to-liquid change, which forms clouds, is condensation.

The answer: condensation.

### #2: Infiltration and Runoff

After a heavy rainstorm, some water soaks slowly into a forest floor. The rest flows quickly over a paved parking lot into a nearby river. Identify which process is which and explain why the two places differ.

**Solution:**
Match each motion to its term, then compare the ground.

1. Water soaking into the soil is infiltration.
2. Water flowing over the surface into the river is runoff.
3. A forest floor is loose and full of roots and gaps, so water can enter.
4. Pavement does not let water in, so the water has to flow across it.

Both processes can happen to the same rainstorm. The ground decides how much goes each way.

### #3: Transpiration in a Plant

A student ties a clear plastic bag tightly around a leafy branch that is still attached to a plant in the sun. After a few hours, drops of water form on the inside of the bag. Where did the water come from, and what is the process called?

**Solution:**
Trace the water from its source to the bag.

1. The bag covers only leaves, so the water did not come from the soil or from a pond.
2. The plant took in water through its roots and moved it up to the leaves.
3. The leaves let out water vapor through tiny pores. This is transpiration.
4. The vapor cooled against the bag and condensed into drops.

The answer: the water came from the plant, by transpiration.

### #4: Precipitation Over Mountains

Moist wind from the sea blows toward a mountain range and is pushed up the slope. It rains heavily on the sea-facing side. Explain why rain falls there.

**Solution:**
Follow the air as it rises.

1. The mountain forces the moist air upward.
2. Rising air cools.
3. Cooler air cannot hold as much water vapor, so the vapor condenses into cloud droplets.
4. The droplets join into drops that are heavy enough to fall as precipitation.

The answer: rising air cools, vapor condenses, and the drops fall as rain on the slope that faces the wind.

### #5: Why Most Water Vapor Comes From the Ocean

Explain why most of the water vapor in the air comes from the ocean rather than from lakes, rivers, or plants.

**Solution:**
Compare how much exposed water each source has.

1. Evaporation needs liquid water that the Sun can heat.
2. The ocean covers most of Earth's surface and holds about $96.5\%$ of its water.
3. This gives the ocean the largest area of exposed water, so it supplies the most vapor.
4. Lakes, rivers, wet soil, and plants add vapor too, but each has far less exposed water than the ocean.

The answer: the ocean supplies the most because it has the most exposed water, not because other sources give none.

### #6: Ocean Density

Four water samples are listed. Which sample is the densest and sinks below the others?

- A: warm and less salty
- B: warm and very salty
- C: cold and less salty
- D: cold and very salty

**Solution:**
Use the two things that raise density.

1. Cold seawater is denser than warm seawater, so C and D beat A and B on temperature.
2. Saltier seawater is denser than less salty seawater, so B and D beat A and C on salt.
3. Sample D wins on both counts.

The answer: D, cold and very salty. This is the kind of water that sinks near the poles and drives the deep currents.

### #7: Currents and Heat

A warm surface current flows from the tropics along a coast far from the equator. What does it do with heat, and what is the main force that drives a surface current like this?

**Solution:**
Link the movement of water to the movement of heat.

1. The current starts in warm tropical water, so it carries heat with it.
2. As it flows toward higher latitudes, it moves that heat to places that would otherwise be cooler.
3. Surface currents are driven mainly by wind, and they are bent by Earth's spin and the continents.

The answer: it carries heat from the tropics toward the pole, and wind is the main driver.

### #8: Timing of High Tides

At a coast with two high tides a day, high tide is at $6$ a.m. on Monday. About when is the next high tide, and about when is high tide on Tuesday morning?

**Solution:**
Use the spacing of tides.

1. Two high tides are about $12$ hours and $25$ minutes apart.
2. Add that to $6{:}00$ a.m. to get about $6{:}25$ p.m. on Monday.
3. Each day's tides come about $50$ minutes later than the day before.
4. High tide on Tuesday morning is therefore at about $6{:}50$ a.m.

The answer: about $6{:}25$ p.m. on Monday and about $6{:}50$ a.m. on Tuesday. Local conditions can shift the exact times.

### #9: Spring or Neap Tide

On what Moon phases do spring tides happen, and on what phases do neap tides happen? Explain the difference in the pull.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: three top-view sketches of Sun, Earth, and Moon: new moon (Moon between Sun and Earth), full moon (Earth between Sun and Moon), and first quarter (Moon at a right angle to the Sun-Earth line), each labeled spring or neap -->

**Solution:**
Place the Sun, Moon, and Earth for each phase.

1. At new moon the Moon is between Earth and the Sun. At full moon Earth is between them. Either way the three are nearly in a line.
2. In a line, the Moon's pull and the Sun's pull work together, so the tidal range is largest. These are spring tides.
3. At first quarter and third quarter the Moon is at a right angle to the Sun as seen from Earth.
4. The two pulls then partly cancel, so the tidal range is smaller. These are neap tides.

The answer: spring tides at new moon and full moon, neap tides at first and third quarter.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Match the change of state to the name. Liquid to gas is evaporation, or transpiration when plants release it. Gas to liquid is condensation. This works for water changing state in the air. Boiling and freezing are other changes of state with their own names.

For where water is stored, remember the ocean: about $96.5\%$ of Earth's water is there. For fresh water, remember that most of it is frozen.

For tides, link the phase to the line-up. A line (new moon and full moon) gives spring tides. A right angle (first and third quarter) gives neap tides. Use this for the large-scale pattern. The size of the tide at a given coast also depends on the shape of the coast.

For seawater density, ask two questions: Is it colder? Is it saltier? Each yes makes the water denser. Fresh water below about $4\,^\circ\text{C}$ is the exception.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Mixing Up Evaporation and Condensation

> Evaporation: liquid to gas. Condensation: gas to liquid.

The two are opposites, and questions often swap their descriptions. Heat that turns water into vapor is evaporation. Cooling that turns vapor into droplets or clouds is condensation.

### #2: Treating the Water Cycle as a Fixed Line

> The water cycle has no fixed start, and water can skip stages.

Questions may list the stages in a set order starting at the ocean. Real water can fall as rain directly onto the ocean, freeze in a glacier for years, or soak into the ground and never run off. A correct answer looks at what the question describes, not at a memorized order.

### #3: Confusing Transpiration With Other Evaporation

> Transpiration is water vapor released by plants. Evaporation also happens from water and soil.

Transpiration is specifically plant water loss through the leaves. Evaporation from lakes, wet soil, and the ocean is not transpiration. Both together are evapotranspiration.

### #4: Forgetting That Most Water Is Salty or Frozen

> The ocean holds about $96.5\%$ of Earth's water, but that water is salty.

Most of Earth's water is salt water. Of the fresh water, most is frozen in glaciers and ice caps, then groundwater. Lakes and rivers hold only a small share.

### #5: Thinking Only the Moon Makes Tides

> Tides come from the Moon and the Sun together.

The Moon has the larger effect, but the Sun matters too. This is why the line-up of the Sun, Earth, and Moon decides whether the tide is spring or neap.

### #6: Linking Spring Tides to the Season

> A spring tide is not a spring-season tide.

Spring tides happen about twice a month, in every season. They are named for the water "springing up". Neap tides happen about twice a month too.

### #7: Mixing Up Tides and Currents

> A tide is a rise and fall of water level. A current is a flow of water.

Tides come from the pull of the Moon and the Sun. Surface currents are driven mainly by wind, and deep currents by density.
