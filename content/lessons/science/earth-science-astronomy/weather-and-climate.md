## Core Concept

**Weather** is the state of the air at a place and time. It includes temperature, rain, clouds, wind, air pressure, and humidity. **Climate** describes usual weather patterns and their range over many years.

**Air pressure** is the force that air puts on a surface. **Humidity** describes the amount of water vapor in the air. Water vapor is water in its gas form.

### Atmospheric Layers

The atmosphere is the layer of gases around Earth. Its layers have different temperature patterns. Their heights change with location, season, and changes in the Sun's activity.

**Troposphere:** This lowest layer holds nearly all ordinary weather. It reaches about $6$ km near the poles and $18$ to $20$ km near the equator. The unit km means kilometers.

**Stratosphere:** This layer extends from the top of the troposphere, called the tropopause, to about $50$ km. Most atmospheric ozone is here, especially around $15$ to $35$ km. Ozone is a gas that absorbs much of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet radiation.

Radiation is energy that travels as waves. As ozone absorbs ultraviolet energy, it warms the air. Temperature generally rises with height in the stratosphere.

**Mesosphere:** This layer lies about $50$ to $85$ km above the ground. Most meteors burn up here as they pass through the air.

**Thermosphere:** This layer starts near $85$ km and extends hundreds of kilometers upward. Its gas particles move very fast, so its measured temperature is high. There are too few particles to warm an object as dense, hot air would.

Auroras, the glowing lights seen near the poles, occur in this broad upper region. Many spacecraft that orbit close to Earth also travel here.

**Exosphere:** This outermost layer has very few gas particles and slowly blends into space. Some satellites orbit here too.

### Sea Breezes and Land Breezes

Land heats and cools faster than the sea. When air warms, it expands and can rise. The upward movement of warm air is **convection**.

During a sunny day, land often becomes warmer than the nearby sea. Warm air rises over land, and cooler air moves in from the sea. This surface wind is a **sea breeze**.

At night, land often cools faster than the sea. Air rises over the relatively warmer sea, and surface air moves from land toward the sea. This is a **land breeze**.

Each breeze is named for where it comes from. These are local daily patterns. Stronger winds from a large weather system can hide or change them.

### Philippine Monsoons

A **monsoon** is a wind pattern that changes direction with the seasons. Seasonal differences in land and ocean heating help cause this change. Near the surface, pressure differences drive air from higher toward lower pressure.

During the Northern Hemisphere's summer, the large Asian landmass heats faster than nearby oceans. Rising warm air helps create lower pressure over land. Moist air flows toward it from the oceans.

During winter, the Asian landmass cools faster than the oceans. Cold, dense air helps build higher pressure over land. Air flows outward toward warmer regions with lower pressure.

Earth's rotation bends moving air. Together with the shifting pressure patterns, this helps produce the seasonal wind reversal over the Philippines.

**Southwest Monsoon (Habagat)** brings warm, moist air from the southwest. It commonly brings rain to western parts of the Philippines. Its usual start is from late May to early June, and it commonly ends in October.

**Northeast Monsoon (Amihan)** brings cooler air from the northeast. It usually starts late in the year and ends around March or early April. It can bring heavy rain to the eastern side of the country.

These dates are guides. PAGASA declares the start and end from observed wind and weather patterns. Mountains and exposure to the wind also affect local rain.

### ITCZ and Rainfall

The **Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)** is a belt near the equator where winds from the northern and southern tropics meet. Convergence means coming together. The meeting air rises, helping form clouds and thunderstorms.

Rising moist air cools. Its water vapor changes into tiny liquid drops, a process called **condensation**. Drops can grow large enough to fall as rain.

The ITCZ shifts north and south with the seasons. It can bring heavy rain to parts of the Philippines. Rain from the ITCZ does not by itself mean a typhoon is present.

### Tropical Cyclones

A **tropical cyclone** is an organized, rotating storm with a low-pressure center that forms over warm tropical seas. Warm water supplies heat and water vapor to the air. A disturbance, such as a group of thunderstorms, can begin the process.

Moist air rises through convection, and surface air moves toward the lower-pressure center. As the rising air cools, water vapor condenses into cloud drops. Condensation releases heat, which helps keep air warm and rising.

Earth's rotation makes the inward-moving air curve. Around Philippine cyclones, the air spirals counterclockwise toward the center. This turning effect is too weak at the equator for cyclones to form there normally.

Warm water alone is not enough. The storm also needs moist air and winds that do not change too sharply with height. Strong changes in wind can pull the storm apart.

Over land or cooler water, the storm loses much of its heat and moisture supply. Its winds usually weaken, but dangerous rain can continue.

PAGASA groups tropical cyclones by their **maximum sustained winds**, the strongest winds averaged over a set time near the center. These are different from brief bursts called gusts. The unit km/h means kilometers per hour.

- Tropical depression: $39$ to $61$ km/h.
- Tropical storm: $62$ to $88$ km/h.
- Severe tropical storm: $89$ to $117$ km/h.
- Typhoon: $118$ to $184$ km/h.
- Super typhoon: $185$ km/h or higher.

A typhoon is one strength category, not a name for every tropical cyclone. The category describes wind strength. It does not tell you the total rain or flood danger at every place.

The western North Pacific can produce tropical cyclones throughout the year. PAGASA identifies July through October as the peak typhoon season. This overlaps much of Habagat, but a cyclone and a monsoon are separate systems.

### Weather Hazards and Protection

**Hydrometeorological hazards** are dangers linked to weather and water. They include floods, rain-triggered landslides, storm surges, strong winds, and droughts. A hazard can cause more harm where people, homes, or farms are exposed to it.

Heavy or long-lasting rain can make rivers overflow and overwhelm drains. Rain can also soak and weaken soil on steep slopes, causing landslides. Clear drains and safe land-use rules help reduce risk before storms.

A **storm surge** is an unusual rise in sea level during a storm. Strong winds push seawater toward the coast. It can flood coastal land, even where local rainfall is light.

Follow official warnings and evacuation orders early. Use local hazard maps to choose safe routes and shelters. Never walk or drive through floodwater.

A **drought** is a long period with too little rain for local needs. Protecting water supplies and planning water use can reduce harm. Cleaning drains helps with floods, but it does not solve a water shortage.

### Global Warming and Climate Change

**Global warming** is the long-term rise in Earth's average surface temperature. The current warming is mainly caused by human increases in **greenhouse gases**. These gases include carbon dioxide released when coal, oil, or gas burns.

The natural **greenhouse effect** keeps Earth warmer than it would otherwise be. Much of the Sun's incoming energy is shortwave radiation. Earth's warmed surface sends out longer-wave energy called **infrared radiation**.

Greenhouse gases and clouds absorb some outgoing infrared energy and send energy out again in all directions. Some returns toward the surface. More greenhouse gases slow energy loss to space, so the surface and lower air warm.

**Climate change** includes lasting changes in temperature, rainfall, ice, sea level, and extreme weather. Global warming is a major part and driver of current climate change. A single hot day or storm cannot show a long-term climate trend.

### Physical and Social Effects

Warming seawater expands and takes up more space. Melting glaciers and ice sheets on land add water to the sea. Both raise sea level and increase coastal flood risk.

Warmer conditions increase evaporation, the change of liquid water into vapor. Warmer air can hold more water vapor, which can supply heavier rain when storms form. Where rain fails to replace lost water, faster drying can worsen drought.

Rainfall changes differ by region. Warming does not mean every place becomes wetter. Higher average temperatures also make extreme heat more likely.

These physical changes affect people. Floods can damage crops and roads, reducing farm income and delaying food deliveries. Water shortages can reduce harvests and increase pressure on household water supplies.

Families may lose homes, miss school, or need to move. Heat can harm health and make outdoor work unsafe. Families with fewer resources often have fewer ways to recover.

Climate change also creates government decisions. Officials must choose how to use public money for water systems, safer housing, and disaster preparation. Moving people away from danger requires safe homes, access to work, and community input.

### Choosing Responses

**Climate mitigation** reduces the causes of climate change. Cutting fossil-fuel use lowers greenhouse gas releases. Protecting forests helps keep stored carbon out of the air.

**Adaptation** means changing how people live or plan to reduce harm from climate effects. Flood warnings, safer building sites, and drought plans are examples. Adaptation reduces risk but cannot prevent every loss.

For a local flood problem, a government can improve waste collection and clear blocked drains. It can also keep new homes away from flood-prone land. These actions address local risk; clearing a drain does not lower greenhouse gas releases by itself.

Choose a response by asking what causes the problem and who faces the harm. Check the cost, long-term upkeep, and effects on nearby communities. A seawall, for example, needs maintenance and does not solve inland flooding from blocked drains.

A community may need both mitigation and adaptation. Cleaner energy addresses future warming. Flood preparation addresses dangers people already face.

## Examples

### #1: Weather or Climate?

"Classify" means put something in the correct group.

Classify each statement as weather or climate: (a) Heavy rain is falling in Manila this afternoon. (b) A region's June-to-August rainfall has increased across several decades.

**Solution:**
1. Statement (a) describes conditions at one place and time. It is weather.
2. Statement (b) describes a pattern over many years. It is climate.

### #2: Which Atmospheric Layer?

A balloon rises above nearly all weather. It measures increasing temperature with height in a region rich in ozone. Which atmospheric layer is it in?

**Solution:**
1. Nearly all ordinary weather occurs in the troposphere. The balloon has risen above it.
2. Ozone absorbs ultraviolet energy in the stratosphere, where temperature generally rises with height. The layer is the stratosphere.

### #3: Coastal Wind Reversal

On a clear afternoon, a coastal town receives wind from the sea. After a calm, clear night begins, the local wind turns toward the sea. Explain the change.

**Solution:**
1. Land warms faster during the day. Rising air over land draws in surface air from the sea, producing a sea breeze.
2. Land cools faster at night. Air rises over the warmer sea, and a land breeze flows toward it.

### #4: Seasonal Pressure Change

During winter, a large Asian land area becomes colder than nearby seas. How does this change help produce Amihan?

**Solution:**
1. Cooling helps create higher surface pressure over the land.
2. Air flows outward toward warmer regions with lower pressure. Earth's turning bends its path.
3. The resulting northeasterly flow reaches the Philippines as Amihan.

### #5: Habagat or Amihan?

PAGASA observes steady, warm, moist southwesterly winds. Frequent rain affects western parts of Luzon and Visayas. Which monsoon is dominant?

**Solution:**
1. Southwesterly means the air comes from the southwest. This matches Habagat.
2. Rain over western sections supports that answer. It does not mean every Philippine location has the same rain.

### #6: Amihan Rainfall

A student says, "Amihan means the whole Philippines is dry." What is wrong with the statement?

**Solution:**
1. Amihan winds can carry moisture toward the eastern side of the country. Those exposed areas can receive heavy rain.
2. Rainfall differs by region. Cooler, drier weather in many western locations does not mean dry weather everywhere.

### #7: Rain Without a Typhoon

No tropical cyclone is nearby. A weather report describes a belt where winds from the northern and southern tropics meet. Why can this belt still bring heavy rain?

**Solution:**
1. The report describes the ITCZ. Air comes together there and rises.
2. Rising moist air cools, and water vapor condenses into clouds. Thunderstorms and heavy rain can develop without a typhoon.

### #8: A Cyclone Loses Strength

An organized tropical cyclone moves from warm water onto a large land area. Why do its winds usually weaken?

**Solution:**
1. Over warm water, evaporation supplies moisture. Rising air condenses, releasing heat that helps sustain the storm.
2. Over land, that warm ocean supply is cut off. Winds usually weaken, although heavy rain and floods can continue.

### #9: Typhoon or Habagat?

A PAGASA bulletin reports maximum sustained winds of $130$ km/h near a cyclone's center. Another report describes widespread southwesterly monsoon winds. What does each report describe?

**Solution:**
1. The cyclone's wind speed lies between $118$ and $184$ km/h. Its category is typhoon.
2. The southwesterly seasonal flow is Habagat. It is a separate system, even if both affect the country at once.

### #10: A Coastal Evacuation

Officials warn of storm surge in a low coastal barangay and order evacuation. Local rain is still light. Should residents wait for heavy rain before leaving?

**Solution:**
1. Storm surge comes from seawater pushed toward the coast. Local rainfall does not measure this danger.
2. Residents should follow the evacuation order and use the designated safe route. Waiting for heavy rain could delay escape.

### #11: Greenhouse Effect

What kind of radiation do greenhouse gases absorb and send out again in the basic greenhouse-effect explanation?

**Solution:**
1. Earth's warmed surface sends out infrared radiation.
2. Greenhouse gases absorb some of that energy and send it out again. Some returns toward the surface, slowing energy loss to space.

### #12: From Warming to Public Decisions

A coastal town plans for rising sea level. Explain how global warming can raise the sea and create economic, social, and government problems.

**Solution:**
1. Warming expands seawater and melts land ice. Both raise sea level and increase coastal flood risk.
2. Floods can damage homes, farms, and roads. Repairs cost money, and lost crops reduce income.
3. Families may need safer housing. Officials must decide how to fund protection or relocation while keeping work and schools accessible.

### #13: Choosing Climate Responses

A town's drains fill with waste, and nearby homes flood during heavy rain. Officials propose regular waste collection and drain clearing. They also propose solar power to replace electricity from coal. What problem does each action address?

**Solution:**
1. Waste collection and drain clearing help water flow away. They reduce local flood risk and can help the town adapt to heavier rain.
2. Replacing coal power with solar power cuts greenhouse gas releases. This is climate mitigation.
3. The actions serve different needs. Solar power does not clear blocked drains, and drain clearing does not reduce the town's coal use.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For weather and climate questions, check the time span. A day's conditions describe weather. A pattern across many years describes climate.

Recall the atmospheric order from the ground: troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere, exosphere. Match each clue to the layer's features, such as weather or ozone.

For winds, check where the air comes from and why it moves. Sea and land breezes change over a day. Monsoon winds change with the seasons.

Use wind direction and the affected region to identify Habagat or Amihan. Use sustained wind strength to name a cyclone's category. Do not use rainfall alone for either task.

For response questions, connect the action to the cause. Cutting greenhouse gas releases is climate mitigation. Reducing harm from climate effects is adaptation.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Monsoon Dates Are Not Fixed

> Seasonal dates are guides, not exact rules for every year.

PAGASA checks observed patterns before declaring a monsoon's start or end. A month alone is a weaker clue than wind direction and conditions.

### #2: Typhoons and Habagat Are Different

> A typhoon is a cyclone category, while Habagat is a seasonal wind pattern.

Their seasons overlap, but tropical cyclones can occur outside Habagat. Do not label every rainy monsoon day a typhoon.

### #3: Amihan Is Not Dry Everywhere

> Northeasterly winds can bring heavy rain to eastern areas.

An answer saying the whole country becomes dry ignores regional differences. Check which areas face the incoming wind.

### #4: Greenhouse Gases Affect Outgoing Infrared Energy

> The greenhouse effect involves energy leaving the warmed Earth.

An answer about simply trapping incoming sunlight misses the main process. Greenhouse gases absorb and send out infrared energy, slowing energy loss to space.

### #5: Layer Heights Are Approximate

> Atmospheric boundaries change with location, season, and solar activity.

Do not treat a layer's listed height as a fixed wall. Use its temperature pattern and other clues too.

### #6: Weakening Winds Do Not End Flood Danger

> A weaker cyclone can still produce dangerous rain.

Wind categories do not measure all hazards. A low wind category alone does not show that a flooded area is safe.

### #7: One Weather Event Does Not Prove a Climate Trend

> Climate claims need evidence across many years.

One cold day does not disprove global warming. One flood also does not prove that climate change alone caused it.

### #8: A Response Must Match the Problem

> Climate mitigation and adaptation solve different parts of the problem.

An emissions plan does not replace an evacuation plan. A local government must address current danger as well as future warming.
