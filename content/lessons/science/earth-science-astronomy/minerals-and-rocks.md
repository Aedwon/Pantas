## Core Concept

A **mineral** is a naturally occurring, solid substance with a specific chemical makeup and an orderly crystal structure. A **rock** is simply a mix of one or more minerals stuck together.

### Identifying Minerals

Since many minerals can look similar, scientists use specific physical properties to tell them apart.

- **Hardness**: measured using the **Mohs hardness scale**, from $1$ (very soft, like talc) to $10$ (very hard, like diamond). A mineral can scratch any mineral with a lower number, and gets scratched by any mineral with a higher number.
- **Streak**: the color of a mineral's powder, made by rubbing it on an unglazed tile. Streak is more reliable than the mineral's outer color, since the outer color can vary while the streak color usually stays constant.
- **Luster**: how the mineral's surface reflects light, mainly grouped as **metallic** (shiny, like metal) or **non-metallic** (dull, glassy, pearly, and so on).
- **Cleavage and fracture**: **cleavage** is breaking along smooth, flat planes (because of weaker bonds in that direction); **fracture** is breaking in a rough, irregular way, with no flat planes.

### The Three Rock Types and the Rock Cycle

**Igneous rock** forms when melted rock (magma or lava) cools and hardens. If it cools slowly underground, it forms large crystals (like granite); if it cools quickly at the surface (like from a volcano), it forms small or no crystals (like basalt or obsidian).

**Sedimentary rock** forms when small pieces of rock, sand, shells, or other debris (**sediment**) pile up in layers and get pressed and cemented together over a long time (like sandstone, shale, and limestone).

**Metamorphic rock** forms when an existing rock (igneous, sedimentary, or even another metamorphic rock) is changed by intense heat and pressure underground, without fully melting. The heat and pressure rearrange its minerals into a new texture (like marble from limestone, or quartzite from sandstone).

The **rock cycle** connects all three types: No rock type is a permanent "final form." Igneous rock can weather into sediment, which becomes sedimentary rock. Any rock can be squeezed by heat and pressure into metamorphic rock. Any rock can melt completely back into magma, starting the cycle over. There is no fixed starting or ending point.

## Examples

### #1: Applying the Mohs Scale

Mineral A can scratch Mineral B, but Mineral B cannot scratch Mineral A. Mineral C can scratch both A and B. Rank the three minerals from softest to hardest.

**Solution:**
Use the scratching rule: a mineral scratches anything softer than itself.

1. Since A can scratch B, A is harder than B.
2. Since C can scratch both A and B, C is the hardest of the three.
3. Order from softest to hardest: B, A, C.

### #2: Classifying Rock Formation

Classify each rock-forming process as igneous, sedimentary, or metamorphic: (a) lava cools quickly on a volcano's surface; (b) layers of sand and shell fragments are compressed over thousands of years; (c) limestone is squeezed by intense heat and pressure deep underground and turns into marble.

**Solution:**
Match each process to its defining action: cooling from melted rock, compressing sediment, or transforming existing rock with heat and pressure.

1. (a) Lava cooling is melted rock hardening: Igneous.
2. (b) Sand and shells compressing into layers over time: Sedimentary.
3. (c) Limestone transforming under heat and pressure without melting: Metamorphic.

### #3: Tracing a Path Through the Rock Cycle

Describe a path through the rock cycle that turns granite (an igneous rock) into marble (a metamorphic rock), passing through a sedimentary stage in between.

**Solution:**
Follow the rock cycle's connecting arrows step by step.

1. Granite, exposed at the surface, slowly weathers and breaks down into small sediment particles over time.
2. These sediment particles are compressed and cemented together into a sedimentary rock, such as limestone (if the sediment happens to be made of shell and mineral fragments).
3. This sedimentary rock is then buried deep underground, where intense heat and pressure transform it, without fully melting it, into a metamorphic rock: marble.
4. Path: igneous (granite) → weathering → sedimentary (limestone) → heat and pressure → metamorphic (marble).

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For mineral identification, remember the ranking order of reliability: streak beats color, since a mineral's outer color can be affected by small impurities, but its powdered streak color usually stays the same.

For rock classification questions, hunt for the one keyword that gives it away: "cooled from magma or lava" means igneous; "layers," "compressed," or "cemented sediment" means sedimentary; "heat and pressure, without melting" means metamorphic.

Picture the rock cycle as a circle with no starting point and no ending point. Any rock type can eventually become any other rock type, given the right process (weathering, compacting, heating, or melting).

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Confusing Streak with Surface Color

> Streak is the color of the mineral's powder, which can be very different from its outer color.

The mineral pyrite looks golden on the outside but leaves a dark greenish-black streak. A question describing "the color left behind when scratched on a tile" is asking about streak, not the visible color of the whole mineral sample.

### #2: Thinking Metamorphic Rock Requires Melting

> Metamorphic rock forms without fully melting. If it melts completely, it becomes magma instead.

Heat and pressure change the rock's minerals and texture while it stays essentially solid. A choice describing "rock that melts and re-forms" describes the igneous process (via magma), not the metamorphic process.

### #3: Believing the Rock Cycle Has a Fixed Order

> Any rock type can become any other rock type — there is no required sequence.

Students sometimes memorize only one path (igneous → sedimentary → metamorphic) and assume it always goes in that order. In reality, a metamorphic rock can weather directly into sediment, or an igneous rock can be metamorphosed directly without ever becoming sedimentary first.
