## Core Concept

### Minerals and Rocks

A **mineral** is a solid that forms in nature, is inorganic, has its atoms arranged in an orderly pattern, and has a definite chemical makeup or a narrow range of makeups. "Inorganic" means it is not made by living things from the carbon compounds of life. Quartz, feldspar, calcite, and olivine are common minerals.

A **rock** is a solid mass made of one or more minerals, or of other natural mineral-like matter, stuck together. Granite holds several minerals. Some rocks are made almost entirely of one mineral, such as quartzite, which is mostly quartz.

Rocks are grouped mainly by how they form. A rock can also contain material that is not a crystal. Obsidian is natural glass with no crystals, so it is a rock but not a mineral.

Coal is a rock made from buried plant remains. It is not a mineral, because it comes from living things.

### Identifying Minerals

Mineral color is not a reliable clue, because one mineral can come in many colors. Quartz can be clear, pink, purple, or smoky gray. Geologists use several simple tests together.

**Hardness** is how well a mineral resists being scratched. A harder mineral scratches a softer one. The **Mohs scale** ranks ten minerals from $1$ (softest) to $10$ (hardest): talc $1$, gypsum $2$, calcite $3$, fluorite $4$, apatite $5$, orthoclase feldspar $6$, quartz $7$, topaz $8$, corundum $9$, diamond $10$.

The Mohs scale only ranks minerals. The steps are not equal in size. A fingernail has a hardness of about $2.5$. A glass plate or a steel nail is about $5.5$.

**Streak** is the color of a mineral's powder. You rub the mineral on an unglazed porcelain plate. Streak can differ from the color of the whole sample. Hematite can look silver-gray or red, but its streak is reddish-brown. A mineral harder than the plate, about $6.5$, scratches the plate and leaves no useful streak.

**Luster** is how the surface reflects light. It can be metallic, like a polished metal, or nonmetallic, such as glassy, pearly, or dull.

**Cleavage** is when a mineral breaks along flat, smooth surfaces. **Fracture** is when it breaks along rough or curved surfaces instead. Mica splits into thin sheets in one direction. Halite breaks into small cubes, with three cleavage directions at right angles. Quartz has no cleavage and breaks with curved surfaces.

Some minerals also show a special property. Calcite fizzes in a drop of dilute acid. Magnetite is attracted to a magnet.

No single test names every mineral. Use two or three tests together.

### Weathering, Erosion, and Deposition

**Weathering** breaks rock down where it sits. It does not move the pieces away. **Physical weathering** breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing the minerals. An example is frost wedging: water gets in a crack, freezes, expands, and pushes the crack wider. **Chemical weathering** changes the minerals. Rainwater with dissolved carbon dioxide is slightly acidic and slowly dissolves limestone. Iron-bearing minerals can rust when oxygen and water reach them.

**Erosion** is the moving of weathered pieces by water, wind, ice, or gravity. **Deposition** is when the moving material is dropped in a new place. The loose pieces are called **sediment**.

Erosion cuts away land and makes landforms such as river valleys and canyons. Deposition builds landforms such as river deltas, beaches, and sand dunes. Dissolving limestone underground can open caves and sinkholes.

### Igneous Rocks

**Igneous rocks** form when molten rock cools and hardens. Molten rock underground is **magma**. Molten rock that reaches the surface is **lava**.

A **crystal** is a piece of mineral with its atoms in an orderly pattern. The **texture** of a rock means the size and arrangement of its grains or crystals. The cooling time controls crystal size. Slow cooling gives crystals more time to grow.

- **Intrusive** igneous rocks cool below the surface. They usually cool slowly, so they often have large crystals you can see. Granite is an example.
- **Extrusive** igneous rocks cool at or near the surface. Fast cooling usually gives small crystals, as in basalt. Very fast cooling can give natural glass, as in obsidian. Pumice has many holes where gas escaped from the lava.

Texture shows how fast a rock cooled. Color and mineral makeup show the kind of magma it came from. Granite is usually light in color, and basalt is usually dark.

### Sedimentary Rocks

**Sedimentary rocks** form from sediment and from minerals that come out of water. Sediment is laid down in layers called **strata**. Over time the layers are buried, and the sediment becomes rock by **lithification**. Lithification has two main steps. **Compaction** squeezes the grains together. **Cementation** happens when minerals dissolved in water fill the gaps and glue the grains.

Some sedimentary rocks form from pieces of older rock. Sandstone forms from sand, shale from very fine mud and clay, and conglomerate from rounded gravel. Other sedimentary rocks form when minerals **precipitate**, which means they come out of water as a solid. Rock salt forms when seawater evaporates. Limestone forms from shells and other calcium carbonate, which can build up from living things or from the water itself. Coal forms from buried plant remains.

In undisturbed layers, the lowest layer is usually the oldest. Fossils are the preserved remains or traces of past life. They are common in sedimentary rock, because it forms at low temperatures and pressures that do not destroy them. A few fossils also occur in other rocks, such as in volcanic ash that hardened. Fossils are rarely, if ever, found in igneous rock that formed from a melt, because the heat would destroy them.

### Metamorphic Rocks

**Metamorphic rocks** form when existing rock is changed by heat, pressure, or hot fluids, **without wholesale melting**. The rock stays solid while its minerals change or regrow into new arrangements. If the rock melts completely and cools again, the new rock is igneous.

Squeezing in one direction can line up flat minerals into layers or bands. This is called **foliation**. Foliated rocks include slate, schist, and gneiss. Rocks without layers, such as marble and quartzite, are non-foliated.

Shale can change to slate, and with more heat and pressure to schist and gneiss. Limestone changes to marble. Sandstone changes to quartzite.

### The Rock Cycle

The **rock cycle** is the set of ways that rock changes from one kind to another. It is a network, not one fixed loop with a set order. Any rock can be weathered into sediment once it is exposed at the surface. Buried rock can undergo metamorphism. Any rock that melts becomes magma, and magma can cool into igneous rock.

A rock does not have to pass through every stage. Igneous rock can become metamorphic rock without ever reaching the surface. Metamorphic rock can be weathered into sediment. A rock can also stay the same type, such as sedimentary rock that is weathered into sediment and becomes sedimentary rock again.

Each step needs its own process and its own material. Heat and pressure cause metamorphism. Cooling hardens melt. Compaction and cementation make sediment into rock.

### Reading a Simple Experiment

A **variable** is something in an investigation that can change. The **independent variable** is the one you change on purpose. The **dependent variable** is what you measure. A **controlled variable** is a condition you keep the same, so that only the independent variable can explain the result.

## Examples

### #1: Mineral or Rock?

Quartz is a solid formed in nature with an orderly internal structure and a definite makeup. Granite contains quartz, feldspar, and other minerals. Which is a mineral and which is a rock?

**Solution:**
Quartz is a mineral. Granite is a rock made of several minerals. Quartzite is also a rock, even though it is mostly one mineral.

### #2: Telling Two Minerals Apart

A yellow metallic mineral scratches a glass plate. It leaves a greenish-black streak. A student says it is gold. Is the student likely right?

**Solution:**
1. The mineral scratches glass, so its hardness is above about $5.5$.
2. Gold is soft, with a hardness of about $2.5$ to $3$. A mineral that scratches glass is too hard to be gold.
3. Gold leaves a yellow streak, not a greenish-black one.

The student is likely wrong. A yellow metallic mineral with a greenish-black streak is more likely pyrite.

### #3: Streak Is Not the Same as Color

A mineral looks silver-gray in a sample but leaves a reddish-brown streak. A student says the streak must be wrong. What is the correct explanation?

**Solution:**
Streak is the color of the powder, and the powder can differ from the whole sample. Hematite is an example. The streak is more reliable than the color of the sample, because powder color is steadier.

### #4: Cleavage or Fracture?

One mineral splits into thin flat sheets. Another breaks with curved, shell-like surfaces. Which shows cleavage and which shows fracture?

**Solution:**
Splitting into flat sheets is cleavage along one direction. Mica does this. Breaking along curved surfaces is fracture. Quartz does this.

### #5: Cooling Rate and Texture

Two magmas have a similar makeup. One cools slowly deep underground. The other erupts and cools quickly at the surface. **Infer** which rock likely has larger crystals. "Infer" means to reach a conclusion from evidence.

**Solution:**
The slowly cooled intrusive rock likely has larger crystals, because the crystals have more time to grow. The quickly cooled extrusive rock likely has small crystals. If it cools extremely fast, it can form glass with no crystals, such as obsidian.

### #6: A Controlled Cooling Test

A student pours the same amount of the same hot, saturated salt solution into three identical dishes. Dish A cools in an ice bath. Dish B cools at room temperature. Dish C is kept warm. Later the student measures the size of the salt crystals in each dish. What is the independent variable, the dependent variable, and two controlled variables?

**Solution:**
1. The student changes the cooling condition, so cooling rate is the independent variable.
2. The student measures crystal size, so crystal size is the dependent variable.
3. The same solution and the same amount are kept constant, so they are controlled variables. The identical dishes are another.

Only cooling differs between the dishes, so a difference in crystal size can be linked to cooling. This is a small model of how cooling time may affect crystal size in igneous rock.

### #7: From Loose Grains to Rock

Sand is buried under many layers of mud and sand. Describe how it becomes sandstone.

**Solution:**
1. The weight of the layers above squeezes the sand grains together. This is compaction.
2. Water between the grains carries dissolved minerals. The minerals fill the gaps and glue the grains. This is cementation.
3. Compaction and cementation together are lithification. The loose sand is now sandstone.

### #8: Weathering, Erosion, and Deposition

A river carries sand and mud from a mountain slope and drops it where the river meets the sea. Name the process at each place.

**Solution:**
1. On the slope, rock is broken down where it sits. This is weathering.
2. The river carries the pieces away. This is erosion.
3. At the river mouth, the river slows and drops the sediment. This is deposition. Over time it builds a delta.

### #9: Is It Metamorphic?

A geologist finds a rock with bands of flat, aligned minerals. The minerals are the same ones that a nearby shale contains, but they are larger. Is the rock igneous, sedimentary, or metamorphic?

**Solution:**
The aligned bands mean foliation, which comes from heat and pressure on a solid rock. Shale has changed into a foliated rock such as slate or schist. The rock is metamorphic.

### #10: A Valid Rock-Cycle Pathway

Describe a plausible pathway that turns granite into quartzite through a sedimentary stage.

**Solution:**
1. Granite at the surface weathers, and erosion carries the grains away. Quartz resists chemical weathering well, so it is left as grains.
2. Quartz-rich sand is deposited, compacted, and cemented into sandstone.
3. If the sandstone is later buried and heated and squeezed enough without melting, it becomes quartzite.
4. The path works because the parent rock of quartzite is a quartz-rich sandstone. Marble comes mainly from carbonate rocks such as limestone, so quartz sand would not give marble.

### #11: A Shortcut in the Cycle

Describe one path from an igneous rock back to an igneous rock.

**Solution:**
1. The igneous rock is buried deeply and heated until it melts. It becomes magma.
2. The magma moves up and cools. A new igneous rock forms.

The rock never became sedimentary or metamorphic. The rock cycle has no required order.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Classify a rock by **how it formed**, not by how it looks. Cooled melt is igneous. Sediment made into rock, or minerals that come out of water, is sedimentary. Solid rock changed by heat, pressure, or fluids is metamorphic.

For texture, remember that bigger crystals usually mean slower cooling. No crystals at all points to very fast cooling, as with obsidian.

Two metamorphic pairs to remember are limestone to marble and sandstone to quartzite. Shale to slate is a third, and it is foliated.

To identify a mineral, test hardness first, then streak, cleavage or fracture, and luster. Do not rely on color.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Forgetting "Inorganic" in the Mineral Definition

> Not every natural crystal or solid counts as a mineral.

A mineral must be inorganic and have an orderly structure and a definite makeup. Coal is natural and solid, but it comes from plants, so it is not a mineral. Obsidian is natural, but it has no crystals.

### #2: Trusting Color

> Color alone does not name a mineral.

Quartz comes in many colors. A mineral's streak can differ from its color, and streak is usually steadier. Streak is not useful for a mineral harder than the plate.

### #3: Saying Metamorphism Means Melting

> Metamorphism changes a rock while it stays largely solid.

If the rock melts completely and later cools, the new rock is igneous.

### #4: Mixing Up Weathering and Erosion

> Weathering breaks rock down in place, and erosion moves the pieces.

A test may describe a rock cracking in place and call it erosion. That is weathering.

### #5: Believing the Rock Cycle Has a Fixed Order

> The rock cycle is a network, not a required sequence.

A rock can skip stages or return to the same type. A path still needs the right process and material. A quartz-rich sandstone becomes quartzite, not marble.
