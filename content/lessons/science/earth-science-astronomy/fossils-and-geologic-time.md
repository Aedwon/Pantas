## Core Concept

A **fossil** is any preserved trace of an ancient living thing: bones, shells, footprints, or imprints left in rock. Fossils are usually found inside sedimentary rock, since that rock forms in layers that can trap and preserve remains before they decay.

### Relative Dating

**Stratigraphy** is the study of rock layers (called **strata**). The main tool for relative dating is the **Law of Superposition**: in an undisturbed sequence of sedimentary rock layers, each layer is younger than the layer below it, and older than the layer above it. This makes sense because sediment piles up over time, with the newest material always on top.

Relative dating tells us the order in which rock layers (and the fossils within them) formed, but it does not tell us an exact age in years.

### Absolute (Radiometric) Dating

To find an actual number of years, scientists use **radiometric dating**. Certain elements are radioactive, meaning their atoms slowly and steadily break down (decay) into a different, stable element over time. The **half-life** of a radioactive element is the time it takes for half of it to decay into that stable form.

By measuring how much of the original radioactive element remains in a rock, compared to how much of the stable decayed element has built up, scientists can calculate how many half-lives have passed, and therefore the rock's actual age in years.

### The Geologic Time Scale

Earth's $4.6$-billion-year history is organized into the **geologic time scale**, divided into units from largest to smallest: **eons**, **eras**, **periods**, and **epochs**.

The largest chunk of Earth's history, called the **Precambrian**, covers roughly the first $4$ billion years, before complex life became common. After the Precambrian comes the **Phanerozoic** eon, divided into three eras:

- **Paleozoic** ("old life"): includes the first fish, land plants, and early reptiles.
- **Mesozoic** ("middle life"): the age of the dinosaurs, also when the first mammals and flowering plants appeared.
- **Cenozoic** ("new life"): the current era, often called the age of mammals, which is when humans evolved.

## Examples

### #1: Applying the Law of Superposition

A rock cliff shows four visible layers, labeled W, X, Y, and Z from bottom to top, with no signs of disturbance. Which layer is the oldest, and which is the youngest?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: a rock cliff cross-section with four horizontal layers stacked and labeled bottom to top W, X, Y, Z; W (bottom) marked oldest and Z (top) marked youngest -->


**Solution:**
Apply the Law of Superposition directly.

1. In an undisturbed sequence, the bottom layer is the oldest, since it was deposited first.
2. The top layer is the youngest, since it was deposited most recently.
3. Layer W (bottom) is the oldest. Layer Z (top) is the youngest.

### #2: Reading a Fossil's Relative Age

A trilobite fossil is found in Layer W (the bottom layer), and a mammal fossil is found in Layer Z (the top layer) of the same rock cliff from Example 1. What can you conclude about which organism lived first?

**Solution:**
Connect the rock layer's age to the fossil trapped inside it.

1. A fossil's age matches the age of the rock layer it is found in.
2. Since Layer W is older than Layer Z, the trilobite (in Layer W) must have lived before the mammal (in Layer Z).
3. This matches what we know from the geologic time scale: trilobites are common Paleozoic fossils, while most mammals appeared much later, in the Cenozoic.

### #3: Half-Life Calculation

A radioactive element has a half-life of $1$ billion years. A rock sample originally contained a certain amount of this element, and now only one-quarter of that original amount remains (the rest has decayed into a stable element). How old is the rock?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: a decay curve (or bar steps) showing the parent element halving each billion years: 100% → 50% (1 half-life) → 25% (2 half-lives) → so one-quarter remaining means 2 billion years -->


**Solution:**
Figure out how many half-lives have passed.

1. After one half-life, half of the original amount remains: $\dfrac{1}{2}$.
2. After a second half-life, half of that remaining half is left: $\dfrac{1}{2} \times \dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{1}{4}$.
3. Since $\dfrac{1}{4}$ of the original element remains, two half-lives have passed.
4. Each half-life is $1$ billion years, so the rock is:

$$2 \times 1 \text{ billion years} = 2 \text{ billion years old}$$

### #4: Naming the Era

A rock layer contains fossils of dinosaurs and early flowering plants, but no fossils of any mammals larger than small, shrew-like creatures. Which era does this layer most likely belong to?

**Solution:**
Match the fossil clues to the correct era's defining life forms.

1. Dinosaurs and the first flowering plants are hallmark fossils of the mesozoic era.
2. Only small, shrew-like mammals existed during the Mesozoic; large mammals became dominant only later, in the Cenozoic era, after the dinosaurs went extinct.
3. The answer: Mesozoic era.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For Law of Superposition questions, just remember: Bottom is oldest, top is youngest, like pancakes stacked one at a time: the first one made sits at the bottom of the plate.

For half-life problems, track the fraction remaining by repeatedly halving: $1 \to \dfrac{1}{2} \to \dfrac{1}{4} \to \dfrac{1}{8}$, and count how many halving steps you took. That count is the number of half-lives elapsed; multiply by the half-life length to get the age.

Remember the three Phanerozoic eras in order using their meaning: Paleozoic ("old life," early sea creatures and first land plants), Mesozoic ("middle life," age of dinosaurs), Cenozoic ("new life," age of mammals, our current era).

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Relative Dating Gives Order, Not a Number

> Relative dating (Law of Superposition) tells you which layer is older. Only radiometric dating gives an actual age in years.

A question asking "how many years old is this layer" cannot be answered using superposition alone: that method only orders layers relative to each other. Only radiometric (absolute) dating provides a specific number of years.

### #2: Assuming a Disturbed Sequence Still Follows "Bottom is Oldest"

> The Law of Superposition only applies to undisturbed layers.

If rock layers have been folded, flipped, or faulted by geological forces (which does happen), the simple "bottom is oldest" rule can no longer be trusted without further evidence. Exam questions usually specify "undisturbed" for a reason: watch for that word.

### #3: Miscounting Half-Lives

> Each half-life cuts the remaining amount in half again, not by a fixed equal amount each time.

A common mistake is assuming that if $\dfrac{1}{4}$ remains after $2$ half-lives, then $\dfrac{1}{8}$ would need only $1$ more half-life to reach zero. In fact, decay never reaches exactly zero: every half-life only cuts the current remaining amount in half, forever approaching (but never fully hitting) zero.

### #4: Mixing Up the Three Eras

> Dinosaurs belong to the mesozoic, not the Cenozoic.

Since the Cenozoic is called the "age of mammals," some students mistakenly place dinosaurs there too. Dinosaurs went extinct at the end of the Mesozoic era, and it was only afterward, in the Cenozoic, that mammals became the dominant large land animals.
