## Core Concept

### Fossils

A **fossil** is evidence of past life preserved in rock, ice, amber, or other material. Fossils are found mostly in **sedimentary rock**, which forms when sediment (sand, mud, shell bits) is buried and pressed into layers. Each layer is called a **stratum**, and many layers are called **strata**.

A **body fossil** is a piece of the organism itself, such as a bone, shell, leaf, or pollen grain. A **trace fossil** is a sign of what the organism did, such as a footprint, a burrow, or fossil dung called a **coprolite**.

Fossils do not have to be "turned to stone." Insects in amber and mammoths frozen in ice are fossils too.

Most organisms leave no fossil. Hard parts such as shells and bones, and fast burial in mud or sand, make fossils much more likely. Soft-bodied animals that rot or get eaten are rarely preserved, so the fossil record is incomplete.

### Relative Dating

To **infer** means to work out an answer from evidence. **Relative dating** infers which rocks and events are older or younger, without giving an age in years. It uses three ideas.

**Law of superposition.** In a sequence of sedimentary layers that has not been overturned, each layer is older than the layer above it. New sediment settles on top of older sediment.

**Cross-cutting relationships.** A fault (a crack where rock has slipped) is younger than the rock it cuts. An **intrusion** is magma that pushed into existing rock and then cooled. It is younger than the rock it cuts through. Something must exist before it can be cut.

**Index fossils.** Geologists **correlate** rock layers when they match layers in different places as the same age. An **index fossil** helps with this. It is the fossil of an organism that lived widely across the world, was easy to recognize, and existed for a short span of geologic time. If two distant layers hold the same index fossil, the layers formed within that span.

Each idea has limits. Folding can turn layers upside down, and a fault can push older rock over younger rock. A missing layer, where sediment was eroded away or never deposited, leaves a gap in the record.

Fossils can also be eroded out of an old layer and buried again in a younger one. This is called **reworking**. Geologists therefore check the field evidence before they trust any one clue.

### Numerical Dating

**Numerical dating** gives an age in years. The main method is **radiometric dating**, which uses radioactive decay.

An **isotope** is a version of an element with a certain number of neutrons. Some isotopes are **radioactive**, which means their atoms change over time into atoms of another isotope. The starting isotope is the **parent**. The isotope it changes into is the **daughter**.

The **half-life** is the time it takes for half of the parent atoms in a sample to decay. After one half-life, $\dfrac12$ of the parent is left. After two half-lives, $\dfrac14$ is left, then $\dfrac18$, and so on. Each half-life halves what remains:

$$\text{fraction left}=\left(\dfrac12\right)^{n}$$

Here $n$ is the number of half-lives that have passed. The age of the sample is then:

$$\text{age}=n\times\text{half-life}$$

Different isotopes have very different half-lives. Carbon-14 has a half-life of about $5{,}730$ years. Uranium-238 has a half-life of about $4.47$ billion years.

These formulas work for an ideal sample. It must be a **closed system**, meaning no parent or daughter atoms entered or left after the rock formed. It must also have started with no daughter atoms, or the amount at the start must be known.

Real dating needs more. A scientist measures the amounts of parent and daughter in a sample. Then the scientist uses the isotope's known decay rate.

Some parents do not turn into a stable daughter in one step. They pass through several unstable daughters first, as uranium-238 does on its way to lead-206. The decay is then counted from the parent to the final stable product.

The method must also fit the material:

- Carbon-14 dating works on material that was once alive, such as wood, charcoal, bone, or shell. It is useful back to about $50{,}000$ years. After that, too little carbon-14 is left to measure.
- Rocks that are millions or billions of years old are dated with isotopes of long half-life, such as uranium-238.
- Radiometric dating usually dates a mineral at the time it formed from molten rock. Igneous rock, which forms from cooled magma or lava, is dated well. Most sedimentary rock is made of old grains, so those grains show the age of their source rock, not of the layer. Geologists often date volcanic ash layers above and below a sedimentary layer to give it an age range.

### Geologic Time

Earth is about $4.54$ billion years old. Scientists divide this time into units, from largest to smallest: **eon**, **era**, **period**, and **epoch**.

There are four eons: the Hadean, the Archean, the Proterozoic, and the Phanerozoic. **Precambrian** is an informal name for the first three together, which came before the Phanerozoic. It is about $88\%$ of Earth's history.

The Phanerozoic Eon began about $539$ million years ago. It has three eras, from oldest to youngest: the Paleozoic, the Mesozoic, and the Cenozoic. A **mass extinction** is a short span of time in which a large share of living species die out. Mass extinctions mark the end of the Paleozoic and of the Mesozoic.

Life in each era:

- **Precambrian:** The oldest widely accepted fossils of single-celled life are about $3.5$ billion years old. Large animals came late, near the end of the Precambrian or after it.
- **Paleozoic (about $539$ to $252$ million years ago):** Many kinds of sea animals appeared early in the era. Fish, land plants, amphibians, and reptiles came later. It ended in the largest mass extinction known, about $252$ million years ago.
- **Mesozoic (about $252$ to $66$ million years ago):** Dinosaurs lived through this era, and early mammals lived alongside them. Flowering plants spread in the Cretaceous Period, the last period of the era. It ended about $66$ million years ago in a mass extinction that wiped out the non-bird dinosaurs.
- **Cenozoic (about $66$ million years ago to today):** Mammals and birds became very diverse. Humans appeared late, in the last tiny part of the era.

Calling Mesozoic mammals "small shrew-like animals" oversimplifies them. They were more varied than that.

## Examples

### #1: Relative Age from Superposition

Four sedimentary layers have not been overturned. They are labeled W at the bottom, then X, then Y, with Z at the top. Which layer is oldest, and which is youngest?

**Solution:**

1. The layers have not been overturned, so superposition applies.
2. The bottom layer was laid down first, so W is oldest.
3. The top layer was laid down last, so Z is youngest.

The order from oldest to youngest is W, X, Y, Z.

### #2: Ordering Events with Cross-Cutting Relationships

A cliff shows four horizontal sedimentary layers: P at the bottom, then Q, R, and S at the top. A fault cuts through P, Q, and R. It stops at the bottom of S, and S is not broken. A dark igneous dike (a sheet of cooled magma) cuts through all four layers. What is the order of events, from first to last?

**Solution:**

1. Layers P, Q, and R formed first, in that order, by superposition.
2. The fault cuts P, Q, and R, so it is younger than all three.
3. The fault does not cut S, so S formed after the fault, on top of the broken rock.
4. The dike cuts all four layers, so it is younger than S.

The order is P, Q, R, then the fault, then S, then the dike.

### #3: Using an Index Fossil Carefully

The same index fossil is found in two undisturbed sedimentary beds that are hundreds of kilometers apart. What can geologists infer, and what can they not infer?

**Solution:**

1. The organism lived only during a short span of time.
2. Both beds hold its fossil, so both formed during that span. The beds can be correlated.
3. The fossil does not give an age in years. A numerical age needs radiometric dating.
4. Geologists also check that the fossil was not reworked from an older layer.

### #4: Half-Life Reasoning

A radioactive parent isotope has a half-life of $1$ billion years. In an ideal closed system, what fraction of the original parent remains after $3$ half-lives?

**Solution:**

1. Each half-life halves the parent that remains:

$$1\to\dfrac12\to\dfrac14\to\dfrac18$$

2. After $3$ half-lives, $\dfrac18$ of the original parent remains.

### #5: Inferring a Numerical Age from Parent and Daughter

An igneous rock holds a parent isotope with a half-life of $1$ billion years. A measurement finds $3$ daughter atoms for every $1$ parent atom. The rock started with no daughter atoms, and it stayed a closed system. How old is the rock?

**Solution:**

1. Every daughter atom came from a parent atom. So the rock began with $1+3=4$ parts of parent.
2. Only $1$ of those $4$ parts is still parent:

$$\dfrac14=\left(\dfrac12\right)^{2}$$

3. So $n=2$ half-lives have passed.
4. Multiply by the half-life:

$$\text{age}=2\times 1\text{ billion years}=2\text{ billion years}$$

The rock is about $2$ billion years old.

### #6: Carbon-14 Dating and Its Limit

A piece of ancient charcoal has $\dfrac18$ of the carbon-14 that living wood has. The half-life of carbon-14 is $5{,}730$ years. How old is the charcoal? Could carbon-14 date a $200$-million-year-old fossil shell?

**Solution:**

1. The fraction left is $\dfrac18=\left(\dfrac12\right)^{3}$, so $n=3$ half-lives have passed.
2. Multiply by the half-life:

$$\text{age}=3\times 5{,}730=17{,}190\text{ years}$$

3. The charcoal is about $17{,}000$ years old.
4. A shell that old has gone through far more than $50{,}000$ years, so almost no carbon-14 is left to measure. Carbon-14 cannot date it. A long-lived isotope such as uranium-238, in igneous rock near the fossil, can give an age range.

### #7: Placing Events on the Geologic Time Scale

Which list puts these events in order from oldest to youngest? The events are the first flowering plants, the first land plants, and the extinction of the non-bird dinosaurs.

**Solution:**

1. The first land plants appeared in the Paleozoic Era.
2. The first flowering plants spread in the Cretaceous Period, near the end of the Mesozoic Era.
3. The non-bird dinosaurs died out at the end of the Mesozoic Era, about $66$ million years ago. This was after flowering plants had appeared.

The order is the first land plants, the first flowering plants, and then the extinction of the non-bird dinosaurs.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For relative-dating questions, first find what the clue compares. Superposition compares stacked layers. Cross-cutting compares a feature with the rock it cuts. An index fossil compares layers in different places.

To order events, list the layers from the bottom by superposition. Then place each cut feature after the rocks it cuts, and before any layer it does not cut that lies across it. These steps assume the layers were not overturned.

For half-life questions that give only a fraction, repeat the halving. Count the halvings to get $n$, then multiply by the half-life if an age is needed.

If a question gives a parent to daughter ratio, add the two to find the starting amount. A ratio of $1:1$ means $1$ half-life, $1:3$ means $2$, and $1:7$ means $3$. This works for a closed system that started with no daughter atoms.

To place life on the time scale, link each era to its main event. The Paleozoic had the spread of sea animals and then land life. The Mesozoic had dinosaurs. The Cenozoic had the spread of mammals and birds.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: "Precambrian" Is Not One Formal Eon

> Precambrian is not a single eon.

It is an informal name for time before the Phanerozoic. It includes the Hadean, Archean, and Proterozoic eons.

### #2: Older Rock Is Not Always Lower

> The bottom layer is oldest only if the layers were not overturned.

Folding can turn a stack upside down. A fault can slide older rock over younger rock. Check for signs of this in the rock.

### #3: Not Every Fossil Is an Index Fossil

> A fossil helps date a layer well when its organism lived briefly over a wide area.

An organism that lived for hundreds of millions of years, or only in one small place, cannot match layers well. Reworked fossils can also look older than the layer they sit in.

### #4: Half-Life Does Not Mean the Sample Is Gone

> After one half-life, half the parent is left, not none.

Two half-lives leave $\dfrac14$, not zero. Also, the daughter is not always stable right away, since some decay chains pass through unstable daughters.

### #5: Carbon-14 Is Not for Rocks

> Carbon-14 dates once-living material younger than about $50{,}000$ years.

Do not use it for an old rock or a very old fossil. Use isotopes with half-lives of millions or billions of years instead.
