## Core Concept

A **fossil** is evidence of past life preserved in a geologic context. Fossils include body remains such as bones, shells, leaves, and pollen, as well as trace fossils such as footprints, burrows, and coprolites. Fossils do not have to be completely mineralized or “turned to stone.”

### Relative Dating

**Relative dating** places rocks and events in order without assigning a numerical age in years.

- **Law of superposition**: in an undisturbed sequence of sedimentary layers, lower layers are older than layers above them.
- **Cross-cutting relationships**: a fault or intrusion is younger than the rock it cuts.
- **Index fossils**: fossils that are widespread and limited to a relatively short geologic time interval can help correlate the relative ages of rock layers in different places.

These principles have exceptions and complications—layers can be overturned or faulted, and fossils can sometimes be eroded from older rocks and redeposited in younger sediment—so geologists interpret them together with field evidence.

### Absolute/Numerical Dating

**Radiometric dating** estimates numerical ages by measuring radioactive parent isotopes and their daughter products in suitable minerals or rocks. A **half-life** is the time required for half of a radioactive parent isotope in a closed system to decay.

A parent isotope does **not** necessarily decay directly to a stable daughter in one step. Some dating systems involve decay chains with radioactive intermediate daughters before a stable end product is reached. The dating calculation depends on the specific isotope system, decay constants, mineral behavior, and whether the system remained sufficiently closed.

### Geologic Time

Earth is about **4.54 billion years** old. The informal term **Precambrian** covers the enormous span before the Phanerozoic Eon and includes the Hadean, Archean, and Proterozoic eons. The **Phanerozoic Eon** contains the Paleozoic, Mesozoic, and Cenozoic eras.

The Mesozoic is known for dinosaurs and also includes a diverse history of early mammals and the rise of flowering plants. The Cenozoic follows the end-Cretaceous mass extinction and includes major radiations of mammals and birds; calling the Mesozoic mammals merely “small shrew-like mammals” oversimplifies their diversity.

## Examples

### #1: Relative Age from Superposition

Three undisturbed sedimentary layers are labeled A at the bottom, B in the middle, and C at the top. Which is oldest?

**Solution:**
Under the law of superposition, A is oldest, then B, then C.

### #2: Using an Index Fossil Carefully

The same well-established index fossil occurs in two undisturbed sedimentary beds hundreds of kilometers apart. What can geologists infer?

**Solution:**
The fossil can support correlation of the beds to the geologic interval in which that organism lived. It does not by itself provide an exact numerical age, and the geologic context must be checked for reworking or disturbance.

### #3: Half-Life Reasoning

A radioactive parent isotope has a half-life of $1$ billion years. In an ideal closed system, what fraction of the original parent remains after $3$ half-lives?

**Solution:**
Each half-life halves the remaining parent:

$$1\to\dfrac12\to\dfrac14\to\dfrac18$$

After $3$ half-lives, $\dfrac18$ of the original parent remains.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For relative-dating questions, identify what the principle actually compares: superposition compares stacked layers; cross-cutting relationships compare a feature with the rocks it cuts; index fossils correlate intervals.

For half-life problems involving only the fraction of parent remaining, use repeated halving. Real radiometric dating uses isotope measurements and calibrated decay constants, not merely visual inspection of a rock.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: “Precambrian” Is Not One Formal Eon

> Precambrian is an informal umbrella term for time before the Phanerozoic.

It includes the Hadean, Archean, and Proterozoic eons.

### #2: Every Daughter Product Is Not Immediately Stable

> Radioactive decay can proceed through a chain of unstable daughter isotopes before reaching a stable end product.

### #3: Fossils Can Be Reworked

> A fossil found in a sedimentary layer is usually interpreted in its depositional context, but older fossils can sometimes be eroded and redeposited into younger sediments.

Use multiple lines of stratigraphic evidence when precise correlation matters.
