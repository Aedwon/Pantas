## Core Concept

The Solar System holds many kinds of objects with different names. To name an object, ask what it is made of, where it is, and what it is doing. This lesson sorts objects by those three clues. Stars and galaxies are in the lesson Stars and Galaxies.

Two words come up again and again. **Gravity** is the pull that masses have on each other. An **orbit** is the curved path an object follows around another object because of gravity.

### The Solar System: Planets and Moons

The **Solar System** is the Sun and all the objects that orbit it. An **AU** (astronomical unit) is the average distance from Earth to the Sun, about $150$ million km.

The International Astronomical Union (IAU) is the group that sets official names in astronomy. It says a **planet** orbits the Sun, has enough gravity to pull itself into a nearly round shape, and has cleared its orbit.

To clear its orbit means the planet is far more massive than everything else near its orbit and controls it. Other bodies there were swept up, flung away, or became its moons. A few small bodies may stay, held in steady paths by the planet's gravity, such as Jupiter's Trojan asteroids.

A **dwarf planet** orbits the Sun and is nearly round, but has not cleared its orbit. It is also not a moon. Pluto and Ceres are examples.

Planets sort into two families by what they are made of. The **terrestrial planets** are Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars. They are small and rocky, with a solid surface.

The giant planets are much larger and have no solid surface to stand on. **Gas giants**, Jupiter and Saturn, are mostly hydrogen and helium. **Ice giants**, Uranus and Neptune, hold more water, ammonia, and methane in a thick layer around a small core. All four giant planets have rings.

Some facts help in comparing planets:

- Mercury is the smallest planet and has no moon.
- Venus has the hottest surface, about $465$ °C, because its thick air traps heat. It has no moon and spins backward compared with most planets.
- Jupiter is the largest planet, about $11$ times as wide as Earth.
- Saturn is so light for its size that its average density is less than the density of water.
- Uranus is tipped on its side, with its axis tilted about $98$ degrees.
- Neptune is the farthest planet from the Sun.

A **natural satellite**, or moon, is a natural object that orbits a planet, dwarf planet, or asteroid. Moons differ a lot. Earth's Moon is rocky and about one quarter as wide as Earth. Ganymede, Jupiter's largest moon, is wider than the planet Mercury.

Many moons are not plain rocky balls. Europa, a moon of Jupiter, has an icy shell over a rocky interior and is thought to hide a salty ocean under the ice. Titan, Saturn's largest moon, has a thick atmosphere. Io, another moon of Jupiter, has many active volcanoes. Mars has two small moons, Phobos and Deimos, which are lumpy and not round.

### Asteroids and Comets

An **asteroid** is a small rocky or metallic body that orbits the Sun and gives off no tail in its normal state. Most asteroids are found in the **main asteroid belt** between Mars and Jupiter, but asteroids also orbit elsewhere. Some come close to Earth, and some share the orbit of a planet, such as the Trojan asteroids of Jupiter.

Asteroids range from small rocks to bodies hundreds of kilometers wide. Some asteroids have their own moons. Ceres, the largest object in the main belt, is a dwarf planet.

A **comet** is a small body of ice and dust that orbits the Sun, often in a long, stretched-out oval. Here "ice" means frozen water and other frozen gases such as carbon dioxide. These ices are **volatile**, which means they turn into gas easily when warmed. Comets come mostly from the cold outer Solar System, beyond Neptune, where the ices stay frozen.

The solid center of a comet is its **nucleus**, usually only a few kilometers wide. When a comet comes near the Sun, sunlight warms the nucleus. The ice turns straight into gas and carries dust away with it.

The gas and dust form a glowing cloud around the nucleus, called the **coma**. They also form one or two tails. A tail is not solid. It is thin gas and dust that shines in sunlight.

The **solar wind** is a stream of charged particles flowing outward from the Sun. It pushes on the gas of a comet, which forms the **ion tail**, a fairly straight, bluish tail. Sunlight itself gives a gentle push to the dust, which forms the **dust tail**, a wider, whitish tail that often curves.

Both tails point away from the Sun. The dust tail curves because the dust keeps some of the comet's motion along its orbit. This means a comet moving away from the Sun has its tails ahead of it.

Comets are active only near the Sun. Far from the Sun, a comet is a cold, dark, inactive nucleus with no coma or tail. Halley's comet returns about every $76$ years.

### Meteoroid, Meteor, Meteorite

These words describe related but different things:

- A **meteoroid** is a small natural rock or grain traveling through space. It is smaller than an asteroid, and about one meter wide is a common dividing line. Meteoroids often come from asteroids or comets.
- A **meteor** is the streak of light that appears when a meteoroid enters an atmosphere at high speed. It heats the air around it and glows. People often call it a shooting star, but it is not a star.
- A **meteorite** is material from the incoming object that survives the trip and reaches the ground.

A **meteor shower** happens when Earth passes through a stream of debris, so many meteoroids enter the atmosphere in a short time. Many streams are left by comets. The Perseids come from comet Swift-Tuttle, and the Geminids come from an asteroid named Phaethon.

Meteorites come in three main types: stony, iron, and stony-iron. Stony meteorites are the most common. Some meteorites found on Earth came from the Moon or Mars.

## Examples

### #1: Meteoroid, Meteor, or Meteorite?

A small rocky particle is moving through space toward Earth. It enters the atmosphere and produces a bright streak, and a fragment later lands on the ground. Name each stage.

**Solution:**
1. In space, before it meets the atmosphere: meteoroid.
2. The bright streak in the atmosphere: meteor.
3. The surviving piece on the ground: meteorite.

### #2: Asteroid or Comet?

An object orbits the Sun. As it approaches the Sun, it becomes surrounded by a coma with gas and dust tails. Is it better classified as an asteroid or a comet?

**Solution:**
1. A coma and tails come from ice turning into gas when warmed.
2. This activity is typical of a comet.
3. An ordinary asteroid is rocky or metallic and has no coma or tail.

The object is a comet.

### #3: Which Way Does the Tail Point?

A comet is moving away from the Sun after passing close to it. Where do its tails point?

**Solution:**
1. Solar wind and sunlight push material away from the Sun.
2. So the tails point away from the Sun, whatever direction the comet moves.
3. The comet is also moving away from the Sun, so the tails point ahead of the comet along its path.

The tails are not a wake trailing behind the comet like the wake of a boat.

### #4: Planet or Dwarf Planet?

An object orbits the Sun, is nearly round, and moves among many other objects in the asteroid belt. It is not far more massive than they are, and it does not control the belt. How does the IAU classify it?

**Solution:**
1. It orbits the Sun and is nearly round.
2. It is not far more massive than the other bodies near its orbit, and it does not control them, so it has not cleared its orbit.
3. Sharing space with other bodies does not decide this alone. Jupiter has Trojan asteroids in its orbit, but it controls them.
4. A round body that orbits the Sun and has not cleared its orbit is a dwarf planet.

It is a dwarf planet. Ceres is an example.

### #5: Which Family of Planets?

A planet has no solid surface. It is made mostly of hydrogen and helium and has rings. Is it a terrestrial planet or a gas giant?

**Solution:**
1. A terrestrial planet is rocky and has a solid surface, so this planet is not one.
2. A planet that is mostly hydrogen and helium with no solid surface is a gas giant.
3. Having rings does not decide the family, because all four giant planets have rings.

It is a gas giant, such as Jupiter or Saturn.

### #6: Are All Moons Alike?

A student says that every moon is a small, round, rocky ball like Earth's Moon. Give two moons that show the statement is wrong.

**Solution:**
1. Europa has an icy shell over its rocky interior, so it is not a plain rocky ball like Earth's Moon.
2. Phobos, a moon of Mars, is small and lumpy, so it is not round.
3. Ganymede is even wider than the planet Mercury, so moons are not always small.

Moons differ in size, shape, and make-up.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

**Meteor terms.** Use location. A meteoroid is in space, a meteor is the streak in the atmosphere, and a meteorite is on the ground.

**Asteroid or comet.** Both orbit the Sun. Look at what the object is made of and whether it is active: rocky objects without tails are usually asteroids, and icy objects that grow a coma and tails near the Sun are comets. A few objects blur the line, such as asteroids that sometimes shed dust, so use this as a guide rather than a law.

**Tail direction.** A comet's tails point away from the Sun. This holds even when the comet moves toward or away from the Sun.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Calling the Space Rock a Meteor at Every Stage

> A meteor is the streak of light, not the name for the rock at every stage.

Use meteoroid in space and meteorite for a piece that reaches the ground.

### #2: Assuming Every Asteroid Is in the Main Belt

> The main belt holds many asteroids, but asteroids also orbit elsewhere.

Some are near-Earth asteroids and some are Trojans. Not every body in the belt is an asteroid either, since Ceres is a dwarf planet.

### #3: Treating a Comet Tail Like a Wake

> A tail points away from the Sun, not always behind the comet.

Solar wind and sunlight push the material, so the tail's direction depends on where the Sun is. The tail leads the comet when it moves away from the Sun.

### #4: Treating Pluto as a Planet and Moons as Alike

> Pluto is a dwarf planet, and moons are not all small rocky balls.

The IAU says a planet must have cleared its orbit, and Pluto has not. Moons vary: some are icy, some have air, and some are lumpy.
