## Core Concept

### Seasonal Constellations

A **constellation** is a group of stars that people have named as a pattern in the sky, such as Orion or Scorpius. We usually see the stars only when the sky is dark, at night, when our side of Earth faces away from the Sun. The stars in the direction of the Sun are lost in daylight.

As Earth revolves, the direction of the Sun against the background stars changes through the year. So the part of the sky that we face at night changes too.

Take a constellation near the Sun's yearly path across the sky, such as Orion or Scorpius. If it is high at midnight in one season, it lies in the Sun's direction about six months later. Daylight then hides it.

If it is high in the evening, it is seen in the dark sky before sunrise about six months later.

The **north celestial pole** is the point in the sky that Earth's axis points toward in the north. The **south celestial pole** is the matching point in the south. Earth's rotation makes the stars seem to circle around them. Precession, below, explains more.

Constellations close to a celestial pole can break this rule. Near the north celestial pole, a constellation never sets in places far enough north of the equator, and near the south celestial pole the same holds far enough south. Such a constellation is above the horizon all year.

We can see it on any clear night when the sky gets dark. Where the Sun does not set, the sky does not get dark.

For example, Orion is easy to see in the evening in the early months of the year, from the Philippines. Around June, the Sun is in Orion's part of the sky, so Orion is lost in daylight.

Scorpius is seen in the evening in the middle of the year. Rotation moves the stars across the sky each night. Revolution changes which stars are in the night sky as the months pass.

## Examples

### #1: Seasonal Constellations

Orion is seen high in the evening sky from the Philippines in the early months of the year, but not in the evening in the middle of the year. What explains this?

**Solution:**
1. We see the stars at night, when our side of Earth faces away from the Sun.
2. Earth revolves, so through the year the night side faces different parts of the sky.
3. Around June, the Sun lies in Orion's part of the sky, so daylight hides Orion. So Orion is not seen in the evening then.
4. Rotation alone cannot explain this, because rotation repeats every day.
