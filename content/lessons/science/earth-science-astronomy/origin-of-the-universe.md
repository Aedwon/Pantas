## Core Concept

Two different questions are easy to mix up. One asks how the whole universe began and changed. The other asks how our own Solar System formed. Science answers them with two different models, and each model rests on its own evidence.

### The Big Bang Model

The **Big Bang model** says the early universe was extremely hot and dense. Since then it has been expanding and cooling for about $13.8$ billion years. A **galaxy** is a huge group of stars, gas, and dust held together by gravity. The Milky Way is our galaxy.

The name is misleading. The Big Bang was not a bomb going off at one place inside empty space. In the model, space itself expands, and galaxies are carried apart as it stretches. No center of the expansion has been found in ordinary space.

A good picture is a loaf of raisin bread rising in an oven. As the dough swells, every raisin moves away from every other raisin. No raisin is the center, and the dough is the stretching space.

### Evidence 1: Redshift and Expansion

Light behaves like a wave, and its **wavelength** is the distance from one wave crest to the next. A **spectrum** is light spread out by wavelength. Atoms of each element absorb or emit light at fixed wavelengths, which show up as dark or bright lines in a spectrum.

When the wavelengths of a galaxy's light are longer than the same lines measured in a laboratory, the light is **redshifted**. Red light has the longest wavelength of visible light, so stretching moves lines toward the red end. A shift toward shorter wavelengths is a **blueshift**.

Redshift is measured as a number $z$:

$$z = \dfrac{\lambda_{\text{observed}} - \lambda_{\text{emitted}}}{\lambda_{\text{emitted}}}$$

Here $\lambda$ (the Greek letter lambda) stands for wavelength. A larger $z$ means the light was stretched more.

In the 1920s, astronomers found that most galaxies are redshifted. Edwin Hubble showed in $1929$ that, on large scales, farther galaxies have larger redshifts. This is what an expanding universe predicts. The light of a distant galaxy travels for a long time, and the space it crosses stretches meanwhile, so its wavelengths stretch too.

This rule has limits. Galaxies that are close together are held by gravity, and gravity beats the expansion at that scale. The Andromeda galaxy is blueshifted because it is moving toward the Milky Way. The expansion is not seen inside a galaxy, inside the Solar System, or between neighbors that gravity binds.

### Evidence 2: The Cosmic Microwave Background

Early on, the universe was so hot that matter was a glowing mix of charged particles, and light kept being scattered. When the universe was about $380{,}000$ years old, it had cooled enough for atoms to form. Light could then travel freely across space.

That light is still arriving from every direction. It is called the **cosmic microwave background (CMB)**. Expansion has stretched its wavelengths so much that it is now microwaves, with a temperature of about $2.7$ K. Two features matter: it is almost the same in every direction, and its spectrum matches the glow of a hot, dense early universe that has cooled.

Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson found the CMB by accident in $1965$. Maps later showed tiny differences in temperature, about one part in $100{,}000$. Gravity later pulled matter together at the slightly denser spots, and these became galaxies.

### Evidence 3: Light Elements

In the first few minutes, the universe was hot and dense enough for nuclei to fuse, a process called **Big Bang nucleosynthesis**. A **nucleus** is the center of an atom, and **fusion** means joining small nuclei into a bigger one.

The model predicts that this made mostly hydrogen and helium, with tiny amounts of deuterium (a heavy form of hydrogen) and lithium. The prediction for helium is about one quarter of the ordinary matter by mass. Observations of very old gas clouds match this.

The universe cooled and thinned too fast to build much more. Heavier elements such as carbon, oxygen, and iron formed later, mainly inside stars and in star explosions. So the match is evidence for the early hot stage, but it does not explain every element.

### What the Model Does Not Say

The Big Bang model describes how the universe changed from a hot, dense state. It does not say what caused the Big Bang or what came before the earliest times that current physics can describe. It also does not describe how the Solar System formed.

### Formation of the Solar System

The Solar System formed about $4.6$ billion years ago, billions of years after the Big Bang. The leading explanation is the **nebular model**, from ideas of Immanuel Kant and Pierre-Simon Laplace in the 1700s. A **nebula** is a cloud of gas and dust in space. The cloud here was a cold part of a **molecular cloud**, a dense cloud where atoms join into molecules.

First, gravity pulls a region of the cloud inward, and it collapses. A spinning cloud that shrinks spins faster. This is called conservation of **angular momentum**, and an ice skater pulling in her arms shows the same effect.

The spinning flattens the cloud into a **protoplanetary disk**, a flat, rotating disk of gas and dust. Most of the material falls to the center and forms the young Sun. Dust grains in the disk stick together in a process called **accretion**, which means growing by gathering more material. They build bodies called **planetesimals** (small bodies kilometers across), and then larger planets.

Near the Sun it was warm, so mostly rock and metal could stay solid, and this explains the small rocky planets. Farther out, ice could also form, so large planets could grow and gather gas. The details of planet formation are still being studied. The nebular model is a simplified picture of a complicated process.

Several observations support the model. All eight planets orbit the Sun in the same direction, in nearly the same flat plane, as a disk would produce. Radioactive dating of meteorites gives ages of about $4.6$ billion years. Telescopes also see disks of gas and dust around young stars elsewhere.

## Examples

### #1: Big Bang Misconception

A diagram shows all matter exploding outward from one point into an already existing empty universe. What is wrong with this picture?

**Solution:**
1. The Big Bang was not an explosion from a center into surrounding empty space.
2. In the model, space itself expands from an earlier hot, dense state.
3. No center of expansion has been found in ordinary space. Observers in any galaxy would see other distant galaxies moving away.

### #2: Computing Redshift

In a laboratory, one spectral line of a gas has a wavelength of $600$ nm. In the light of a distant galaxy, the same line is at $660$ nm. What is the redshift $z$, and what does it suggest?

**Solution:**
1. Find the change in wavelength.
$$660 - 600 = 60 \text{ nm}$$
2. Divide by the emitted wavelength.
$$z = \dfrac{60}{600} = 0.1$$
3. The wavelength is stretched by $10\%$. The galaxy is redshifted, which fits space expanding while its light traveled.

### #3: A Blueshifted Galaxy

The light of the Andromeda galaxy shows lines at slightly shorter wavelengths than in the laboratory. Does this contradict the expanding universe?

**Solution:**
1. Shorter wavelengths mean a blueshift. Andromeda is moving toward us.
2. Andromeda and the Milky Way are close, and gravity holds them in the same group. Gravity beats the expansion at this scale.
3. The expansion pattern of redshift growing with distance applies to galaxies far apart, so there is no contradiction.

### #4: What the CMB Tells Us

Why is the cosmic microwave background evidence for the Big Bang model?

**Solution:**
1. The CMB is relic light from the time when the young universe cooled enough for atoms to form, about $380{,}000$ years after the Big Bang.
2. A hot, dense early universe predicts light that fills the sky almost evenly. The CMB is nearly uniform in every direction.
3. Its measured spectrum matches that of a hot early stage, stretched by expansion into microwaves.

### #5: Light Elements as Evidence

Which observation about the elements supports the Big Bang model, and which elements does it not explain?

**Solution:**
1. The model predicts that early fusion made about one quarter helium by mass, and most of the rest hydrogen.
2. Observations of very old gas clouds match these helium and hydrogen amounts well.
3. The universe cooled too fast to build heavy elements. Carbon, oxygen, and iron formed mainly in stars, so the match does not cover them.

### #6: From Cloud to Planets

Put these stages of Solar System formation in order: protoplanetary disk, molecular cloud collapse, planetesimals, planets.

**Solution:**
1. A region of a molecular cloud collapses under gravity.
2. The collapsing material spins faster and flattens into a protoplanetary disk, with the young Sun at the center.
3. Dust and solid particles accrete into planetesimals.
4. Continued growth and interactions produce planets and other bodies.

### #7: Which Model Fits?

Which model does each statement support? (a) Distant galaxies are redshifted. (b) The planets orbit the Sun in nearly one plane. (c) Helium is about one quarter of ordinary matter by mass.

**Solution:**
1. Redshift of distant galaxies supports the Big Bang model of the whole universe.
2. Orbits in one plane fit a flat protoplanetary disk, so (b) supports the nebular model.
3. The helium amount is a prediction of Big Bang nucleosynthesis, so (c) supports the Big Bang model.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Sort each question by scale. Whole universe, galaxies, and the early hot stage point to the **Big Bang model**. The Sun, planets, and disks point to the **nebular model**, which is not a part of the Big Bang model.

For Big Bang evidence, remember **redshift + CMB + light elements**. For the Solar System, remember **cloud collapse, spinning disk, accretion**.

For redshift, a larger $z$ means more stretching. Use it as a sign of expansion only for galaxies far enough away that gravity does not bind them to us.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: The Big Bang Was Not a Firecracker in Empty Space

> The Big Bang was an expansion of space itself, and no center has been found.

The explosion picture gives the wrong geometry. A choice that says matter flew outward from a point into empty space describes the wrong model.

### #2: Not Every Galaxy Is Redshifted

> Redshift grows with distance on large scales, but nearby galaxies can be blueshifted.

Andromeda is blueshifted because gravity and its own motion matter more than expansion at that distance. A statement that all galaxies are redshifted is too strong.

### #3: The Big Bang Did Not Make Every Element

> Light elements came from the Big Bang. Heavier elements formed mainly in stars.

Hydrogen, helium, and tiny amounts of lithium formed in the first minutes. Carbon, oxygen, and iron came later from stars and their explosions.

### #4: The CMB Came Before the First Stars

> The CMB light was released about $380{,}000$ years after the Big Bang, before the first stars formed.

It is relic light from the early universe, so it is not starlight.

### #5: A Nearby Supernova Is a Possible Trigger, Not a Settled Fact

> Some models suggest a nearby star explosion helped start the collapse of the cloud.

This is one proposed trigger. The nebular model is that a cloud collapsed, flattened, and built planets. It does not need one trigger to be proven.
