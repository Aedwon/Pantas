## Core Concept

Stars and galaxies are the largest objects in this topic. To name one, ask what it is made of and how big it is. This lesson sorts stars and galaxies by those clues. Planets, moons, asteroids, comets, and meteors are in the lesson Heavenly Bodies (Planets, Moons, Asteroids, Comets, Meteors), and the Solar System is explained there.

Two words come up again and again. **Gravity** is the pull that masses have on each other. An **orbit** is the curved path an object follows around another object because of gravity.

### Stars

A **star** is a huge ball of very hot gas that makes its own light. In the hottest gas, atoms break into charged pieces. Hot gas in this state is called **plasma**.

A star shines because of **nuclear fusion**. In fusion, the centers of small atoms join to make a bigger atom, and this releases energy. In most of a star's life, it fuses hydrogen into helium in its core. Our Sun is a star.

A star's own gravity holds the ball together. The star's gas pushes outward as it heats up. The two effects balance for most of the star's life.

### Comparing Stars

Stars differ in color, temperature, size, and brightness. These properties are linked, and they let us compare stars.

**Color and surface temperature.** The color of a star shows its surface temperature. A blue-white star is the hottest, with a surface above about $10{,}000$ °C. A white star is cooler, a yellow star like the Sun is cooler still, and an orange star is cooler than that.

A red star is the coolest, with a surface below about $3{,}500$ °C. The Sun's surface is about $5{,}500$ °C. Scientists also sort stars into the spectral classes O, B, A, F, G, K, and M, from hottest to coolest. The Sun is class G.

**Size.** Stars come in very different sizes. A red giant is a huge, cool, reddish star. A white dwarf is a small, hot, faint star about as wide as Earth.

**Luminosity and apparent brightness.** **Luminosity** is the total energy a star gives off each second. **Apparent brightness** is how bright the star looks from Earth.

Apparent brightness depends on luminosity and on distance. A faraway star with high luminosity can look as bright as a nearby star with low luminosity. Luminosity grows with the star's surface temperature and with its size, so a cool red giant can still be very luminous because it is so large.

### How Stars Change

A star is born inside a **nebula**, a cloud of gas and dust. Gravity pulls part of the cloud into a hot, dense ball called a protostar. When the core becomes hot enough, fusion begins and a star is born.

The long, steady stage that follows is called the **main sequence**. The star fuses hydrogen into helium in its core. The Sun is about $4.6$ billion years old and is in this stage.

What happens next depends mostly on the star's mass, which is the amount of matter in it. Massive stars use their fuel fast and live a shorter time than small stars. Red dwarfs, the smallest stars, burn fuel so slowly that they live far longer than the present age of the universe.

When a star like the Sun runs low on core hydrogen, it swells into a red giant. It then blows off its outer layers as a shell of gas called a planetary nebula, which has nothing to do with planets. The hot core left behind is a white dwarf, which slowly cools and fades.

A star many times the Sun's mass swells into a supergiant. It ends in a huge explosion called a **supernova**. The core that remains is a neutron star, a tiny but very dense ball about $20$ km across, or a black hole. A **black hole** is an object so dense that its gravity is too strong for even light to escape.

### Galaxies

A **galaxy** is a huge group of stars, gas, dust, and dark matter held together by gravity. Dark matter does not give off light, but we know it is there because of its pull. A galaxy can hold from thousands to hundreds of billions of stars or more, and one galaxy is much larger than one star.

Distances here are measured in light-years. A **light-year** is the distance light travels in one year, about $9.46$ trillion km. It is a unit of distance, not of time.

The **Milky Way** is the galaxy that holds our Solar System. It is a spiral galaxy about $100{,}000$ light-years across, with at least $100$ billion stars. The nearest large galaxy is Andromeda, about $2.5$ million light-years away.

Galaxies have three common shapes: spiral, elliptical, and irregular. Nearly all the single stars we see at night belong to the Milky Way. A **constellation** is only a pattern that people see in the sky. Its stars are at very different distances and are not a group held together by gravity.

## Examples

### #1: Star or Galaxy?

A telescope shows a fuzzy, spiral-shaped object about $2.5$ million light-years away. It is made of about a trillion points of light. Is it a star or a galaxy?

**Solution:**
1. The object is made of a very large number of stars, so it is a group, not one star.
2. A group of stars, gas, and dust held together by gravity is a galaxy.
3. The spiral shape and the distance match the Andromeda galaxy.

It is a galaxy, not a star.

### #2: Same Brightness, Different Stars

Two stars have the same apparent brightness in the night sky. Star P is $10$ light-years from Earth. Star Q is $100$ light-years away. Which star has the greater luminosity?

**Solution:**
1. Apparent brightness depends on luminosity and on distance.
2. The stars look equally bright, but Q is much farther away.
3. Distance makes Q look dimmer than it is.
4. So Q must give off more energy each second than P.

Star Q has the greater luminosity.

### #3: Hot Star or Cool Star?

Star A is blue-white. Star B is red. Which star has the hotter surface, and what does that say about their colors?

**Solution:**
1. Surface temperature sets a star's color.
2. Blue-white stars have the hottest surfaces. Red stars have the coolest.
3. So Star A is hotter than Star B.

The color shows temperature but not size: a red star can still be very large.

### #4: How Will This Star End?

Star X has about the same mass as the Sun. Star Y has many times the mass of the Sun. Compare how the two stars end.

**Solution:**
1. Mass decides a star's path.
2. Star X ends as a red giant, then sheds a planetary nebula and leaves a white dwarf.
3. Star Y ends as a supergiant, then explodes as a supernova and leaves a neutron star or a black hole.
4. Star Y also uses its fuel faster, so it lives a shorter time.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

**Stars.** Surface temperature sets color, and a star's mass mostly decides its life story. Use these two links first when comparing two stars.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Judging Stars by How They Look

> A bright star in the sky is not always a luminous or hot star.

A star can look bright because it is close, not because it gives off a lot of energy. A cool red star can also be larger and more luminous than a small hot one.

### #2: Mixing Up Stars, Galaxies, and Constellations

> A galaxy holds many stars, and a constellation is only a sky pattern.

The stars in a constellation are not held together by gravity. A galaxy is held together by gravity and can hold from thousands to hundreds of billions of stars or more.
