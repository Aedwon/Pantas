## Core Concept

Earth's rigid outer shell (the crust and the rigid top of the mantle) is broken into large pieces called **tectonic plates**, which slowly move over the softer, slowly-flowing mantle beneath them.

### Continental Drift and Seafloor Spreading

In $1915$, the scientist Alfred Wegener proposed **continental drift**: the idea that all the continents were once joined together in one giant landmass called **Pangaea**, and slowly drifted apart over millions of years. His evidence included matching coastlines (like puzzle pieces), matching rock layers and mountain ranges across oceans, and identical fossils found on continents now far apart. However, Wegener could not explain what force moved the continents, so many scientists doubted his idea at first.

The missing piece came from **seafloor spreading**, proposed by Harry Hess in $1959$. At long underwater mountain ranges called **mid-ocean ridges**, hot magma rises up from below, cools, and forms new ocean floor, which then pushes older seafloor away on both sides, like a conveyor belt. This spreading provided the moving force that continental drift was missing, and together these ideas became the modern theory of **plate tectonics**.

### Types of Plate Boundaries

- **Divergent boundary**: two plates move apart from each other. Magma rises to fill the gap, creating new crust (this is exactly what happens at mid-ocean ridges).
- **Convergent boundary**: two plates move toward each other and collide. If one plate is denser, it gets pushed underneath the other in a process called **subduction**; if neither plate sinks, they crumple upward into mountain ranges.
- **Transform boundary**: two plates slide past each other sideways, without creating or destroying crust. Friction along these boundaries builds up stress that releases suddenly as earthquakes.

### Faults, Earthquakes, and Seismic Waves

A **fault** is a crack in Earth's crust where rock on either side has moved. When built-up stress along a fault suddenly releases, the ground shakes: an **earthquake**. This releases energy as **seismic waves**.

**P waves** (primary waves) are the fastest seismic waves and can travel through both solids and liquids. **S waves** (secondary waves) are slower and can only travel through solids, not liquids. Because P waves always arrive first, scientists use the time gap between the P wave and the (later) S wave to calculate how far away an earthquake's origin was.

### Volcanoes and Magmatism

**Volcanoes** most often form at convergent boundaries (where subducting plates melt and produce rising magma) and at divergent boundaries (where magma rises to fill the gap between separating plates). A special case is a **hotspot**: a place where magma rises from deep within the mantle, independent of any plate boundary, which can create volcanic islands (like the Hawaiian Islands) even in the middle of a plate.

## Examples

### #1: Identifying the Boundary Type

Two plates are sliding sideways past each other, causing frequent strong earthquakes but no new mountains or volcanoes. What type of plate boundary is this?

**Solution:**
Match the described motion to the boundary type.

1. Sideways sliding motion, without creating or destroying crust, describes a transform boundary.
2. Since crust is neither created (as at divergent boundaries) nor destroyed (as at convergent boundaries), no new landforms like volcanoes or mountain ranges typically form here.
3. The frequent earthquakes come from built-up friction being suddenly released as the plates grind past each other.
4. The answer: transform boundary.

### #2: Evidence for Continental Drift

Identical fossils of an ancient reptile are found both on the eastern coast of South America and the western coast of Africa, two continents now separated by a wide ocean. How does this support Wegener's continental drift theory?

**Solution:**
Connect the fossil pattern to Wegener's original claim.

1. Wegener proposed that all continents were once joined together as one landmass, Pangaea.
2. If South America and Africa were once connected, an ancient land-dwelling reptile could have lived and spread across the single joined landmass.
3. After the continents drifted apart, the same fossil species would be found on both continents, even though a wide ocean now separates them.
4. This matching fossil evidence is exactly the kind Wegener used to support continental drift.

### #3: Using P and S Waves to Find Distance

A seismograph station records the P wave from an earthquake arriving several seconds before the S wave. Explain why this time gap tells scientists how far away the earthquake occurred, and why a larger gap means a greater distance.

**Solution:**
Recall the relative speeds of the two wave types.

1. P waves travel faster than S waves, so P waves always arrive at a station first.
2. The farther the earthquake's origin is from the station, the more time the faster P wave gains over the slower S wave during the journey.
3. This means a longer gap between the P wave's arrival and the S wave's arrival indicates a greater distance to the earthquake's origin.
4. Stations use this time-gap method (comparing readings from multiple stations) to pinpoint an earthquake's location.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Match each boundary type to its "verb": divergent means "apart" (creates crust), convergent means "together" (destroys or crumples crust), transform means "sideways" (neither creates nor destroys). If a question describes plate motion, find the matching direction word first.

Remember the wave rule with one phrase: "P is first, and P can swim." P waves arrive first and can pass through both liquid and solid; S waves arrive second and cannot pass through liquid at all. This single fact is also how scientists proved Earth's outer core is liquid.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Crediting Wegener with the Full Modern Theory

> Wegener proposed continental drift, but he could not explain the moving force; seafloor spreading (Hess, $1959$) supplied that missing piece.

A choice may say "Wegener's theory was immediately accepted." In fact, it was doubted for decades because Wegener could not explain what force moved the continents. Only after seafloor spreading was discovered did the full theory of plate tectonics become widely accepted.

### #2: Assuming All Volcanoes Sit on Plate Boundaries

> Hotspot volcanoes, like those forming the Hawaiian Islands, occur in the middle of a plate, not at its edge.

Most volcanoes do form at convergent or divergent boundaries, but a hotspot's magma source comes from deep within the mantle and is not tied to any boundary. A question describing a volcanic island chain far from any plate edge may be describing a hotspot.

### #3: Mixing Up P Waves and S Waves

> P waves are faster and travel through solids and liquids. S waves are slower and travel through solids only.

A common wrong answer swaps these properties. Anchor the memory device: P comes first (faster) and passes through everything; S comes second (slower) and cannot swim through liquid.

### #4: Confusing Convergent Boundary Outcomes

> Whether a convergent boundary produces subduction or a mountain range depends on the density of the colliding plates.

Not every convergent boundary looks the same. If one plate is much denser (typically oceanic crust), it sinks beneath the other (subduction), often creating volcanoes above it. If both plates are similar in density (often two continental plates), neither sinks, and they crumple upward into a mountain range instead.
