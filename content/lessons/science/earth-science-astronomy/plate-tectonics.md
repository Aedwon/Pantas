## Core Concept

**Plate tectonics** describes Earth's rigid lithosphere as broken into moving plates that interact at boundaries. The plates move slowly over the weaker asthenosphere, while the mantle beneath them remains mostly solid but can flow over geologic timescales.

### Continental Drift and Seafloor Spreading

Alfred Wegener assembled evidence that continents had once been joined and later moved, including matching coastlines, fossils, rocks, and paleoclimate indicators. His continental-drift proposal lacked a convincing mechanism at the time.

Mid-20th-century mapping and magnetic studies of the ocean floor led to the **seafloor-spreading** model: magma rises at mid-ocean ridges, cools to form new oceanic crust, and the crust moves away from the ridge. Symmetric magnetic stripes and the increasing age of seafloor away from ridges provided major evidence.

Seafloor spreading is a central **process and observation** in plate tectonics, but it is misleading to describe it as the single force that drives all plates. Plate motion reflects several interacting forces; modern explanations commonly emphasize gravity-related forces such as **slab pull**, together with mantle flow and ridge-related forces.

### Plate Boundaries

- **Divergent boundary:** plates move apart and new lithosphere can form at spreading centers.
- **Convergent boundary:** plates move toward each other; oceanic lithosphere may subduct, or continental plates may collide.
- **Transform boundary:** plates slide laterally past one another; crust is neither systematically created nor destroyed along the transform itself.

### Subduction and Volcanism

At many subduction zones, the descending oceanic plate carries water-bearing minerals downward. Released water and other volatiles lower the melting temperature of hot mantle rock above the slab, promoting **partial melting in the mantle wedge**. The resulting magma can rise and feed volcanic arcs.

It is therefore too simple to say that the whole subducting plate merely “melts into magma.” Magma sources can be complex, but water-assisted mantle melting is a standard introductory explanation for many subduction-zone volcanoes.

### Earthquakes and Faults

A **fault** is a fracture or zone of fractures along which rocks have moved. Earthquakes occur when accumulated stress is released through sudden slip along faults.

- **Normal faults** are associated with extension.
- **Reverse/thrust faults** are associated with compression.
- **Strike-slip faults** involve mainly horizontal motion.

Earthquake foci at subduction zones can extend from shallow to great depths along the descending slab, producing a Wadati-Benioff zone.

## Examples

### #1: Magnetic Stripes

Why do symmetric bands of normal and reversed magnetic polarity on opposite sides of a mid-ocean ridge support seafloor spreading?

**Solution:**
1. Basalt forming at the ridge records the direction of Earth's magnetic field as it cools.
2. Earth's magnetic field has reversed many times.
3. New crust forms at the ridge and moves outward on both sides.
4. The resulting mirrored magnetic pattern records successive periods of crust formation and spreading.

### #2: Subduction-Zone Volcanoes

Why can an oceanic plate descending beneath another plate promote volcanism above the subduction zone?

**Solution:**
1. The descending slab transports water-bearing material to depth.
2. Water and other volatiles are released into the overlying hot mantle.
3. These volatiles lower the melting temperature of mantle rock and promote partial melting.
4. Buoyant magma can rise toward the surface and feed a volcanic arc.

### #3: Boundary Identification

Two plates slide horizontally past each other. What boundary type is this, and is crust systematically created or destroyed there?

**Solution:**
It is a transform boundary. Crust is not systematically created or destroyed along the transform boundary itself, although earthquakes are common because the plates can lock and then slip.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Boundary motion mnemonic: **divergent = divide**, **convergent = come together**, **transform = slide past**.

For ocean-floor evidence, remember **youngest crust near ridge + older outward + symmetric magnetic stripes**.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Plates Do Not Float on a Global Ocean of Magma

> The asthenosphere is mostly solid rock that can deform and flow slowly.

Localized melt occurs beneath spreading centers and subduction zones, but most mantle material is solid.

### #2: Seafloor Spreading Is Not the Only Plate-Driving Force

> Plate motion results from interacting forces, with slab pull, mantle flow, and ridge-related forces among the important mechanisms.

Avoid reducing the entire theory to one conveyor-belt force.

### #3: The Subducting Slab Does Not Simply Melt Wholesale

> Water released from the slab commonly promotes partial melting in the overlying mantle wedge.

This is a more accurate introductory picture of arc-magmatism generation.
