## Core Concept

**Plate tectonics** says that Earth's outer shell is broken into large plates. The plates move slowly and meet at boundaries. Most volcanoes and earthquakes happen near these boundaries.

### Plates and the Mantle

Earth has layers. The **crust** is the thin, rocky outer skin. **Oceanic crust** lies under the oceans, and **continental crust** makes up the continents. The **mantle** is the thick layer of hot rock under the crust.

The **lithosphere** is the rigid outer shell: the crust plus the top of the mantle. Below it is the **asthenosphere**, a softer layer of the mantle.

This layer is mostly solid rock, not liquid. It flows very slowly, over millions of years.

### Continental Drift and Seafloor Spreading

Alfred Wegener gathered evidence that the continents were once joined and later moved apart. It included matching coastlines, fossils, rocks, and signs of old climates. He could not explain what moved them.

Later, maps and magnetic surveys of the ocean floor gave an explanation. A **mid-ocean ridge** is a long underwater mountain chain. In **seafloor spreading**, magma (melted rock) rises at the ridge, cools into new oceanic crust, and moves away from the ridge on both sides.

Two clues support this model. The rock gets older farther from the ridge.

The second clue is magnetic. Earth's magnetic field has flipped direction many times, and new rock records the field as it cools. So matching magnetic stripes form on both sides of the ridge.

Several forces move the plates. One main force is **slab pull**: the cold, heavy edge of a sinking plate pulls the rest of the plate after it. Mantle flow and ridge push, the push of the raised ridge, also help.

### Plate Boundaries

- **Divergent boundary:** plates move apart. Magma rises and makes new crust. This happens at mid-ocean ridges and in continental rifts such as the East African Rift.
- **Convergent boundary:** plates move toward each other.
- **Transform boundary:** plates slide sideways past each other. Crust is not made or destroyed here.

At a convergent boundary, what happens depends on the two plates. Density is how heavy a material is for its size. Oceanic crust is denser than continental crust. An older, colder oceanic plate is denser than a younger one.

If an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, the oceanic plate sinks. If two oceanic plates meet, the denser, older one sinks.

This sinking is **subduction**. It makes a **trench**, a deep, narrow valley in the sea floor.

When two continents meet, both are too light to sink. They crumple and push up mountains in a **collision**. The Himalayas formed this way, where India meets Asia.

### Subduction and Volcanism

A sinking oceanic plate carries water-filled minerals down with it. At depth, the plate releases the water.

The water lowers the melting point of the hot mantle rock above the plate. This rock partly melts, and the magma rises to form a chain of volcanoes called a **volcanic arc**. If the upper plate is oceanic, the arc is a chain of volcanic islands.

### Earthquakes and Faults

A **fault** is a crack in rock where the two sides have moved. Rock under stress stores energy. When it suddenly slips along a fault, the energy is released as an **earthquake**.

The **focus** (plural: foci) is the point underground where the slip starts. The **epicenter** is the point on the surface directly above it.

Earthquakes send out body waves through Earth and slower surface waves along the ground. Two body waves show distance: the faster **P wave** arrives first, and the slower **S wave** arrives second. A longer gap between them means a more distant earthquake.

Faults are named by how the two blocks moved. The block above a tilted fault is the **hanging wall**. The block below it is the **footwall**.

- **Normal fault:** the hanging wall moves down. The rock is being pulled apart (extension).
- **Reverse fault:** the hanging wall moves up. The rock is being squeezed (compression). A low-angle reverse fault is a thrust fault.
- **Strike-slip fault:** the two blocks slide sideways past each other. The rock is being pushed in opposite directions along the fault (shear). This is the fault type at a transform boundary.

At a subduction zone, many foci lie along the sinking plate and get deeper farther from the trench. This is the **Wadati-Benioff zone**.

### Hazards and the Philippines

A **hazard** is a natural event that can cause harm. **Risk** is the harm that may result. Risk is greater where the hazard is strong, many people and buildings lie in its path, and the buildings are weak.

The Philippines lies in the **Ring of Fire**, a belt of volcanoes, trenches, and earthquake zones around the Pacific Ocean. The country lies between the Eurasian Plate and the Philippine Sea Plate.

It has a trench on each side: the Manila Trench on the west and the Philippine Trench on the east. The Philippine Fault Zone, a strike-slip fault system, crosses it.

Strong earthquakes cause ground shaking, **ground rupture** (the ground breaks along the fault), **liquefaction** (loose, wet soil acts like a liquid), landslides, and **tsunami** (huge waves from a strong earthquake under the sea).

Volcanoes such as Mayon, Taal, and Pinatubo cause lava flows, ashfall, **pyroclastic density currents** (fast, very hot clouds of gas, ash, and rock), and **lahars** (mudflows of volcanic ash and rock mixed with water).

People lower the risk with hazard maps, safe building codes, drills, and evacuation plans. A hazard map shows which areas a hazard can reach, so people can avoid them.

## Examples

### #1: Divergent Boundary Clues

A survey finds an underwater mountain chain with shallow earthquakes along it. The rock is youngest at the center and older on both sides. Bands of normal and reversed magnetic polarity match on both sides. What kind of boundary is this, and why do the bands support seafloor spreading?

**Solution:**

1. Young rock at the center with older rock on both sides means new crust forms there. This is a divergent boundary, a mid-ocean ridge.
2. New rock records the direction of Earth's magnetic field as it cools. The field has reversed many times.
3. Each new strip of crust moves outward on both sides, so the stripes form a mirror image.

### #2: Subduction or Collision?

Name the process at each convergent boundary: (a) an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, (b) two oceanic plates meet, (c) two continental plates meet.

**Solution:**

1. In (a), the denser oceanic plate sinks. This is subduction, with a trench and a volcanic arc, as in the Andes.
2. In (b), the older, colder plate sinks. This is subduction, with a trench and a volcanic island arc, as in the Aleutian Islands.
3. In (c), neither plate sinks. They collide and push up high mountains, as in the Himalayas.

### #3: Subduction-Zone Volcanoes

Why can an oceanic plate sinking beneath another plate cause volcanoes above the subduction zone?

**Solution:**

1. The sinking plate carries water-filled minerals down into the mantle.
2. At depth, the plate releases the water into the hot mantle rock above it.
3. The water lowers the melting point of that rock, so it partly melts.
4. The magma is lighter than the rock around it, so it rises and feeds a volcanic arc.

### #4: Reading a Fault

The rock layers on either side of a tilted fault no longer line up. The hanging wall has moved down relative to the footwall. What type of fault is this, and what kind of stress caused it?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: side view of a tilted fault; the labeled hanging wall has moved down, the labeled footwall has not, arrows pointing apart -->
**Solution:**

1. The hanging wall moved down, so this is a normal fault.
2. Normal faults form when rock is pulled apart. The stress is extension.

### #5: Reading an Earthquake and Volcano Map

A map shows two belts of earthquakes. Belt A curves beside a deep trench, with volcanoes on its inland side. Its foci are $20$ km deep near the trench and $250$ km deep farthest inland. Belt B is a straight line across land with shallow foci and no volcanoes. What boundary lies along each belt, and which way does the plate dip under belt A?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: map of belt A, a curved line of earthquake dots labeled 20 km near a trench and 250 km farthest inland, with volcano triangles inland; belt B, a straight line of shallow dots across land with no volcanoes -->
**Solution:**

1. Earthquakes and volcanoes gather in narrow belts along plate boundaries.
2. Belt A has a trench, a volcano chain, and foci that deepen inland. This is subduction at a convergent boundary. The plate dips down toward the inland side.
3. Belt B has shallow earthquakes, no trench, and no volcanoes. Plates slide past each other and crust is not made or destroyed. This is a transform boundary.

### #6: Hazard Near a Trench

Residents of a coastal town facing the Philippine Trench feel very strong shaking. Then the sea suddenly pulls back from the shore. Which hazard is coming, and what should they do?

**Solution:**

1. Strong shaking followed by a sudden drop in sea level warns of a tsunami.
2. Residents should move at once to higher ground away from the shore, without waiting for a warning.

### #7: Hazard on a Volcano Slope

Loose ash from an old eruption covers a volcano's slopes. A village lies beside a river that starts there, and heavy rain begins. Which hazard threatens the village, and what should its leaders do?

**Solution:**

1. Rain mixes with the ash and makes a lahar, a mudflow that rushes down river channels.
2. The village lies in its path, so the risk is high.
3. Leaders should use the hazard map to move residents to higher ground away from the river.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For a fault question, find the hanging wall first. On a tilted fault, down means normal and up means reverse. If the blocks only slide sideways, it is strike-slip.

For a map question, a trench with a volcanic chain beside it usually points to subduction. A straight line of shallow earthquakes with no volcanoes usually points to a transform boundary. A lone volcano far from any boundary may be a hot spot (see Trap #1).

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Plates Do Not Float on a Sea of Magma

> The asthenosphere is mostly solid rock that flows slowly.

Some melt does form: under ridges, above sinking plates, and at **hot spots**. A hot spot is a place where hot rock from deep in the mantle melts and feeds volcanoes, as under Hawaii.
