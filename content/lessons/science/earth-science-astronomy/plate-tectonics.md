## Core Concept

**Plate tectonics** says that Earth's outer shell is broken into large plates. The plates move slowly and meet at boundaries. Most volcanoes and earthquakes happen near these boundaries.

### Plates and the Mantle

The **lithosphere** is the rigid outer shell: the crust plus the top of the mantle. Below it is the **asthenosphere**, a softer layer of the mantle. This layer is mostly solid rock, not liquid. It flows only over millions of years.

### Continental Drift and Seafloor Spreading

Alfred Wegener gathered evidence that the continents were once joined and later moved apart. It included matching coastlines, fossils, and rocks, and signs of old climates on different continents. He could not explain what moved them.

Later, maps and magnetic surveys of the ocean floor gave an explanation. In **seafloor spreading**, magma rises at a mid-ocean ridge, cools into new oceanic crust, and moves away from the ridge on both sides.

Two clues support this model. The rock gets older farther from the ridge. And Earth's magnetic field has flipped direction many times, so new rock records the field as it cools. This makes matching magnetic stripes on both sides of the ridge.

Several forces move the plates. One main force is **slab pull**: the cold, heavy edge of a sinking plate pulls the rest of the plate after it. Mantle flow and ridge push also help.

### Plate Boundaries

- **Divergent boundary:** plates move apart. Magma rises and makes new crust. This happens at mid-ocean ridges and in continental rifts such as the East African Rift.
- **Convergent boundary:** plates move toward each other.
- **Transform boundary:** plates slide sideways past each other. Crust is not made or destroyed here.

At a convergent boundary, what happens depends on the two plates. Oceanic crust is denser than continental crust. So when an oceanic plate meets another plate, it sinks. This is **subduction**. It makes a deep ocean **trench**.

When two continents meet, both are too light to sink. They crumple and push up mountains. This is a **collision**. The Himalayas formed this way, where India meets Asia.

### Subduction and Volcanism

A sinking oceanic plate carries water-filled minerals down with it. At depth, the plate releases the water. The water lowers the melting point of the hot mantle rock above the plate. This rock partly melts, and the magma rises to form a chain of volcanoes called a volcanic arc.

### Earthquakes and Faults

A **fault** is a crack in rock where the two sides have moved. Rock under stress stores energy. When it suddenly slips along a fault, the energy is released as an **earthquake**.

The **focus** is the point underground where the slip starts. The **epicenter** is the point on the surface directly above it.

Earthquakes send out two kinds of waves. The faster **P wave** arrives first. The slower **S wave** arrives second. A longer gap between them means a more distant earthquake.

To name a fault, see how the two blocks moved. The block above a tilted fault is the **hanging wall**. The block below it is the **footwall**.

- **Normal fault:** the hanging wall moves down. The rock is being pulled apart (extension).
- **Reverse fault:** the hanging wall moves up. The rock is being squeezed (compression). A low-angle reverse fault is a thrust fault.
- **Strike-slip fault:** the two blocks slide sideways past each other. This is the fault type at a transform boundary.

At a subduction zone, earthquakes occur along the sinking plate. Their focus gets deeper farther from the trench. This line of deepening earthquakes is called the **Wadati-Benioff zone**.

### Hazards and the Philippines

The **Ring of Fire** is a belt of volcanoes, trenches, and earthquake zones around the Pacific Ocean. The Philippines lies in it.

The country lies between the Eurasian Plate and the Philippine Sea Plate. It has a trench on each side: the Manila Trench on the west and the Philippine Trench on the east. The Philippine Fault Zone, a strike-slip fault system about $1{,}200$ km long, crosses it.

Strong earthquakes cause:

- **Ground shaking** and **ground rupture** (the ground breaks along the fault).
- **Liquefaction:** loose, wet soil loses strength and acts like a liquid.
- **Landslides** on shaken slopes.
- **Tsunami:** a strong earthquake under the sea moves the water and makes huge waves.

Volcanoes such as Mayon, Taal, and Pinatubo cause:

- **Lava flows** and **ashfall**.
- **Pyroclastic density currents:** fast, very hot clouds of gas, ash, and rock.
- **Lahars:** mudflows of volcanic ash and rock mixed with water.

People lower the risk with hazard maps (which show areas at risk), safe building codes, drills, and evacuation plans.

## Examples

### #1: Magnetic Stripes

Why do matching bands of normal and reversed magnetic polarity on both sides of a mid-ocean ridge support seafloor spreading?

**Solution:**

1. New rock at the ridge records the direction of Earth's magnetic field as it cools.
2. The field has reversed many times.
3. Each new strip of crust moves outward on both sides, so the stripes form a mirror image.

### #2: Divergent Boundary Clues

A survey finds a long underwater mountain chain. The rock is youngest at the center and older on both sides. Shallow earthquakes occur along the chain. What kind of boundary is this, and what is happening there?

**Solution:**

1. Young rock at the center with older rock on both sides means new crust forms there.
2. This is a divergent boundary, a mid-ocean ridge. Magma rises and cools into new oceanic crust.

### #3: Subduction or Collision?

Name the process at each convergent boundary: (a) an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, (b) two continental plates meet.

**Solution:**

1. In (a), the denser oceanic plate sinks under the continent. This is subduction. A trench and a volcanic arc form, as in the Andes.
2. In (b), neither plate can sink. They collide and push up high mountains, as in the Himalayas.

### #4: Subduction-Zone Volcanoes

Why can a sinking oceanic plate cause volcanoes on the plate above it?

**Solution:**

1. The sinking plate releases water into the hot mantle above it.
2. The water lowers the melting point of the mantle rock, so it partly melts.
3. The magma is lighter than the rock around it, so it rises and feeds a volcanic arc.

### #5: Transform Boundary

Two plates slide sideways past each other. What type of boundary is this? Is crust made or destroyed?

**Solution:**
This is a transform boundary. Crust is not made or destroyed. Earthquakes are common because the plates lock, build stress, and then suddenly slip.

### #6: Reading a Fault

The rock layers on either side of a tilted fault no longer line up. Compared with the footwall, the hanging wall has moved down. What type of fault is this, and what kind of stress caused it?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: side view of a tilted fault; the labeled hanging wall has moved down, the labeled footwall has not, arrows pointing apart -->
**Solution:**
The hanging wall moved down, so this is a normal fault. Normal faults form when rock is pulled apart. The stress is extension.

If the hanging wall had moved up, it would be a reverse fault, caused by squeezing.

### #7: Earthquake Depths at a Trench

Earthquake foci along a line running inland from an ocean trench are $20$ km deep near the trench, $100$ km deep farther inland, and $250$ km deep farthest inland. A volcano chain lies on the inland side. What boundary is this, and which way does the plate dip?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: cross-section of a trench with a plate sinking to the right; foci dots at 20 km, 100 km and 250 km depth, deeper farther right; a volcano chain on the right -->
**Solution:**

1. A trench, a volcanic chain, and foci that deepen in one direction point to subduction at a convergent boundary.
2. The foci follow the sinking plate. It dips down toward the inland side, where the foci are deepest.

### #8: Hazard Near a Trench

A coastal town in the eastern Philippines faces the Philippine Trench. Residents feel very strong shaking, and then the sea suddenly pulls back from the shore. Which hazard is coming, and what should they do?

**Solution:**

1. Strong shaking followed by a sudden drop in sea level are warning signs of a tsunami.
2. Residents should move at once to higher ground away from the shore, without waiting for an official warning.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

**Divergent** means plates divide: ridges, rifts, new crust. **Convergent** means plates come together: trenches, volcanic arcs, mountain belts. **Transform** means plates slide past each other.

For ocean-floor evidence, remember: youngest rock at the ridge, older rock farther away.

For a fault question, find the hanging wall first. Down means normal, up means reverse, sideways means strike-slip.

For a map question, a trench with a volcanic chain beside it points to subduction.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Plates Do Not Float on a Sea of Magma

> The asthenosphere is mostly solid rock that flows slowly.

Melt forms only in small amounts, under ridges and above sinking plates.

### #2: Seafloor Spreading Is Not the Only Force

> Several forces move the plates, including slab pull, mantle flow, and ridge push.

Do not reduce the theory to one conveyor belt.

### #3: The Sinking Plate Does Not Melt All at Once

> Water from the sinking plate melts the mantle above it.

Arc magma comes from the partly melted mantle, not from the whole slab.

### #4: Collisions Are Not Subduction

> Oceanic plates sink. Continental plates collide.

Two continents are too light to sink, so they build mountains.
