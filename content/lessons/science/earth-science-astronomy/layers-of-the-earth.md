## Core Concept

Earth is not the same all the way through. Its inside is divided into layers. The usual division is by **composition**, which means what the layer is made of.

There are three main layers: the **crust**, the **mantle**, and the **core**. Many books split the core into two parts, a liquid **outer core** and a solid **inner core**. That gives a four-layer model.

Think of a peach. The skin is thin, the flesh is thick, and the pit is at the center. Earth has a thin crust, a thick mantle, and a metal core.

### Crust

The **crust** is the thin, rocky outer skin of Earth. It is the layer we live on. It is made of solid rock.

There are two kinds of crust. **Oceanic crust** lies under the oceans. It is mostly **basalt**, a dark, dense rock. It is usually about $5$ to $10$ km thick.

**Continental crust** forms the continents. Much of it is **granite**-like rock, which is lighter in color and lower in density. Its thickness varies a lot. It averages about $35$ km, and it can be $60$ km or more under tall mountain ranges.

Continental crust is less dense than oceanic crust. A rough value is about $2.7$ g/cm³ for continental crust and about $3.0$ g/cm³ for oceanic crust. Density means how much mass fits in a given volume.

### Mantle

The **mantle** lies below the crust and reaches a depth of about $2{,}900$ km. It is the thickest layer. It is made mostly of **silicate** rock, which means rock built on silicon and oxygen, rich in magnesium and iron.

The boundary between crust and mantle is called the **Mohorovičić discontinuity**, or the Moho. Seismic waves change speed there because the rock changes.

The mantle is mostly solid, but it is not rigid like a brick. Under heat and great pressure, solid rock can creep very slowly over millions of years. Scientists say the mantle **flows**, and they do not mean it is a liquid.

### Outer Core and Inner Core

The **core** is made mostly of iron and nickel, with small amounts of lighter elements. It begins at a depth of about $2{,}900$ km.

The **outer core** is liquid. It is about $2{,}200$ km thick, from about $2{,}900$ km to about $5{,}150$ km deep. Scientists think that the motion of this liquid metal produces Earth's magnetic field.

The **inner core** is a solid ball at the center. Its radius is about $1{,}220$ km. It is hotter than the outer core, yet it stays solid. The reason is pressure. At the center, pressure is so high that the metal cannot melt, even at that high temperature.

So a higher temperature does not always mean liquid. Melting depends on both temperature and pressure.

### Two Ways to Divide the Layers

Earth can also be divided by how the rock behaves, which is called **mechanical** (physical) behavior. This is different from composition.

The **lithosphere** is the cool, stiff outer shell. It includes the crust and the very top of the mantle. It is broken into the tectonic plates.

Below it is the **asthenosphere**, a part of the upper mantle that is hotter and weaker. It is solid and flows slowly. The plates move over it. A small amount of melted rock may exist in parts of it, but it is not a sea of magma.

The lithosphere is not the same as the crust. It is thicker, because it contains some mantle too.

### How We Know

No one has seen the inside of Earth directly. The deepest hole ever drilled, the Kola Superdeep Borehole in Russia, reached about $12$ km. The distance to Earth's center is about $6{,}371$ km, so the drill reached a tiny part of the way. It stayed in the crust.

So scientists use indirect evidence. The most important kind is **seismic waves**, which are waves of energy that travel through Earth after an earthquake. A machine called a seismograph records them.

There are two main kinds of body waves, which travel through the inside of Earth:

- **P waves** (primary waves) are pushing and pulling waves. They travel through solids and through liquids. They arrive first.
- **S waves** (secondary waves) are side-to-side shaking waves. They need a material that springs back when it is pushed sideways. A solid does this. A liquid just flows out of the way and does not spring back, so it cannot pass on a side-to-side wave. S waves arrive after P waves.

Waves change speed and bend (this is called **refraction**) when the material changes. They can also bounce back (**reflection**). By timing the waves at many seismographs, scientists work out where the layers are and what they are like.

When a large earthquake occurs, S waves do not appear at seismographs on the far side of Earth, beyond roughly $104^\circ$ from the earthquake. This is the **S-wave shadow zone**. It shows that something liquid lies in the way.

P waves pass through the outer core, but they slow down and bend sharply. This leaves a **P-wave shadow zone**, roughly between $104^\circ$ and $140^\circ$ from the earthquake. The shadow zone shows that the outer core has very different properties from the mantle.

The inner core was found in $1936$ by the Danish scientist Inge Lehmann. She noticed P waves that were reflected from a boundary deep inside the core. Later study of the waves showed that the inner core is solid.

Other evidence supports the picture. These include gravity measurements, Earth's magnetic field, laboratory experiments at high pressure, and meteorites. Meteorites are rocks from space, and some are rich in iron like the core.

## Examples

### #1: Why the Outer Core Is Liquid

Seismographs on the far side of Earth from an earthquake record P waves but no S waves. What does this show about the outer core?

**Solution:**
1. S waves cannot travel through a liquid, because a liquid does not spring back when pushed sideways.
2. The S waves from the earthquake did not come out through the core, so the core must contain a liquid region.
3. P waves can travel through liquids, but they slow down and bend at the core boundary. This also fits a liquid outer core.

The outer core is therefore liquid.

### #2: Oceanic and Continental Crust

Which type of crust is thinner and denser?

**Solution:**
1. Oceanic crust is about $5$ to $10$ km thick. Continental crust averages about $35$ km.
2. Oceanic crust is made of basalt, which is denser than the granite-like rock of continental crust.
3. So oceanic crust is thinner and denser.

Continental crust is usually thicker, but its thickness varies from place to place.

### #3: A Hot Inner Core That Is Solid

The inner core is hotter than the outer core. Why is it solid and the outer core liquid?

**Solution:**
1. Melting depends on temperature and also on pressure.
2. Pressure increases with depth, and it is greatest at the center.
3. At the inner core, the pressure is so high that the iron-rich metal stays solid despite the heat.
4. The outer core has less pressure, so its metal is liquid.

### #4: Composition or Behavior?

A student says that the lithosphere and the crust are the same layer. Is this correct?

**Solution:**
1. The crust is a layer defined by composition. It is the rock above the Moho.
2. The lithosphere is defined by behavior. It is the cool, stiff shell.
3. The lithosphere includes the crust and also the uppermost mantle.
4. So the statement is not correct. The lithosphere is thicker than the crust.

### #5: Can Scientists Sample the Core Directly?

Have humans drilled through the crust into the mantle or the core?

**Solution:**
1. The deepest borehole, the Kola Superdeep Borehole, reached about $12$ km.
2. This is well inside the crust. It is far above the mantle, which begins at the Moho.
3. No hole has reached the core, which is about $2{,}900$ km down.
4. So scientists study the mantle and core indirectly, mainly with seismic waves. They also use rocks that volcanoes bring up from the upper mantle.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For the four-layer model, remember the order from outside in: **crust, mantle, liquid outer core, solid inner core**. The outer core is the one large liquid layer. The mantle holds at most small pockets of melt.

For wave questions, use this check: S waves stop at a liquid, and P waves pass through both solids and liquids. If a question says that S waves do not pass through a region, think "liquid."

This shortcut tells about the S waves only. It does not tell you how thick the liquid region is. Other waves, such as P waves, give the boundaries.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: "The Mantle Is Molten"

> The mantle is mostly solid rock that flows very slowly. It is not a sea of liquid magma.

Flowing and liquid are different. Parts of the mantle may hold a small amount of melt, and magma forms in some places. But S waves travel through most of the mantle, which shows it is solid.

### #2: Hotter Does Not Always Mean Liquid

> The inner core is hotter than the outer core and is solid.

Pressure raises the melting point of the metal. A question that asks which layer is liquid cannot be answered by temperature alone.

### #3: Crust Thickness Is Not One Number

> Continental crust averages about $35$ km, but it is thicker under mountains and thinner elsewhere.

Oceanic crust is also not exactly one number. Do not pick an answer that gives one fixed value for all of either type.

### #4: Lithosphere Is Not the Same as Crust

> The crust is defined by what it is made of. The lithosphere is defined by how it behaves.

The lithosphere includes the crust and the top of the mantle.

### #5: Deep Layers Are Not Known by Drilling

> We know the deep layers by indirect evidence, mainly seismic waves.

The deepest drill reached about $12$ km, which is the crust.
