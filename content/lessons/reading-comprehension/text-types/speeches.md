## Core Concept

A **speech** is spoken language addressed to an audience. A **scripted** speech is written out in advance and read or recited. An **extemporaneous** speech is spoken from notes or from memory, and the exact words are chosen in the moment. Many speeches fall between the two.

In this lesson you read printed excerpts. You judge the words only. Tone of voice, pace, and gestures add meaning when a speech is heard, but a printed excerpt cannot show them. This lesson teaches how to read speeches, not how to write or deliver one.

### Audience, Context, and Purpose

The **audience** is the people the speaker addresses. The **context** is the occasion, place, and time of the speech. The **purpose** is what the speaker wants to achieve.

Common purposes are to inform, to persuade, to inspire, to commemorate (honor a person or an event), and to entertain. A speech can combine purposes. To find the main purpose, ask what the speaker most wants the audience to think, feel, or do by the end. The other purposes then support it.

### Style

**Style** is the manner of the speaker's words. A **formal** style uses full sentences, careful word choice, titles of address, and no slang. A **casual** style uses contractions, everyday words, jokes, and direct talk to "you".

Style is a clue to the context, not proof of it. A speaker may use a casual style at a formal event to seem friendly. A style that does not suit the audience can also cause misunderstanding.

### Speech Acts

A **speech act** is the action a speaker performs by speaking, such as promising, warning, requesting, thanking, apologizing, announcing, or congratulating. The form of a sentence does not always show its act. "Could you please sit down?" has the form of a question but acts as a request.

The same sentence can perform different acts in different contexts. "I will be there" is a promise if the speaker commits to come. It is only a prediction if the speaker is guessing. Use the context to decide.

### Ethos, Pathos, and Logos

Persuasive speeches often use three appeals from classical rhetoric (the art of persuading with words).

- **Ethos:** how the speaker shows credibility, character, authority, or trustworthiness.
- **Pathos:** how the speech engages emotions, values, hopes, fears, or sympathies.
- **Logos:** how the speech gives reasons, evidence, examples, causes, comparisons, or other arguments.

These appeals can overlap. A statistic supports logos, but who presents it also affects ethos. An example can have both logical and emotional force. Name the main function of the passage, and say when it serves more than one.

### Judging Evidence

A **claim** is a statement the speaker wants the audience to accept. **Evidence** is a fact, number, example, or expert statement that supports the claim. Test evidence with three checks.

- **Source:** who produced it, and could they gain from the claim being accepted?
- **Method:** how was it gathered, from how many cases, and compared with what?
- **Relevance:** does it bear on this exact claim?

A number alone is not strong reasoning. A trusted source does not make irrelevant evidence relevant. A single personal story can be relevant as an example, but it cannot show a general trend.

### Emotion and Manipulation

Emotional appeal is a normal part of speaking. It is fair when the feeling fits true facts and the speech also gives the reasons the claim needs. It becomes **manipulation** when it replaces needed evidence, exaggerates, or hides facts so that the audience feels what the facts do not support.

A tribute or farewell is a different case. Its purpose is to honor a person, so strong emotion is expected and no evidence is needed.

### Rhetorical Devices

- **Repetition:** the same word or phrase recurring for emphasis or structure.
- **Parallelism:** repeated grammatical patterns that create clarity, rhythm, or emphasis. The words can differ while the pattern stays the same.
- **Rhetorical question:** a question used mainly to make a point or prompt thought, not to get a literal answer. If the speaker really wants an answer, such as "Does anyone have a question?", it is an ordinary question.

One passage can use more than one device at once.

### Communication Breakdown and Repair

A **communication breakdown** happens when the audience does not receive the meaning the speaker intended. Common causes are poor sound, unfamiliar words, long or unclear sentences, a fast pace, and different background knowledge.

A **repair strategy** is a step that fixes the breakdown. A speaker can rephrase in simpler words, give an example, slow down, or check understanding. A listener can ask for a repeat or restate the message in their own words. A good repair matches the cause: speaking louder does not explain an unfamiliar word.

## Examples

### #1: Audience, Context, and Mixed Purpose

Read the excerpt and name the audience and the main purpose.

"Good evening, parents of Grade $3$ pupils. Next Saturday, the barangay clinic will give free measles shots from $8$ a.m. to noon. Please bring your child's health card. A child who is vaccinated also protects classmates who cannot be."

**Solution:**

1. The speaker says "parents of Grade $3$ pupils", so the audience is parents at a school meeting.
2. The excerpt gives a time and a place, so it informs.
3. It also says "Please bring" and gives a reason to act, so it wants parents to bring their children.

The main purpose is to persuade parents to act. The facts about time and place support that purpose.

### #2: Scripted or Unscripted

Which excerpt is more likely from an extemporaneous speech?

Excerpt A: "Today we mark ten years since the school opened its doors."

Excerpt B: "So, um, what I wanted to say is, well, we started small, and, uh, look at us now."

**Solution:**

1. Excerpt A has complete, polished wording, as a prepared text would.
2. Excerpt B has fillers ("um", "uh") and restarts, which suggest the words were chosen while speaking.

Excerpt B is more likely extemporaneous. This is only a clue, because a scripted speech can include pauses on purpose.

### #3: Ethos

"Having served on the front lines of this crisis for over a decade, I can tell you firsthand what our community needs."

Which appeal is the main one?

**Solution:** The speaker points to a decade of relevant experience to show credibility. The main appeal is **ethos**.

### #4: Pathos

"Picture a parent unable to afford medicine for a sick child."

Which appeal is the main one?

**Solution:** The image invites sympathy and concern. The main appeal is **pathos**.

### #5: Logos

"Districts using the program reported a larger increase in literacy rates than comparison districts."

Which appeal is the main one?

**Solution:** The sentence compares districts with and without the program to support a claim. If the evidence is accurate and fairly interpreted, the main appeal is **logos**.

### #6: Overlapping Appeals

"I have taught in this school for $20$ years, and I have watched $3$ in $10$ of my students leave before graduation. Think of those empty desks."

Name every appeal and where it appears.

**Solution:**

1. "I have taught in this school for $20$ years" builds ethos through experience.
2. "$3$ in $10$ of my students" offers a statistic as evidence, so it supports logos.
3. "Think of those empty desks" invites feeling, so it is pathos.

The statistic also carries emotion, and the experience makes it easier to trust. One passage can use all three appeals, and one phrase can serve two.

### #7: Evaluating Evidence

A mayor says: "Our new bike lane has reduced traffic jams. We surveyed $40$ riders at the lane, and $38$ said they love it." A critic replies that jams on the same road have not changed. What is the main weakness of the mayor's evidence?

**Solution:**

1. The claim is about traffic jams.
2. The survey asked riders, who chose the lane and are likely to approve. This is a weak source and method.
3. Liking the lane says nothing about how many cars are stuck, so the evidence is not relevant to the claim.

The mayor's evidence does not measure traffic. A count of jams before and after the lane opened would be relevant.

### #8: Rhetorical Question

"Who among us has never needed help from a neighbor?" What does the speaker intend?

**Solution:**

1. The speaker does not expect a spoken answer.
2. The honest answer is that everyone has needed help.
3. The question reminds the audience of a shared experience.

The speaker intends to build common ground before asking for help for others. At the end of the speech, "Does anyone have a question?" would be a literal question, because it asks for answers.

### #9: Repetition

"We will rebuild our roads. We will rebuild our schools. We will rebuild our trust."

Name the devices.

**Solution:** The phrase "We will rebuild" recurs, which is **repetition**. The three sentences also share one pattern, so they form **parallelism** too. One passage can use two devices.

### #10: Parallelism with Different Words

"Read the notes, check the facts, ask the questions." Is this parallelism or repetition?

**Solution:**

1. Each phrase follows the pattern: command verb, then "the", then a noun.
2. The verbs and nouns change each time.
3. Only "the" recurs, so the words are not repeated for emphasis.

The sentence shows **parallelism**. The repeated pattern gives it a steady rhythm.

### #11: Fair Emotion or Manipulation

Speaker X: "Last year, three students were hurt crossing at this corner. Think of your child there. That is why I ask you to support a crossing guard."

Speaker Y: "Anyone who opposes the crossing guard does not love children. Imagine the guilt you will carry."

Which speaker uses emotion in place of evidence?

**Solution:**

1. Speaker X gives a fact (three students hurt), then an emotional image that fits the fact, then a request.
2. Speaker Y gives no fact. It blames opponents and pushes guilt.

Speaker Y relies on manipulation. Speaker X's emotion is fair because it rests on a stated fact.

### #12: Formal or Casual Style

Opening A: "Honorable members of the board, I thank you for the opportunity to present this proposal."

Opening B: "Hey, everybody! Great to see you all, can't believe it's been ten years!"

Which opening suits a school board hearing, and which suits a class reunion?

**Solution:**

1. Opening A uses a title of address and full, careful sentences, which are formal clues.
2. Opening B uses "Hey" and contractions, which are casual clues.

A suits the hearing and B suits the reunion. These are clues, not proof, so check the audience and occasion too.

### #13: Speech Acts

The principal says: "Could you all please take your seats?" Later she says: "I will send the results by Friday." Name the act in each sentence.

**Solution:**

1. The first sentence has the form of a question. Its act is a request that the audience sit down.
2. The second sentence commits her to a future action, so it is a promise.

The act depends on what the speaker does with the words, not only on the sentence form.

### #14: Breakdown and Repair

An engineer tells a group of farmers: "Evapotranspiration will rise this dry season." The farmers look puzzled. What caused the breakdown, and which repair fits best?

**Solution:**

1. The farmers have not heard the word "evapotranspiration" before. The cause is an unfamiliar word.
2. A repair must match that cause. The engineer should explain the word in plain terms, such as water leaving the soil and the plants into the air, and give an example.

Speaking louder or faster would not fix an unfamiliar word.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

**Start with audience, context, and purpose.** Ask what the speaker wants this audience to understand, believe, feel, or do. If the purposes are mixed, pick the one the ending of the excerpt serves.

**Treat ethos, pathos, and logos as overlapping lenses.** Name the main function of the passage, then check whether a second appeal also applies.

**Judge evidence by source, method, and relevance.** A number can still fail one of the three checks.

**Read the act, not only the sentence form.** Ask what the speaker is doing: asking, requesting, promising, or warning.

**Match the repair to the cause.** Find why the audience did not understand, then pick the step that removes that cause.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: "Any Number Means Logos"

> A number does not make an argument sound.

An unsupported or irrelevant statistic is not good reasoning. Check its source, method, and relevance before you credit it as logos.

### #2: Treating Ethos, Pathos, and Logos as Separate Boxes

> One passage can build credibility, emotion, and reasoning at once.

Choose the dominant function of the passage when the question asks for one. Do not assume other appeals are absent.

### #3: "Pathos Is Manipulation"

> Emotional appeal is normal, and a tribute depends on it.

Emotion is fair when it fits true facts. It becomes manipulation when it replaces evidence a claim needs, or exaggerates.

### #4: "Every Speech Is a Written Persuasive Text"

> Speeches can be unscripted or nonpersuasive.

They can inform, commemorate, entertain, or inspire. Check the purpose before you look for persuasion.

### #5: "Every Question Is Rhetorical"

> A question in a speech may ask for a real answer.

A question is rhetorical only when it is used to make a point. Check whether the speaker waits for a reply.

### #6: "Louder or Faster Repairs Any Breakdown"

> A repair that ignores the cause does not fix the breakdown.

Repeating the same words does not help when the audience does not know a word. Rephrase or explain instead.
