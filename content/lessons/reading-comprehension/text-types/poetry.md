## Core Concept

**Poetry** communicates ideas and emotions through careful word choice, rhythm, and imagery, often in fewer words than prose, packing extra meaning into figurative language.

**Figurative language** describes things in a non-literal way to create a vivid image or deeper meaning:
- A **simile** compares two different things using "like" or "as": "Her smile was *like* sunshine."
- A **metaphor** compares two different things without using "like" or "as," stating one thing simply is another: "Her smile *was* sunshine."
- **Personification** gives human qualities to something non-human: "The wind *whispered* through the trees."
- **Imagery** is descriptive language that appeals to the five senses, helping the reader vividly picture, hear, smell, taste, or feel the scene.

**Rhyme scheme** is the pattern of rhyming sounds at the end of each line, usually labeled with letters (A, B, C...) where matching letters mark lines that rhyme with each other. For example, a poem with the pattern abab has its first and third lines rhyming, and its second and fourth lines rhyming.

**Tone** is the poet's attitude toward the subject (playful, somber, angry, hopeful), often revealed through word choice and imagery rather than stated directly.

Poetry rarely states its meaning directly — readers must infer the poem's message from its images, figurative language, and tone, much like inferring a theme in a short story.

## Examples

### #1: Identifying Figurative Language

Identify the type of figurative language: "Time is a thief that steals our youth."

**Solution:**
1. The sentence compares "time" to "a thief" without using "like" or "as," directly stating that time is a thief.
2. This is a metaphor.

### #2: Identifying a Simile

Identify the type of figurative language: "The old man's hands were as rough as tree bark."

**Solution:**
1. The sentence compares the man's hands to tree bark using "as," a comparison word.
2. This is a simile.

### #3: Determining the Rhyme Scheme

Identify the rhyme scheme of this stanza: "The moon shines bright above the *bay* (line 1) / The stars begin to gently *glow* (line 2) / The waves roll in and drift *away* (line 3) / While quiet winds begin to *blow* (line 4)."

**Solution:**
1. Line $1$ ends in "bay" and line $3$ ends in "away" — these rhyme with each other.
2. Line $2$ ends in "glow" and line $4$ ends in "blow" — these rhyme with each other.
3. Label the first rhyme sound as A, and the second rhyme sound as B: line $1$ = A, line $2$ = B, line $3$ = A, line $4$ = B.
4. The rhyme scheme is abab.

### #4: Inferring Tone and Meaning

A poem describes "gray skies pressing low," "empty streets," and "a single light flickering, unanswered." What is the likely tone of this poem, and what feeling does the imagery suggest?

**Solution:**
1. The imagery — gray skies, empty streets, a flickering and "unanswered" light — consistently suggests loneliness, absence, or sadness, rather than joy or excitement.
2. The tone is likely somber or melancholy.
3. The specific word "unanswered" reinforces a feeling of isolation or unmet longing, suggesting the poem may be about loneliness, loss, or waiting for something (or someone) that has not come.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

**Check for "like" or "as" to tell similes from metaphors.** If the comparison word is present, it is a simile; if the comparison is stated directly as "is" or "was" with no comparison word, it is a metaphor.

**Label rhyme scheme systematically.** Go line by line, assigning the first new rhyming sound the next unused letter (A, then B, then C, and so on), and reuse a letter only when a line's ending sound matches an earlier line exactly.

**For tone and meaning questions, list the poem's recurring images first**, then ask what feeling those images have in common (all suggesting sadness, or all suggesting energy and joy, for example).

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Confusing Simile and Metaphor

> A simile uses "like" or "as"; a metaphor states the comparison directly, with no comparison word.

"Life is *like* a rollercoaster" (simile) and "Life *is* a rollercoaster" (metaphor) express a similar idea, but the presence or absence of "like"/"as" is the specific grammatical signal that distinguishes them on an exam.

### #2: Mislabeling the Rhyme Scheme by Skipping a Line

> Assign rhyme scheme letters in order, line by line — do not skip ahead or reuse a letter for a sound that has not actually appeared yet.

A common error jumps to label a rhyme "B" before checking whether an earlier line already used a different letter for that exact same sound.

### #3: Taking a Poem's Imagery Too Literally

> Poetic imagery usually represents a broader feeling or idea, not just a literal physical description.

"Gray skies" in a poem about loneliness is not primarily about actual weather — it is a symbolic image reinforcing the poem's emotional tone. Reading imagery only literally, without connecting it to the poem's overall feeling, misses the deeper meaning being tested.
