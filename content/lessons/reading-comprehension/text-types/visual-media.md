## Core Concept

**Visual media** shares meaning through pictures, words, and their arrangement. To **interpret** a visual means to explain what its details show or suggest. Read the whole visual before deciding its message.

First separate what you can see from what you conclude. A worried face is a visible detail. Saying that a person fears a new rule is a conclusion that needs support from the rest of the image.

### Comic Strips

A **panel** is one framed part of a comic. Read panels in the order shown. Track actions, words, faces, and changes in the scene.

The last panel may contain a surprise, but it needs the earlier panels to make sense. The main point can also appear earlier or develop across the whole strip.

### Editorial Cartoons

An **editorial cartoon** uses pictures to comment on a public issue. It may support a view, question a decision, or show a problem. Some cartoons criticize several sides at once.

**Symbolism** uses an object to stand for an idea. **Labels** name what a person or object represents. A boulder labeled “taxes” must be read in relation to the people and actions around it.

**Exaggeration** makes a feature much larger or stronger than normal. **Caricature** exaggerates a person's features or behavior to help readers recognize or judge that person.

An **analogy** compares different situations to explain an idea. A cartoon might compare a public office to a slow-moving snail. **Juxtaposition** places things side by side so their difference or connection is clear.

**Irony** shows a gap between what is said or expected and what happens. A sign saying “Fast Service” beside a very long waiting line can create irony. Use the full scene to explain the gap.

### Charts, Graphs, and Tables

Read the title first. Then check labels, units, source notes, and the **legend**, which explains colors or patterns. A unit tells what a value measures, such as pesos or people.

An **axis** is a reference line on a graph. Its **scale** shows which values the tick labels represent. Count the equal spaces between labeled ticks to find the value of each step.

A **bar chart** uses bar length to show an amount. Read each bar against the scale, then rank the values from greatest to least. The tallest bar is highest only when greater values run upward on that scale.

Bars that show total amounts normally start at zero. A **truncated axis** leaves out part of the scale. This can make small differences look large because only the part above the starting value is drawn.

The following relationships help check a bar chart:

$$
\text{Shown length}=\text{value}-\text{axis start}
$$

$$
\text{Value ratio}=\dfrac{\text{first value}}{\text{second value}}
$$

A **line chart** places points on a scale and joins them to show change. Read the time order before describing a rise or fall. A rising line shows an increase when larger values run upward.

A line chart can start above zero to show small changes clearly. Check its labels instead of judging change from steepness alone. To find the change between values, use:

$$
\text{Change}=\text{later value}-\text{earlier value}
$$

A positive result means an increase. A negative result means a decrease. The size of a decrease is the earlier value minus the later value.

A **pie chart** divides a circle into sectors, or slices, showing parts of one whole. A full circle has $360$ degrees.

A **radius** is a line segment from the center of a circle to its edge. Its length tells how far the edge is from the center. The plural of radius is **radii**.

Sectors in the same pie chart have equal radii. A larger sector angle then covers more area and shows a larger share. Compare angles or stated shares when separate pie charts have different sizes.

$$
\text{Share}=\dfrac{\text{sector angle}}{360^\circ}
$$

$$
\text{Part amount}=\text{share}\times\text{total}
$$

Use the angle or stated share, not just how large a slice looks. Colors may only name categories. Do not treat a darker color as a larger amount unless the legend says so.

A **table** arranges information in rows and columns. A row runs across, and a column runs down. Read the row label and column heading together to find the right entry.

To match a statement to a table, check its category, time period, value, and unit. A correct number from the wrong row still gives a wrong statement.

## Examples

### #1: Comic Sequence

The comic shows a man building a sandcastle, admiring it, and then watching a wave wash it away. Which message is best supported by the full sequence?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: three-panel comic, panel 1 a man carefully building an elaborate sandcastle, panel 2 the completed castle with the man smiling, panel 3 a wave washing it away while the man watches with a resigned expression; no dialogue -->

**Solution:**

1. The first panels show effort followed by success.
2. The wave then destroys the result. The sequence supports the idea that careful work can be undone by forces outside a person's control.
3. The comic does not prove that the work was pointless. That stronger judgment goes beyond the pictured events.

### #2: Editorial Cartoon

In the cartoon, what view of the depicted tax burden is best supported by the labels and the figure's expression?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: editorial cartoon showing a large boulder labeled "taxes" rolling downhill toward a small worried figure labeled "citizens" -->

**Solution:**

1. The labels connect the boulder with taxes and the figure with citizens.
2. The large moving boulder and worried face show a threat. The cartoon presents the tax burden as harmful or frightening to citizens.
3. It does not name a particular tax law. A claim about a specific law would need more evidence.

### #3: Irony in a Cartoon

What makes the sign in this cartoon ironic?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: public service counter with a sign reading "Fast Service"; a long line of tired people waits beside a large snail carrying a stack of forms toward the counter; no other text -->

**Solution:**

1. The sign promises fast service, but the line and snail suggest slow service.
2. This gap creates irony. The snail also compares the service process to a slow animal.

### #4: Contrasting Words and Actions

How do the official's features and the side-by-side scenes support the cartoon's message?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: editorial cartoon with two side-by-side scenes featuring the same fictional official labeled "Official"; left scene shows the official with an extremely large mouth speaking into many microphones beside a sign "Public promises"; right scene shows the official with tiny hands beside a large pile of untouched repair requests and a sign "Public work"; keep the official's face and clothes recognizable in both scenes -->

**Solution:**

1. The very large mouth and tiny hands exaggerate the official's features. This caricature suggests much talk and little work.
2. Placing the scenes side by side creates juxtaposition. Readers can compare the public promises with the untouched requests.
3. Together, these details criticize an official who makes promises but does little to carry them out. The cartoon does not identify a real person or prove a claim about one.

### #5: Ranking Bar Values

The chart shows monthly sales. Which month has the second-highest sales, and what is the full order from highest to lowest?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: vertical bar chart titled "Monthly Sales"; horizontal labels January, February, March, April; vertical axis labeled "Sales (thousands of pesos)" from 0 to 100 in steps of 10; bars at January 50, February 70, March 60, April 90; no value labels above bars -->

**Solution:**

1. Each tick step is $10$ thousand pesos. Read the bar tops as January $50$, February $70$, March $60$, and April $90$ thousand pesos.
2. Compare the values from greatest to least: April, February, March, January.
3. February is second-highest. Its bar reaches $70$ thousand pesos.

### #6: A Truncated Bar Axis

Does the chart show that Brand A's value is five times Brand B's value? Explain using the scale.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: two bar charts comparing Brand A at 85 and Brand B at 81; left chart vertical axis runs from 80 to 86 in steps of 1 with bars drawn from 80; right chart shows the same values on a vertical axis from 0 to 90 in steps of 10; label the charts "Starts at 80" and "Starts at zero" -->

**Solution:**

1. On the left chart, the visible lengths start at $80$.

$$
85-80=5
$$

$$
81-80=1
$$

2. The visible lengths have a ratio of $5:1$. The actual values have a different ratio.

$$
\dfrac{85}{81}\approx 1.05
$$

3. Brand A's value is only about $1.05$ times Brand B's. The truncated scale makes the difference look much larger.

### #7: Line-Chart Changes

According to the line chart, during which interval did requests fall? What was the increase from Monday to Thursday?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: line chart titled "Requests Received"; horizontal axis Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday; vertical axis labeled "Requests" from 40 to 65 in steps of 5; points at Monday 50, Tuesday 60, Wednesday 55, Thursday 65 joined in time order -->

**Solution:**

1. Read the points as Monday $50$, Tuesday $60$, Wednesday $55$, and Thursday $65$. Requests fell from Tuesday to Wednesday.
2. Find the change from the first day to the last day.

$$
65-50=15
$$

3. Requests increased by $15$ overall. The axis starts at $40$, so use the point values to measure change.

### #8: Reading a Pie Sector

The pie chart shows how $120$ requests were sent. How many were sent online?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: flat pie chart titled "Ways Requests Were Sent"; sectors labeled "Online 180 degrees", "In person 120 degrees", and "Mail 60 degrees"; note below chart "Total: 120 requests"; sectors share the same radius -->

**Solution:**

1. The online sector covers $180^\circ$ out of $360^\circ$.

$$
\text{Online share}=\dfrac{180}{360}=\dfrac{1}{2}
$$

2. Multiply that share by the total.

$$
\dfrac{1}{2}\times120=60
$$

3. The chart shows $60$ online requests. The online sector also takes up half the circle's area.

### #9: Matching a Table to a Statement

Using the table, compare Center B's afternoon total with its morning total. Complete the statement: “Center B handled ___ more requests in the afternoon than in the morning.”

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: table titled "Requests Handled" with column headings "Center", "Morning", "Afternoon"; row A has 24 and 30; row B has 18 and 27; row C has 32 and 28; note "All entries are numbers of requests" -->

**Solution:**

1. Find row B. Its morning entry is $18$, and its afternoon entry is $27$.
2. Subtract the morning total from the afternoon total.

$$
27-18=9
$$

3. Complete the statement with $9$. Center B handled $9$ more requests in the afternoon.

## Reading Strategies

For a comic, ask what changes from panel to panel. Use expressions and actions as evidence. Do not read only the last panel.

For a cartoon, identify what the labels name. Then check what threatens, helps, blocks, or contrasts with what. Keep the conclusion as narrow as the picture supports.

For a chart, read values before comparing them. For a table, trace the requested row and column to their shared entry. Check the unit before writing the answer.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Reading Only the Final Panel

> A final surprise depends on what came before it.

Skipping earlier panels can hide the effort, promise, or expectation that the ending changes. Read the full sequence.

### #2: Giving Every Symbol a Fixed Meaning

> A symbol's meaning depends on its context.

An eagle or boulder does not mean the same thing in every cartoon. Labels and relationships guide the reading.

### #3: Forcing One Simple Claim onto a Cartoon

> A cartoon can question or criticize several things at once.

Explain the details that are present. Do not add a policy, person, or cause the image does not identify.

### #4: Demanding Zero on Every Graph

> Bar lengths and line positions need different checks.

A nonzero start can mislead when comparing total bar lengths. A line chart may use a nonzero scale to show change clearly, but its labels still need careful reading.

### #5: Using the Right Value from the Wrong Place

> Match the category and heading before taking a number.

A table's afternoon value cannot answer a morning question. A chart's unit may also change what a number means.
