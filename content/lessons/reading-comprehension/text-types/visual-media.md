## Core Concept

**Visual media** can communicate through images, words, layout, sequence, symbols, and quantitative encodings. Interpretation should use all relevant visual evidence rather than rely on one "shortcut" panel or symbol.

### Comic Strips

Comic strips create meaning through the relationship among panels: sequence, framing, dialogue, captions, facial expressions, gesture, setting, timing, and visual contrast. A punch line or reversal often occurs late in a comic, but the main point is not **always** in the final panel. Read the full sequence.

### Editorial Cartoons

Editorial cartoons comment on public issues using techniques such as symbolism, caricature, analogy, labeling, juxtaposition, irony, and exaggeration. Many take a recognizable stance, but a cartoon can also pose a question, expose a contradiction, or satirize several sides at once.

- **Symbolism:** an image represents or evokes a broader idea.
- **Caricature:** selected traits, often physical or behavioral, are exaggerated for recognition, humor, or criticism.
- **Labels:** text identifies what an object, figure, or idea represents.

Interpret a symbol from context. An eagle does not have one universal meaning in every image, and a labeled boulder's significance depends on its relation to other elements.

### Charts and Graphs

Charts encode quantitative information through position, length, angle, area, color, or other visual channels. Read titles, units, labels, legends, source notes, and axis scales before drawing a conclusion.

A **bar chart** uses bar length to represent magnitude. Because length is measured from a baseline, truncating the quantitative axis can exaggerate differences; a zero baseline is normally appropriate when bar lengths encode absolute magnitudes. By contrast, a **line chart** emphasizes position and change, so its vertical axis does not necessarily need to begin at zero. The scale should match the analytical purpose and make changes interpretable without distortion.

## Examples

### #1: Comic Sequence

A three-panel comic shows a man carefully building an elaborate sandcastle, the completed castle, then a wave washing it away while he watches with a resigned expression.


**Solution:**

The final panel matters because it reverses the earlier buildup, but the interpretation depends on **all three panels**: deliberate effort, completion, then sudden destruction. A supported reading concerns impermanence, uncontrollable natural forces, or the fragility of effort. The exact theme should match any surrounding text or answer choices.

### #2: Editorial Cartoon

A cartoon shows a large boulder labeled "taxes" rolling toward a worried figure labeled "citizens."


**Solution:**

The scale, motion, labels, and facial expression frame taxes as a threatening burden on citizens. This supports a critical stance toward the depicted tax burden, but do not infer a more specific policy claim than the cartoon itself supplies.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

**For comics, trace the change across panels.** Ask what each panel adds and how the sequence creates contrast, surprise, or development.

**For cartoons, decode relationships before stance.** Identify labels/symbols, then ask what is threatening, supporting, blocking, mocking, or being compared.

**For charts, identify the visual encoding.** Bar length, point position, line slope, area, and color do different jobs and have different scale considerations.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: "The Last Panel Contains the Main Point"

> Many jokes and reversals culminate in the final panel, but some comics distribute meaning across the whole sequence or place the key information earlier.

### #2: Reading Symbols as Universal Codes

> Symbols gain meaning from context, labels, culture, and relationships within the image.

### #3: Assuming Every Editorial Cartoon Has One Simple Position

> Cartoons can criticize several actors, present irony, or expose contradictions without reducing to one slogan.

### #4: "Every Graph Must Start at Zero"

> Zero is especially important for bar charts whose lengths encode magnitude. Line charts and other positional displays may legitimately use nonzero ranges when the scale is clear and suited to the analytical question.
