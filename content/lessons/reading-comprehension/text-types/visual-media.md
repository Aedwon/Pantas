## Core Concept

Not every reading comprehension passage is plain text. **Visual media** questions ask you to interpret meaning from images combined with (or instead of) words.

**Comic strips** tell a short story or joke through a sequence of panels, usually relying on both the dialogue (in speech bubbles) and the visual details (facial expressions, actions, setting) to build meaning. The humor or point often depends on a twist or contrast in the final panel.

**Editorial cartoons** use images, symbols, and often exaggeration (**caricature**) to comment on a current event or issue, usually taking a clear stance, similar to a written editorial. Common techniques include:
- **Symbolism**: an object or figure standing in for a bigger idea (an eagle representing a nation, a scale representing justice).
- **Caricature**: exaggerating a real person's features for comic or critical effect.
- **Labels**: words written directly on parts of the image to make the symbolism unmistakable (labeling a boulder "taxes" as it crushes a figure labeled "citizens").

**Charts and graphs** present numerical data visually. Reading comprehension questions about them test whether you can accurately extract information (reading specific values, comparing categories, spotting trends) and avoid misreading the visual due to a misleading scale or an unlabeled axis.

## Examples

### #1: Interpreting a Comic Strip's Final Panel

A three-panel comic strip shows a man carefully building an elaborate sandcastle, then in the final panel, a wave washes it away as he looks on with a resigned expression. What is the likely point of this comic strip?

**Solution:**
1. The buildup (careful, elaborate construction) contrasts sharply with the sudden, effortless destruction by the wave in the final panel.
2. This contrast, combined with the man's resigned (rather than shocked) expression, suggests a point about the fleeting or temporary nature of effort against larger, uncontrollable forces (like nature, or the passage of time).
3. The humor or insight comes specifically from that final-panel contrast, a common structure in comic strips.

### #2: Interpreting Symbolism in an Editorial Cartoon

An editorial cartoon shows a large boulder labeled "taxes" rolling toward a small figure labeled "citizens," who looks worried. What is the cartoonist's likely stance on this tax issue?

**Solution:**
1. The boulder (a large, crushing object) is labeled "taxes," and it is shown threatening a much smaller figure labeled "citizens."
2. This visual relationship (big, threatening object vs. small, vulnerable figure) suggests the cartoonist views the tax as burdensome or harmful to ordinary citizens.
3. The cartoonist's stance is critical of the tax policy, portraying it as an unfair or overwhelming weight on citizens.

### #3: Reading a Bar Graph Correctly

A bar graph shows monthly sales for a store: January at $50$, February at $70$, March at $60$, and April at $90$ (in thousands of pesos). Which month had the second-highest sales?

**Solution:**
1. List the values: January $50$, February $70$, March $60$, April $90$.
2. The highest value is April ($90$).
3. The second-highest value is February ($70$), which is higher than March's $60$ and January's $50$.
4. The answer is February.

### #4: Spotting a Misleading Graph Scale

A bar graph's vertical axis starts at $80$ instead of $0$, making a bar for $85$ appear almost five times taller than a bar for $81$. Explain why this visual impression is misleading.

**Solution:**
1. The actual difference between $85$ and $81$ is only $4$ units, a small and modest difference.
2. But because the axis starts at $80$ (not $0$), the visual height difference between the two bars is exaggerated far beyond what the actual numbers represent.
3. This is a classic misleading graph technique: always check the axis starting point before judging how large a visual difference actually is.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

**For comic strips, focus on the last panel** — the humor, twist, or main point is almost always concentrated there, building on the setup from earlier panels.

**For editorial cartoons, identify what each labeled or symbolic element represents first**, then determine the relationship between them (which one is winning, threatening, helping, or losing) to find the cartoonist's stance.

**For charts and graphs, always check the axis scale first**, before comparing bar heights or line steepness visually — a truncated or unusually scaled axis can distort your first impression.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Focusing Only on the Dialogue in a Comic Strip

> The visual details (facial expressions, actions, background) often carry just as much meaning as the words in the speech bubbles.

A comic strip's point can hinge entirely on a character's facial expression in the final panel, even if the dialogue itself seems neutral. Read the images as carefully as the text.

### #2: Missing the Cartoonist's Stance by Reading the Cartoon Too Literally

> Editorial cartoons use symbolism and exaggeration deliberately — look past the literal image to the idea it represents.

A boulder is not literally about geology; in an editorial cartoon labeled "taxes," it represents a burden. Reading only the literal picture without connecting it to the labeled concept misses the cartoon's actual argument.

### #3: Trusting a Graph's Visual Impression Without Checking the Scale

> A graph's axis may not start at zero, which can make small differences look much larger than they actually are.

Always read the actual numbers on the axis before concluding "this bar is way bigger" — the visual proportions can be intentionally or accidentally misleading.
