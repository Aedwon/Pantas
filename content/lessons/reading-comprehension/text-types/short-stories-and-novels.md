## Core Concept

**Short stories** and **novel excerpts** can be studied through plot, character, setting, conflict, narration, style, and theme. These are tools for reading, not rules that every story must follow.

Every claim needs evidence: point to the words that show it. To **infer** means to work out something the text does not say directly, using clues.

### Plot

**Plot** is the order in which a story tells its events. A common model has five parts: exposition (the start), rising action, climax, falling action, and resolution (the end). Not every story fits it. A story can start in the middle, jump back in time, or end without an answer.

The **climax** is the main turning point, where the central problem is decided or changes direction. It is not just the most exciting scene.

### Character

The **protagonist** is the main character, whose goals and choices the story follows. The **antagonistic force** is whatever opposes the protagonist. It need not be a villain. It can be a storm, a custom, or the protagonist's own fear.

Two questions about a character are different:

- **Round or flat?** This is about complexity. A round character has several sides, which may clash. A flat character shows only one or two traits.
- **Dynamic or static?** This is about change. A dynamic character changes in a big way. A static character stays the same.

A flat character can change, and a round character can stay the same.

### Setting

**Setting** is the time, the place, and the social situation, such as the customs and the weather. It can create a problem for a character or limit what the character can do.

### Conflict

**Conflict** is a struggle that drives the story. A story can have several. The common types are:

- **Character vs. character:** two people oppose each other.
- **Character vs. nature:** a character faces weather, animals, or a place.
- **Character vs. society:** a character faces a rule, a custom, or a group.
- **Character vs. self:** the character struggles inside with a choice, a fear, or a feeling. Being tired or hurt is a hardship of the body. It is an inner conflict only when the character fights with his or her own mind.

### Narration and Point of View

The **narrator** is the voice that tells the story. The **point of view** is where the narrator stands.

- **First person:** a character tells the story with "I". You know only what that character knows.
- **Third person limited:** an outside voice shows the thoughts of one character.
- **Third person omniscient:** an outside voice knows the thoughts of many characters.

A narrator may be wrong or unfair, so compare what the narrator says with what the characters do.

### Theme and Style

**Theme** is an idea the story explores, such as pride, fear, or belonging. It is not always a moral. Find it by looking at what repeats: similar choices, images, and results. Then state it in a full sentence.

**Style** is how the author writes: the words, the sentence length, and the images. Style helps create mood. You can say what a style seems meant to do, but only from the words on the page.

## Examples

### #1: Conflict Inside and Outside

> Mang Ador's hands were raw from the line. The big fish had dragged his boat past the reef, and the sun was low. He wanted to cut the rope, but he remembered the empty pots at home. "Not yet," he whispered.

Which conflict does the whispered "Not yet" show?

**Solution:**

1. The fish, the reef, and the low sun are character vs. nature.
2. Raw hands are a hardship of the body, not an inner conflict.
3. "Not yet" shows him choosing between giving up and feeding his family. That is character vs. self.

The passage has both conflicts, but the question asks about "Not yet".

### #2: Telling the Types of Conflict Apart

Name the main conflict in each case.

- **A:** Lia and her cousin both claim the same boat and will not speak to each other.
- **B:** An old rule in the village says only men may speak at the meeting, but Tala wants to speak.

**Solution:**

1. In A, two people oppose each other: character vs. character.
2. In B, Tala is opposed by a rule of the village, not by one person: character vs. society.

### #3: Finding the Climax

> The dam is cracking. Mira warns the mayor, who laughs at her. She runs upriver and opens the gate herself as the wall gives way. The water drains safely into the fields. The villagers thank her.

Which event is the climax?

**Solution:**

1. The warning is tense, but the problem is still open. This is rising action.
2. Opening the gate decides whether the village is saved. It is the climax.
3. The draining water and the thanks come after it: falling action and resolution.

### #4: A Story Told Out of Order

> Today, Ben sold the last of his father's tools. He remembered the summer he was ten, when his father let him hold the old plane. That summer he promised to open a shop.

Which happened first in time, the sale or the promise?

**Solution:**

The promise came first. The story starts with the sale, then jumps back. The order of telling is not always the order of happening.

### #5: Round or Flat, Dynamic or Static

> Dino shouted at every clerk who made a mistake. Yet each Friday he quietly mailed half his pay to his sick sister and hid the receipts. After the flood, he stacked sandbags all night beside the same clerks. At dawn he asked each of them for their name.

> Mrs. Reyes, the landlady, appears only on rent day in her gray shawl. She says "Next month" and nothing else.

Describe Dino and Mrs. Reyes.

**Solution:**

1. Dino is harsh but secretly kind. He has several sides, so he is round.
2. He starts by shouting at the clerks and ends by asking their names. That is a big change, so he is dynamic.
3. Mrs. Reyes shows one trait and one action, so she is flat. She does not change, so she is static.

### #6: Setting

> The ferry had not run since the typhoon, so Ana walked the long dike at night with her little sister on her back.

How does the setting shape what Ana must do?

**Solution:**

The time (after a typhoon) and the place (a dike at night) remove the easy way home. They force Ana into a hard, risky walk.

### #7: Point of View

> **A:** I slammed the door. Nobody in this house ever listens to me.

> **B:** Rica slammed the door. She believed nobody in the house listened to her. In the kitchen, her mother only wished the girl would stay for supper.

Name the point of view of each passage.

**Solution:**

1. A uses "I", so it is first person. We know only what Rica says and thinks.
2. B shows the thoughts of Rica and of her mother, so it is third person omniscient.
3. Only B tells us the mother's wish. In A, we cannot tell if Rica is right.

### #8: Theme From a Pattern

> Scene one: Lina hides her grandmother's woven mat when her friends visit. Scene two: at the fiesta, classmates ask her to teach them to weave. Scene three: Lina sits and weaves beside her grandmother in front of the whole town.

State a theme, and connect it to real life.

**Solution:**

1. Look for what repeats. Lina goes from hiding the mat to sharing it, as others show interest.
2. A supported theme is that pride in a family tradition grows when it is shared.
3. In real life, a student may feel shy about a home language until classmates show they value it.

### #9: Reading a Filipino Excerpt

> Madilim na nang makauwi si Aling Rosa mula sa palengke. Nakita niyang bukas ang pinto at wala ang anak niyang si Ben. Sa mesa ay may isang sulat. Hindi niya ito agad binuksan dahil natatakot siyang malaman ang nakasulat.

(*Madilim*: dark. *Palengke*: market. *Sulat*: letter. *Natatakot*: afraid.) What conflict is shown at the end? Which statement is supported: "Ben ran away", or "Aling Rosa fears what the note says"?

**Solution:**

1. Aling Rosa wants to know the truth, but she is afraid. That is character vs. self.
2. The text says she fears what is written, so the second statement is supported.
3. The text never says why Ben is gone or what the letter says. "Ben ran away" goes beyond the evidence.

## Reading Strategies

**Track goals, obstacles, and change.** These show the central conflict and the turning points better than the loudest scene does.

**Quote the evidence.** Before you pick an answer, find the words that support it. An open door and a letter do not tell you what is in the letter.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Treating the Five-Part Plot as a Rule

> The five parts are a model, not a rule for all stories.

### #2: Equating Flat with Static and Round with Dynamic

> Complexity and change are different questions.

### #3: Treating the Antagonist as Always a Villain

> The antagonistic force can be a storm, a custom, or a fear.

### #4: Reducing Theme to a Moral

> A theme can be an idea or a question, not only a lesson.

### #5: Calling the Loudest Scene the Climax

> The turning point matters more than the noise.

### #6: Calling Every Hardship an Inner Conflict

> Hunger and tiredness are body problems. Character vs. self needs an inner struggle shown in the text.
