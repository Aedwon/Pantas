## Core Concept

**Sequencing** questions ask you to identify the correct ORDER in which events happened. This sounds simple, but passages frequently describe events OUT OF chronological order — through flashbacks, foreshadowing, or simply mentioning a later consequence before circling back to explain its cause.

Three tools help determine the true sequence.

**Time-signal words** directly indicate order: *first*, *next*, *then*, *after*, *before*, *later*, *meanwhile*, *finally*, *previously*, *once*, *by the time*. These are the most direct clues, but a passage does not always include them for every event.

**Verb tense** can reveal sequence even without signal words. The PAST PERFECT tense ("had walked," "had finished") usually marks an event that happened BEFORE another past-tense event mentioned nearby ("She had already left by the time he arrived" — "had already left" happened FIRST, "arrived" happened second).

**Logical cause-and-effect** also implies order: a CAUSE must happen BEFORE its EFFECT, even if the passage mentions the effect first and explains the cause afterward.

## Examples

### #1: Using Time-Signal Words

"First, the dough is kneaded for ten minutes. Next, it rests for an hour to rise. Finally, it is baked at high heat until golden brown." What is the correct order of these three steps?

**Solution:**
1. The words "First," "Next," and "Finally" directly label the order.
2. Order: kneading → resting/rising → baking.

### #2: Using Past Perfect Tense to Find an Earlier Event

"By the time the fire department arrived, the family had already evacuated the building safely." Which event happened FIRST: the fire department arriving, or the family evacuating?

**Solution:**
1. "Had already evacuated" is in the PAST PERFECT tense, signaling an action completed BEFORE another past event.
2. "Arrived" is the simple past tense, marking the LATER reference point.
3. The family evacuating happened FIRST, before the fire department arrived.

### #3: Reordering Events Presented Out of Sequence

A passage begins: "The bridge collapsed within minutes, sending shockwaves through the city." It then explains: "Engineers had warned officials about structural weaknesses two years earlier, but the necessary repairs were repeatedly delayed due to budget disputes." What is the ACTUAL chronological order of these events?

**Solution:**
1. The passage presents the COLLAPSE (the effect) FIRST, but then explains the CAUSE (warnings and delayed repairs) afterward.
2. Logically, warnings and delays (the cause) must have happened BEFORE the collapse (the effect), even though the passage describes them in reverse order.
3. True chronological order: engineers warn officials (two years earlier) → repairs are delayed due to budget disputes → the bridge collapses.

### #4: Combining Multiple Sequencing Clues

"She had studied law for six years before finally passing the bar exam. Afterward, she spent two years as a junior associate before being promoted to partner." Order these four events: (a) becoming partner, (b) studying law, (c) passing the bar exam, (d) working as a junior associate.

**Solution:**
1. "Had studied... before finally passing" places studying law BEFORE passing the bar exam.
2. "Afterward" places working as a junior associate AFTER passing the bar exam.
3. "Before being promoted to partner" places becoming partner AFTER working as a junior associate.
4. Full order: (b) studying law → (c) passing the bar exam → (d) working as a junior associate → (a) becoming partner.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

**Underline every time-signal word first** (first, next, then, before, after, finally) as you read, since these give you the most direct, reliable ordering clues.

**Watch specifically for past perfect verbs** ("had done," "had finished," "had already"), since these almost always mark an event that happened BEFORE another nearby past-tense event.

**When a passage states a RESULT before its CAUSE, mentally flip the order** — the cause always happened first in actual chronological time, even if the passage's writing order presents the effect first for dramatic or explanatory purposes.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Assuming the Passage's Written Order Matches Chronological Order

> Passages, especially in news writing or storytelling, often describe events OUT OF chronological order (leading with a dramatic outcome, then explaining the backstory).

The bridge-collapse example describes the collapse FIRST in the passage, but the collapse actually happened LAST in the true timeline of events. Do not assume "mentioned first" means "happened first."

### #2: Missing the Signal Given by Past Perfect Tense

> "Had done" signals an EARLIER event relative to a nearby simple past-tense event — overlooking this tense difference can reverse the true order.

Treating "had already evacuated" and "arrived" as happening at the same time, or in the order they are written, ignores the past perfect tense's specific signal that evacuation happened FIRST.

### #3: Forgetting That a Cause Must Precede Its Effect

> No matter which order a passage MENTIONS a cause and its effect, the cause must have happened FIRST in actual time.

If a passage explains WHY something happened only after already describing WHAT happened, use logical cause-and-effect reasoning to correctly place the cause earlier in the true chronological sequence.
