## Core Concept

**Specific detail** questions ask about a precise fact stated in the passage: a name, date, number, or particular event. Unlike main idea or inference questions, these have one clear, directly stated answer that can be located in the text.

Two reading techniques help you handle these questions efficiently, especially under time pressure.

**Skimming** means reading quickly through a passage to get a general sense of its content and structure, without reading every word carefully. Skimming is useful before answering questions, to build a rough mental map of where different topics appear in the passage (introduction, examples, conclusion).

**Scanning** means searching quickly through a passage for a specific piece of information — a name, number, date, or keyword — without reading everything around it. Scanning is useful after you know exactly what detail a question is asking for, letting your eyes jump straight to the relevant section.

The key skill for detail questions: identify the exact keyword or phrase from the question (a name, number, or unique term), then scan the passage for that keyword, rather than re-reading the entire passage from the beginning.

## Examples

### #1: Scanning for a Specific Number

A passage states: "The bridge, completed in 1932, spans 1,280 meters and was, at the time, the longest suspension bridge in the world." What year was the bridge completed?

**Solution:**
1. The question asks for a specific detail: the completion year.
2. Scan the passage for a number that looks like a year.
3. The passage directly states "completed in 1932." The answer is 1932.

### #2: Scanning for a Specific Name

A passage mentions several scientists but states: "It was Dr. Elena Cruz who first proposed the theory, though her colleague, Dr. Ramon Vega, later refined the mathematical model." Who first proposed the theory?

**Solution:**
1. The question asks specifically about who proposed the theory first, not who refined it later.
2. Scanning the sentence, "Dr. Elena Cruz" is directly linked to "first proposed," while "Dr. Ramon Vega" is linked to "later refined."
3. The answer is Dr. Elena Cruz.

### #3: Using Skimming to Locate the Right Section First

A long passage has four paragraphs: one about a discovery's history, one about its scientific explanation, one about its modern applications, and one about future research. A question asks about a specific detail related to "modern applications." Which paragraph should you scan first?

**Solution:**
1. Skimming the passage's structure first (even just reading the first sentence of each paragraph) reveals which paragraph focuses on which topic.
2. The question specifically concerns "modern applications," matching the third paragraph.
3. Go directly to the third paragraph and scan there, rather than starting from the first paragraph and reading through everything.

### #4: Avoiding a Close but Incorrect Detail

A passage states: "The festival, historically held in March, was moved to April in 2015 due to scheduling conflicts, and has remained in April ever since." In what month is the festival currently held?

**Solution:**
1. The passage mentions both March (the historical month) and April (the month after the 2015 change).
2. The question asks for the current month, which requires noticing the change described ("moved to April... has remained in April ever since").
3. The correct answer is April, not March — reading carefully to the end of the sentence, not stopping at the first month mentioned, is essential here.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

**Extract the exact keyword from the question before scanning.** If a question asks "what year did X happen," scan specifically for numbers and words related to X, rather than re-reading everything.

**Skim first for structure, then scan for details.** A quick skim of paragraph topics (often visible from first sentences) tells you where to scan, saving significant time on longer passages.

**Watch for signal words indicating a change over time**, like "later," "eventually," "used to," or "since," which often mean the passage mentions two different facts (an old one and a current one) — make sure you answer with the one the question actually asks for.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Stopping at the First Matching Detail

> A passage may mention several similar details (multiple names, numbers, or dates) — make sure the one you pick actually answers the specific question asked.

If a passage mentions two scientists, and the question asks about the one who "first" did something, do not simply grab the first name you see while scanning; confirm it matches the exact role the question asks about.

### #2: Confusing Historical and Current Facts

> When a passage describes a change over time (something that used to be true, then changed), the question may specifically ask for the original fact, the new fact, or the reason for the change — read carefully to identify which one is being asked.

The festival month example shows how easy it is to answer with the old fact (March) when the question asks for the current one (April), simply because the old fact appeared first in the sentence.

### #3: Skimming When the Question Actually Requires Careful Scanning

> Skimming is for getting a general overview; it is not sufficient for answering a specific-detail question accurately.

Relying only on a fast skim for a question that asks for an exact number, name, or date risks missing the precise detail; switch to careful scanning once you know exactly what specific fact you need to find.
