## Core Concept

### Following a Pointing Word

**Referential cohesion** means linking ideas with words that point to something else, such as *this*, *these*, *they* and *them*. A pointing word usually looks back to something already named. To find it, check the meaning and whether the word is singular or plural. The nearest noun is not always the match.

Let's find what "These delays" refers to in the passage about the ferry:

> The ferry left an hour late on Monday. On Tuesday, the tide kept it at the pier for two hours. These delays made many workers miss the first shift.

1. "These delays" is plural, so it points to more than one thing.
2. Look back: "left an hour late on Monday" and "kept it at the pier for two hours" are two delays.

"These delays" refers to the ferry leaving late on Monday and waiting at the pier on Tuesday.

**Check:** Put it back in: "The late departures on Monday and Tuesday made many workers miss the first shift." It reads correctly.

:::pair #points-forward
Right: "These delays" = the late departures that come before the sentence.
Wrong: Visits to collect a completed sample; comparison of two services.
The wrong choice points to an action that comes later. A pointing word usually looks back to what was already named.
:::

:::check
In the passage, what does "them" refer to?

> Mrs. Reyes taught her class to fold paper boats. The pupils tested them in a basin of water. Most floated, but two sank.

A) The paper boats

B) The pupils

C) The basin of water

D) Mrs. Reyes
:::answer
A. The pupils tested the paper boats, and "them" is plural. The pupils are the ones doing the testing, so B does not fit.
:::

### What Each Paragraph Does

Each paragraph has a job, such as introducing a problem, giving evidence or proposing a response. The jobs together form a **pattern**. Common patterns are:

- **Sequence**: events or steps in order.
- **Cause and effect**: why something happens.
- **Compare and contrast**: likenesses and differences.
- **Problem and solution**: a difficulty and a response.

Signal words such as *first*, *as a result* and *however* often help, but they do not always appear. Passages can mix patterns, so choose the one that organizes the whole text.

Let's name the pattern of the passage about the school fair:

> The school fair first sold tickets at the gate. Then pupils lined up for the games. Finally, the winners received their prizes on the stage.

1. "first sold tickets", "Then pupils lined up" and "Finally, the winners received" each give a step in time order.
2. Time order holds the whole passage together.

Pattern: sequence.

**Check:** Reorder the sentences and the story no longer makes sense.

:::check
Which pattern organizes the passage?

> Tricycles can enter narrow alleys and cost little for a short trip. Jeepneys carry more people and cost less per person on long routes.

A) Sequence

B) Compare and contrast

C) Cause and effect

D) Problem and solution
:::answer
B. The passage sets two kinds of transport side by side and shows how they differ. No step order, cause or problem is given.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Lunch Money

What does "This habit" refer to, and how is the passage mainly organized?

> Pupils in the Grade 4 room often forgot their lunch money. This habit left some of them hungry by noon. The teacher began a small box where pupils could drop coins every Monday. Since then, nobody has gone hungry at noon.

A) Forgetting lunch money; problem and solution

B) Dropping coins in the box; problem and solution

C) Being hungry at noon; sequence

D) Forgetting lunch money; compare and contrast

**Solution:**

1. "This habit" is singular, and it points to "often forgot their lunch money".
2. "forgot their lunch money" and "left some of them hungry" are a difficulty. "began a small box" is the response.
3. A difficulty followed by a response is problem and solution.

"This habit" refers to forgetting lunch money, and the pattern is problem and solution.

:::pair #pattern-inside
Right: Problem and solution
Wrong: Visits made after revision of the instructions; cause and effect.
Many passages contain causes. Name the pattern that organizes the whole passage, here a difficulty and its remedy.
:::

**Check:** Put it back in: "Forgetting lunch money left some of them hungry by noon." It reads correctly, and no comparison appears.

### #2: The Dry Taps

What does "These long queues" refer to, and how is the passage organized?

> A broken water pump left the village taps dry for three days. As a result, families lined up at the creek with buckets. These long queues delayed the morning market.

**Solution:**

1. "These long queues" is plural, so it points back to people lined up.
2. Look back: "families lined up at the creek with buckets" is the match.

"These long queues" refers to the families lined up at the creek with buckets.

:::try
Finish the last step: is the pattern cause and effect, or sequence?
:::answer
Cause and effect. The broken pump caused the dry taps ("As a result"), and the queues caused the delay ("delayed"). Each event leads to the next, not just follows it in time.

**Check:** Reread each sentence and ask what it caused. The pump caused the queues, and the queues caused the delay.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- A pointing word: look back for what it names, and match its singular or plural form. {#points-forward}
- Several patterns appear: name the one that organizes the whole passage. {#pattern-inside}
- A signal word that is missing: read what each paragraph does. {#what-each-paragraph-does}
:::
