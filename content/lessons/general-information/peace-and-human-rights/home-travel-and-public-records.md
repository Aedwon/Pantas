## Core Concept

### Section $6$: Home Choice and Travel

Article III, Section $6$ protects **liberty of abode** (choice of home) and the **right to travel**. Each freedom carries its own constitutional test.

Liberty of abode may be impaired only by a **lawful order of the court**, within limits set by law. An official cannot force residents to relocate by memo alone.

The right to travel may be limited only when **provided by law** and serving **national security, public safety, or public health**. Both parts must hold: an authorizing law and a listed ground. When a valid law protects one of those grounds, a travel limit can be lawful.

Test a roadblock set by a police chief's verbal order without a statute, citing traffic safety:

1. Check the ground: public safety is a listed interest.
2. Check legal basis: no statute authorizes the roadblock, failing Section $6$.

Result: A safety claim cannot save a travel limit lacking an authorizing law.

:::pair #travel-needs-law-and-ground
Right: Require both an authorizing law and national security, public safety, or public health.
Wrong: Treating an official's bare claim of public safety as enough without a law.
Section $6$ requires travel limits to be provided by law alongside a listed ground.
:::

:::pair #travel-limits-can-be-lawful
Right: Allow travel limits provided by law for national security, public safety, or public health.
Wrong: Treating every travel restriction as forbidden even when authorized by law.
Travel is strongly protected, yet Section $6$ permits lawful restrictions on those three grounds.
:::

:::check
A statute authorizes closing a mountain road during a landslide alert to protect public safety. Does Section $6$ bar that closure?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. The closure is provided by law and serves public safety.
:::

### Section $7$: Access to Public Records

Article III, Section $7$ protects access to information on matters of **public concern**. Citizens may inspect official records and policy research data, subject to limits set by law.

This right does not open private files made confidential by statute, such as medical charts. Still, fear of criticism or office embarrassment is never a lawful limit.

Compare two record requests at city hall:

1. A taxpayer asks for bridge contracts where no legal limit applies: Section $7$ grants access.
2. A neighbor asks for a clerk's private health file protected by law: legal limits protect it.

Result: Section $7$ opens public-concern records unless a specific law restricts them.

:::why
Why does Section $7$ balance public access with legal limits? Open records let citizens inspect public spending, while narrow laws protect valid privacy and security secrets.
:::

:::pair #criticism-is-no-lawful-limit
Right: Require a limit set by law before denying access to records on public concerns.
Wrong: Withholding public records simply because release would draw criticism or embarrass officials.
Only legal limits restrict Section $7$, never an office's fear of criticism.
:::

:::check
A parent asks a school board for its published cafeteria supplier contract, with no restricting law. Must the board grant access?
:::answer
The answer is **Yes**. Supplier contracts concern public funds, and no lawful limit applies.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Forced Relocation Without Court Order

A provincial administrator orders a family by letter to move homes to another town. No court issued an order. Does that follow Section $6$?

**Solution:**

1. Identify the right: choosing where to live is liberty of abode under Section $6$.
2. Check the rule: impairing abode requires a lawful court order within legal limits.

Answer: No, forcing relocation without a court order violates liberty of abode.

**Check:** Choice of home needs a court order; travel limits need a law plus security, safety, or health.

### #2: Public Budget Versus Private Health File

A civic group requests two records: last year's road payroll ledger and an employee's hospital file. No law hides the ledger, while privacy law protects medical files. How does Section $7$ apply?

**Solution:**

1. Check the payroll ledger: public spending is a public concern with no legal limit.
2. Check the medical file: Section $7$ is subject to legal limits protecting private medical data.

Answer: Section $7$ opens the payroll ledger while lawful limits protect the medical file.

**Check:** Public concern and legal limits separate open government records from protected private files.

### #3: Unwritten Island Ban and Hidden Inspection Logs

During a festival, a port chief issues an unwritten ban stopping island departures to keep dock lines short. He also hides port safety inspection logs to avoid bad press. No law restricts either item. Rule on both actions.

**Solution:**

1. Check the travel ban: test the unwritten ban for dock lines against Section $6$'s law and ground rules.
2. Check the inspection logs: test whether avoiding bad press supplies a lawful limit under Section $7$.

:::try
Finish the last step: rule on the unwritten travel ban and the withheld safety logs.
:::answer
The answer is **Both violate Article III**. The travel ban lacks a law and a listed ground under Section $6$, and bad press never overrides Section $7$ access.

**Check:** Check Section $6$ for a law plus security, safety, or health, and Section $7$ for a legal limit.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Official cites public safety: verify a law authorizes the travel limit too. {#travel-needs-law-and-ground}
- Travel limit has legal backing: remember valid laws for security, safety, or health are allowed. {#travel-limits-can-be-lawful}
- Office fears bad press: refuse to treat criticism or embarrassment as a Section $7$ limit. {#criticism-is-no-lawful-limit}
:::
