## Core Concept

### Section $14$: Presumption of Innocence and Defense Rights

Article III, Section $14$ requires criminal due process and presumes every accused **innocent until guilt is proved**. In criminal trials, the accused holds these defense rights:

1. A lawyer and a chance to be heard.
2. Notice of the nature and cause of the charge.
3. A speedy, impartial, and public trial.
4. Face-to-face witness questioning and compulsory process to produce defense witnesses and evidence.

Match the stage to its section. Police questioning belongs to Section $12$, bail to Section $13$, and criminal-trial rights to Section $14$.

Test a judge's order barring defense subpoenas for bank receipts and locking out spectators:

1. Check defense process: blocking subpoenas denies compulsory production of evidence.
2. Check trial fairness: locking spectators out violates the right to a public trial.

Result: Both rulings violate Section $14$ trial protections.

:::pair #match-trial-rights-to-section-fourteen
Right: Place defense evidence production, witness confrontation, and trial fairness under Section $14$.
Wrong: Confusing courtroom defense rights with police custodial-questioning rules under Section $12$.
Check the stage: courtroom defense and witness rights belong in Section $14$.
:::

:::check
A court refuses to tell an accused what crime the charge alleges until trial starts. Which Section $14$ right is broken?
:::answer
The answer is **The right to know the nature and cause of the accusation**. Section $14$ requires notice so the defense can prepare.
:::

### When Trial Continues Without the Accused

**Arraignment** is the formal reading of the charge so the accused can enter a plea. After arraignment, trial may continue in absence only when **both** conditions hold:

1. The accused received **proper notice** of trial.
2. The absence is **unjustifiable** (no valid excuse).

Continuing trial lets the court hear evidence on schedule, yet absence never proves guilt. Prosecutors must still prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt.

Test a case where an accused pleads not guilty, receives notice, then enters intensive care for surgery:

1. Check arraignment and notice: both happened before the hearing.
2. Check excuse: emergency surgery is a valid excuse, so absence is justifiable.

Result: Trial cannot proceed as an unjustified absence because the third condition failed.

:::why
Why require arraignment before trial can proceed in absence? Arraignment ensures the accused formally heard and answered the charge before any hearing continues without them.
:::

:::pair #absence-needs-arraignment-and-notice
Right: Allow trial in absence only after arraignment, proper notice, and unjustifiable absence.
Wrong: Stopping trial whenever an accused skips court after arraignment and proper notice.
Once all three Section $14$ conditions meet, an accused cannot halt trial by skipping.
:::

:::pair #absence-does-not-prove-guilt
Right: Continue hearing evidence when conditions meet without treating absence as guilt.
Wrong: Treating an accused's absence as proof of guilt removing the need for trial.
Section $14$ keeps the presumption of innocence intact, so prosecutors must still prove guilt.
:::

:::check
An accused skips court before arraignment ever took place. May the judge proceed directly with trial in absence?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Trial in absence requires arraignment first, plus proper notice and unjustifiable absence.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Secret Trial and Presumed Guilt

A judge clears spectators from a courtroom and remarks that the accused looks guilty before hearing any witness. Which protections are broken?

**Solution:**

1. Check the closed room: Section $14$ guarantees a public and impartial trial.
2. Check the remark: Section $14$ protects the presumption of innocence until proved guilty.

Answer: The ruling breaks the presumption of innocence and public, impartial trial rights.

**Check:** Confirm the stage is a criminal trial, matching Section $14$.

### #2: Missing Notice After Arraignment

An accused pleads not guilty at arraignment. The clerk mails notice to an old wrong address, so the accused misses trial. May trial continue?

**Solution:**

1. Check arraignment: completed when the plea was entered.
2. Check notice: the accused never received proper trial notice.

Answer: No, trial cannot continue without due notice.

**Check:** All three rules must hold: arraignment, proper notice, and unjustifiable absence.

### #3: Vacation Skip After Arraignment

A defendant is arraigned and receives notice for Monday's trial. He skips court for a beach trip with no excuse. The prosecutor asks the judge to enter a guilty verdict immediately without witness testimony. Rule on both points.

**Solution:**

1. Check continuation: test arraignment, notice, and the beach trip against Section $14$ absence conditions.
2. Check verdict: test whether absence removes the presumption of innocence and trial proof.

:::try
Finish the last step: rule on continuing trial and whether the judge may convict without evidence.
:::answer
The answer is **Trial may continue in absence, but the judge cannot convict without trial proof**. Arraignment, notice, and unjustifiable absence are met, yet absence never proves guilt.

**Check:** Verify all three Section $14$ absence rules, and keep the presumption of innocence.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Evidence or confrontation blocked: match courtroom trial rights to Section $14$. {#match-trial-rights-to-section-fourteen}
- Accused skips after arraignment and notice: allow trial to continue on unjustifiable absence. {#absence-needs-arraignment-and-notice}
- Trial proceeds in absence: keep presumption of innocence and require trial proof. {#absence-does-not-prove-guilt}
:::
