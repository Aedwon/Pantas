## Core Concept

### Section $21$: When Double Jeopardy Attaches

Article III, Section $21$ bars **double jeopardy**, a second prosecution for the same offense after a protected ending. If one act breaks both a statute and an ordinance, conviction or acquittal under either bars another trial.

A police complaint alone never starts this protection. Four conditions must hold together before jeopardy attaches:

1. A **valid charge** was filed.
2. The court had **jurisdiction** (legal power to hear it).
3. The accused was arraigned and entered a **valid plea**.
4. The case ended by **valid conviction, valid acquittal, or dismissal without express consent**.

Test a theft complaint filed in a barangay hall lacking criminal power, then dropped before court filing:

1. Check court power: the hall lacked criminal jurisdiction.
2. Check plea: no arraignment or valid plea occurred in court.

Result: Jeopardy never attached, so filing a valid charge in court is allowed.

:::why
Why wait until arraignment and plea in a proper court? Before a valid charge, proper court, and plea, the accused has never faced legal risk of conviction.
:::

:::pair #acquittal-bars-retrial-like-conviction
Right: Treat both a valid acquittal and a valid conviction after jeopardy attaches as barring retrial.
Wrong: Claiming only a conviction bars retrial while an acquittal allows another prosecution.
Once jeopardy attaches, either a valid conviction or a valid acquittal ends the case permanently.
:::

:::check
In a proper court, an accused pleads not guilty to a valid charge and is convicted after trial. May prosecutors refile the same charge for a harsher penalty?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. A valid conviction after jeopardy attaches bars a second prosecution for that offense.
:::

### Dismissal Rules and Their Exceptions

The word "dismissed" alone never settles double jeopardy. Always check **charge, court, plea, and when and why the case ended**.

A dismissal **before a valid plea** rarely bars refiling. A dismissal **requested or accepted by the accused** after plea also usually allows refiling, **except** in two cases:

1. Dismissal amounting to **acquittal for insufficient evidence** after prosecution evidence closes.
2. Dismissal for **denial of a speedy trial**.

Check two post-plea dismissals in a proper court:

1. The accused asks for temporary dismissal for his lawyer's travel conflict: consent rule allows retrial.
2. The court grants the accused's dismissal motion after $6$ years of unjustified delay: speedy-trial exception bars retrial.

Result: Consent dismissals allow refiling unless based on insufficient evidence or speedy-trial denial.

:::pair #pre-plea-dismissal-no-bar
Right: Allow refiling after an ordinary dismissal entered before arraignment and valid plea.
Wrong: Claiming every dismissal before a valid plea bars another prosecution.
Jeopardy begins only after a valid plea in a proper court, so pre-plea dismissals rarely block refiling.
:::

:::pair #insufficient-evidence-dismissal-bars-retrial
Right: Bar retrial when a requested dismissal rests on insufficient evidence or speedy-trial denial.
Wrong: Treating every dismissal requested by the accused as permitting retrial without checking exceptions.
Those two exceptions override the general consent rule and protect against retrial.
:::

:::check
Before any plea, a judge dismisses an assault charge because the prosecutor omitted a witness list. Does Section $21$ block refiling?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. A dismissal before a valid plea means jeopardy never attached.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Police Report Versus Court Conviction

A complaint is dropped at a police desk before any court filing. Later, prosecutors file a valid charge in a proper court, the accused pleads not guilty, and trial ends in conviction. Which stage barred another filing?

**Solution:**

1. Check the police drop: no court charge, jurisdiction, or plea occurred.
2. Check the court conviction: all four conditions met, creating a double-jeopardy bar.

Answer: Only the valid court conviction after plea bars a second prosecution.

**Check:** Verify all four steps: valid charge, proper court, valid plea, and protected ending.

### #2: Dismissal Without Consent Versus Speedy Trial

After a valid plea in a proper court, Case A is dismissed on the prosecutor's motion without consent. Case B is dismissed on the accused's motion after $7$ years of unjustified delay. Does double jeopardy bar refiling?

**Solution:**

1. Check Case A: dismissal after valid plea without express consent bars retrial.
2. Check Case B: speedy-trial denial is an exception to consent dismissals, barring retrial.

Answer: Double jeopardy bars refiling in both Case A and Case B.

**Check:** Check consent first, then check both exceptions: insufficient evidence and speedy-trial denial.

### #3: Comparing Two Post-Plea Motions

After a valid charge, proper court, and valid plea, two defendants move to dismiss. Defendant X asks for dismissal for a personal schedule clash before trial starts. Defendant Y moves to dismiss because prosecution witnesses failed to appear for $5$ years, violating speedy trial. Rule on refiling in both cases.

**Solution:**

1. Check pre-ending steps: confirm charge, proper court, and valid plea hold in both cases.
2. Check dismissal grounds: compare X's scheduling request against Y's speedy-trial ground.

:::try
Finish the last step: rule on refiling charges against Defendant X and Defendant Y.
:::answer
The answer is **Refiling is allowed against X, but barred against Y**. X consented without an exception, while Y's speedy-trial dismissal triggers the exception barring retrial.

**Check:** Separate ordinary consent dismissals from the two exceptions: insufficient evidence and speedy-trial denial.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- First trial ends in acquittal: bar retrial just like a conviction. {#acquittal-bars-retrial-like-conviction}
- Charge dismissed before plea: do not apply double jeopardy without a valid plea first. {#pre-plea-dismissal-no-bar}
- Accused moved to dismiss: check insufficient evidence and speedy trial before allowing retrial. {#insufficient-evidence-dismissal-bars-retrial}
:::
