## Core Concept

### Rights Belong to Everyone

**Human rights** are basic rights every person has because each person has worth. They do not depend on race, sex, religion, language, nationality, or where a person lives.

**Universality** means these rights belong to everyone. **Nondiscrimination** means people must not lose rights because of unfair treatment based on their identity.

Human rights law mainly places duties on governments and their offices. These duties continue in peace and during armed conflict.

Having rights also brings duties to other people. The **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR)** recognizes duties to the community. A person cannot use a right as permission to destroy someone else's rights.

### The Bill of Rights

Article III of the $1987$ Constitution is the **Bill of Rights**. Its $22$ sections limit government power. **Civil and political rights** protect personal freedom, fair treatment, and participation in public life.

**Due process**, in Section $1$, requires fair legal rules before government takes life, liberty, or property. **Equal protection**, in the same section, bars unequal treatment without a valid reason. These rules do not require identical treatment when a real, lawful difference justifies it.

Section $2$ protects against unreasonable searches and seizures. A **search warrant** is a judge's order allowing a search. It needs **probable cause**, or facts giving a sound reason to believe that evidence of a crime is in the place to be searched.

The judge must personally find probable cause based on sworn statements. The warrant must name the specific place and things covered. A general order to search any home is not enough.

Some searches are lawful without a warrant under recognized exceptions. A missing warrant alone does not settle every case. A search with neither a valid warrant nor a lawful exception violates this protection.

Section $3$ protects private messages. Exceptions require a lawful court order or a public safety or order basis allowed by law. Evidence gained by violating Section $2$ or $3$ cannot be used in a proceeding.

Section $4$ protects speech, expression, the press, peaceful assembly, and petitions asking government to fix wrongs. Peaceful criticism of officials is protected. Freedom of expression does not give permission to threaten violence against others.

Section $5$ protects religion. Government may not impose an official religion or block free religious practice. It may not require a religious test for civil or political rights.

### Other Everyday Protections

Section $6$ protects the choice of home and travel. A lawful court order can limit where a person lives. Travel limits must have a legal basis tied to national security, public safety, or public health.

Section $7$ protects access to information on public concerns, subject to lawful limits. Section $8$ protects groups formed for lawful purposes. Section $9$ requires fair payment when government takes private property for public use.

Section $10$ bars laws that impair contract obligations. Section $11$ says poverty must not block access to courts or adequate legal help.

### Investigation and Trial

Section $12$ protects a person under custodial investigation, such as a suspect held by police for questioning. Police must explain the right to remain silent and to have a competent, independent lawyer. A lawyer must be provided if the person cannot afford one.

Giving up these rights requires writing and the presence of a lawyer. Torture, threats, and forced confessions are forbidden. A confession gained by breaking these rules cannot be used against the person.

Section $13$ protects bail before conviction, with a stated exception for a charge punishable by reclusion perpetua when evidence of guilt is strong. **Bail** is security for release while a case proceeds. **Reclusion perpetua** is a severe prison penalty, and bail must not be excessive.

Section $14$ presumes an accused person innocent until guilt is proved. It protects notice of the charge, a lawyer, a speedy and fair public trial, and the chance to question witnesses and present evidence. After the charge is formally read, trial may proceed without the accused if proper notice was given and the absence has no valid excuse.

Section $15$ concerns **habeas corpus**, a court process for checking whether detention is lawful. Its privilege may be suspended only during invasion or rebellion when public safety requires it. Suspension is not permission to suspend every right.

Section $16$ requires cases to be resolved without undue delay. Section $17$ bars forcing a person to testify against himself or herself.

### Limits on Detention, Punishment, and Laws

Section $18$ bars detention solely for political beliefs. It also bars forced service, except as punishment after a lawful criminal conviction.

Section $19$ bars excessive fines and cruel, degrading, or inhuman punishment. It also protects prisoners from abusive treatment and inhuman prison conditions.

Section $20$ bars imprisonment for debt or failure to pay a poll tax, a tax charged per person. It does not give immunity for a separate crime involving money.

Section $21$ protects against **double jeopardy**, or being put at risk of punishment again for the same offense after the legal conditions for that protection are met. A valid acquittal cannot simply be ignored to try the same offense again.

Section $22$ bars **ex post facto laws**, which punish past acts through a later criminal law or make past crimes more severely punishable. It also bars a **bill of attainder**, a law that imposes punishment without a court trial.

### Ordinary Limits and Emergencies

A lawful limit within a right's own rules differs from suspending a treaty duty in an emergency. For example, travel may face a lawful public-health limit without suspending the whole Bill of Rights.

The **International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR)** is a human-rights treaty. Its Article $4$ permits temporary suspension of some duties only during an officially proclaimed emergency threatening the life of the nation. Measures must be strictly needed, follow other international duties, and meet its nondiscrimination and notice rules.

Some duties cannot be suspended through this rule. The right to life and the ban on torture are examples. A real emergency does not permit torture, and the right to life still bars arbitrary killing, or killing without a lawful basis.

### The Commission on Human Rights

The **Commission on Human Rights (CHR)** is an independent national human-rights office. Article XIII of the $1987$ Constitution creates it. It does not work under a department that can direct its findings.

The CHR investigates violations involving civil and political rights. It may act on its own or on a complaint by any party. It can visit jails and detention facilities and provide legal help and measures to protect victims.

A victim, witness, relative, or organization may report a possible violation. Use a CHR office, its Public Assistance and Complaints Desk, or its official online complaint portal. Give the known facts, people involved, place, date, available evidence, and help sought.

Keep available messages, photos, medical records, and witness details. Do not invent missing information. CHR staff assess the report and may seek more information, interview people, or examine evidence.

The process may lead to findings, referrals, legal assistance, counseling, or recommendations for protection. The CHR does not convict people or replace courts. A complaint to the CHR is not itself a criminal judgment.

### Learning from History

The United Nations adopted the UDHR in $1948$ after World War II. The war's abuses helped drive the effort to set a common standard for all people.

The Holocaust involved Nazi persecution and mass murder, including the murder of Jews because they were Jews. This shows how government power can be used to destroy rights when a group is denied equal human worth.

The UDHR's opening text connects human dignity and equal rights with freedom, justice, and peace. Read in this historical setting, it rejects the idea that a government can decide some groups have no rights. The UDHR is an international declaration; Article III is part of the Philippine Constitution.

## Examples

### #1: Equal Access to a Public Service

A public office refuses an otherwise qualified applicant solely because of her religion. Which human-rights principle and constitutional protection are involved?

**Solution:**

1. Human rights belong to everyone regardless of religion. The refusal violates nondiscrimination.
2. Unequal treatment without a valid reason involves equal protection. A religious test also conflicts with freedom of religion.

### #2: Taking Property

An agency takes land for public use. It skips the required legal process and refuses fair payment. Which protections are involved?

**Solution:**

1. Skipping the required fair process involves due process under Section $1$.
2. Taking private property for public use without fair payment involves Section $9$.

### #3: A General Search Order

A judge signs an order allowing police to search any house in a town for anything suspicious. No lawful warrantless-search exception applies. Does the order meet the Bill of Rights requirements?

**Solution:**

1. A search warrant must rest on probable cause and specify the place and things covered.
2. This order gives no specific place or items. It cannot justify the searches described.
3. Evidence obtained through the unconstitutional search is inadmissible under Section $3$.

### #4: Peaceful Criticism

Residents peacefully gather to criticize a mayor and submit a request to change a policy. Officials seek to stop them only because their message is critical. Which protection applies?

**Solution:**

Section $4$ protects speech, peaceful assembly, and petitions to government. Criticism alone does not remove these protections.

### #5: Questioning a Detained Suspect

Police hold a suspect for questioning. They deny his request for a lawyer and threaten him until he signs a confession. May that confession be used against him?

**Solution:**

1. A suspect under custodial investigation has rights to silence and an independent lawyer.
2. Threats and a confession obtained in violation of these rights breach Section $12$.
3. The confession cannot be used against him.

### #6: A Criminal Trial

A court treats an accused person as guilty before hearing evidence and refuses to let her lawyer question prosecution witnesses. Which protection is involved?

**Solution:**

Section $14$ protects the presumption of innocence and the right to challenge witnesses in a fair trial. Both protections are denied here.

### #7: Matching a Protection to Its Section

Match each situation to the Bill of Rights protection: a poor person is denied court access solely for lack of money; a person is jailed only for political beliefs; a legislature punishes a named person without trial.

**Solution:**

1. Denial solely because of poverty involves access to justice under Section $11$.
2. Detention solely for political beliefs violates Section $18$.
3. Legislative punishment without trial is a bill of attainder, barred by Section $22$.

### #8: Reporting to the CHR

A witness has photos and medical records supporting a report of abuse in detention. Can the witness approach the CHR even though the victim has not filed a complaint?

**Solution:**

1. The CHR may act on a complaint by any party or on its own.
2. The witness may report the facts and available evidence through a CHR complaint channel.
3. The CHR may investigate and help protect the victim. Criminal guilt remains for a court to decide.

### #9: An Emergency and Torture

Officials claim that a serious national emergency allows them to suspend the ICCPR ban on torture. Is the claim valid?

**Solution:**

The ban on torture cannot be suspended under ICCPR Article $4$. Even a qualifying national emergency does not permit it.

### #10: Reading a Historical Source

A passage from the UDHR's opening text connects equal human dignity with peace. In light of World War II and the Holocaust, what lesson does this idea support?

**Solution:**

1. Wartime persecution showed the harm of treating groups as if they had no rights.
2. Equal human dignity means rights cannot depend on belonging to a favored group.
3. The passage supports protection for every person, not unlimited government power over selected groups.

### #11: Rights and Community Duties

A speaker claims freedom of expression permits threats against neighbors who disagree with him. Does the UDHR support his claim?

**Solution:**

1. The UDHR recognizes duties to the community and respect for other people's rights.
2. Freedom is not permission to destroy those rights. The claim is incorrect.

### #12: Constitutional Sources

Which articles of the $1987$ Constitution contain the Bill of Rights and create the CHR?

**Solution:**

Article III contains the Bill of Rights and its $22$ sections. Article XIII creates the CHR.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Match the act to the protection before choosing a label. A search points to Section $2$; forced questioning points to Section $12$; an unfair criminal trial points to Section $14$.

For sources, separate Article III, the Bill of Rights, from Article XIII, the CHR. The UDHR is the international declaration adopted in $1948$.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Rights Do Not Depend on Approval

> An unpopular person still has human rights.

Religion, criticism of officials, or an accusation of crime does not erase basic rights. An accused person remains entitled to a fair process.

### #2: A Right's Limits Need a Legal Basis

> Neither universality nor an emergency settles every limit question.

Read the conditions of the particular protection. Some ordinary limits are lawful, but core treaty duties such as the torture ban cannot be suspended through Article $4$.

### #3: A Warrant Is Not Unlimited Permission

> A judge's signature does not make a general search order valid.

Probable cause and specific descriptions are required. For a warrantless search, check whether a recognized exception applies.

### #4: CHR Findings Are Not Convictions

> Investigation and a court judgment are different acts.

The CHR gathers facts and helps protect rights. It does not impose a criminal conviction.

### #5: A Count Does Not Explain a Right

> The Bill of Rights has $22$ sections, not $22$ identical rules.

Each section has its own protection and conditions. Match the facts to the actual rule instead of relying only on the section count.
