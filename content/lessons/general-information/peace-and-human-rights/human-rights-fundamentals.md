## Core Concept

### Rights Belong to Everyone

**Human rights** are basic rights people have because every person has worth. They do not depend on race, sex, religion, language, nationality, or where someone lives.

**Universality** means these rights belong to everyone. **Nondiscrimination** means people must not lose rights through unfair treatment based on who they are.

Human rights law mainly places duties on governments and their offices. These duties continue in peace and during armed conflict. Some rights allow lawful limits, but an emergency does not erase all rights.

People also have duties to their community. The **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR)** recognizes these duties. No one may use a right to destroy someone else's rights.

### The Bill of Rights

Article III of the $1987$ Constitution is the **Bill of Rights**. It has $22$ sections that limit government power. **Civil and political rights** protect personal freedom, fair treatment, and taking part in public life.

**Due process**, in Section $1$, requires fair laws and fair procedures before government takes life, liberty, or property. **Equal protection**, in the same section, bars unequal treatment without a valid reason. A lawful difference between groups may justify different treatment.

Section $2$ protects against unreasonable searches and seizures. A **search warrant** is a judge's order allowing a search. It needs **probable cause**, facts giving a sound reason to believe evidence of a crime is in the place to be searched.

The judge must personally find probable cause after questioning the complainant and witnesses under oath. The warrant must name the specific place and things covered. Permission to search any home for anything suspicious is not enough.

Some searches are lawful without a warrant under recognized exceptions. A missing warrant alone does not settle every case. A search without a valid warrant or a lawful exception violates this protection.

Section $3$ protects private messages. An exception needs a lawful court order or a public safety or order basis allowed by law. Evidence obtained by violating Section $2$ or $3$ cannot be used in any proceeding.

Section $4$ protects speech, expression, the press, peaceful assembly, and petitions asking government to fix wrongs. Peaceful criticism of officials is protected. This freedom does not permit threats of violence against others.

Section $5$ protects religion. Government may not establish an official religion or block free religious practice. It may not require a religious test for civil or political rights.

### Other Everyday Protections

Section $6$ protects the choice of home and travel. The choice of home may be limited by a lawful court order within limits set by law. Travel limits must be provided by law and serve national security, public safety, or public health.

Section $7$ protects information on matters of public concern. Citizens may access official records and related public documents, subject to limits set by law. It does not open every private or confidential record.

Section $8$ protects the right to form unions, associations, or groups for lawful purposes. Section $9$ requires **just compensation**, fair payment, when government takes private property for public use.

Section $10$ bars laws that weaken duties under contracts. A **contract** is an agreement that creates legal duties. But a valid law to protect public health, safety, morals, or general welfare may still affect those duties. Section $11$ says poverty must not block access to courts or adequate legal help.

### Investigation and Trial

Section $12$ protects a person under **custodial investigation**, such as a suspect held by police for questioning. Police must explain the rights to silence and a competent, independent lawyer. They must provide a lawyer if the person cannot afford one.

Giving up these rights requires writing and the presence of a lawyer. Torture, threats, and forced confessions are forbidden. A confession obtained by breaking these rules cannot be used against the person.

**Bail** is security given for release while a case proceeds. Section $13$ protects bail before conviction. Its stated exception concerns an offense punishable by **reclusion perpetua**, a severe prison penalty, when evidence of guilt is strong.

Bail must not be excessive. A serious accusation alone does not settle whether the exception applies. Both the stated penalty and the strength of the evidence matter.

Section $14$ treats an accused person as innocent until guilt is proved. It protects notice of the charge, a lawyer, and a speedy, fair, public trial. The accused may question witnesses and present evidence.

**Arraignment** is the formal reading of the charge so the accused can answer it. After arraignment, trial may continue without the accused if proper notice was given and the absence has no valid excuse.

Section $15$ concerns **habeas corpus**, a court process for checking whether detention is lawful. Its **privilege** lets a person seek release from unlawful detention through that process. Suspension of this privilege requires invasion or rebellion and a need to protect public safety.

Suspension does not cancel the whole Bill of Rights. Section $16$ protects the speedy handling of cases before courts and other bodies that decide cases. Section $17$ bars forcing a person to give testimony against himself or herself.

The last rule concerns answers that could expose a person to criminal punishment. It is not a general right to refuse every question on every subject.

### Detention, Punishment, and Later Laws

Section $18$ bars detention solely for political beliefs. It also bars **involuntary servitude**, forced labor, except as punishment after a lawful criminal conviction. Article II, Section $4$ separately allows citizens to be required by law to perform personal, military, or civil service to defend the State.

Section $19$ bars excessive fines and cruel, degrading, or inhuman punishment. It also protects prisoners from abusive treatment and inhuman prison conditions.

Section $20$ bars imprisonment for debt or failure to pay a **poll tax**, a tax charged per person. It does not excuse a separate crime involving money. Inability to repay a loan alone is different from proven fraud.

Section $21$ protects against **double jeopardy**, a second prosecution for the same offense after a protected end to the first case. A police complaint alone does not begin this protection.

The first case needs a valid charge before a court with power to hear it. The accused must have been arraigned and entered a valid **plea**, an answer such as guilty or not guilty. These steps place the accused at legal risk of conviction.

A valid acquittal or conviction then bars another prosecution for the same offense. A dismissal without the accused's express consent can also do so. A dismissal before a valid plea generally does not.

A dismissal requested or expressly accepted by the accused generally does not bar another case. Exceptions include a dismissal that amounts to acquittal for insufficient evidence or denial of a speedy trial. Check when and why the case ended.

Section $22$ bars **ex post facto laws**. These later criminal laws punish an earlier act that was lawful when done, or increase punishment for an earlier crime. It also bars a **bill of attainder**, a law imposing punishment without a court trial.

### Ordinary Limits and Emergencies

A right may allow limits under its own rules. A travel limit allowed by law for public health does not suspend the whole Bill of Rights.

The **International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR)** is a human-rights treaty. Article $4$ allows a state to set aside some treaty duties during a grave national emergency. The emergency must threaten the life of the nation and be officially announced.

Each measure must go no further and last no longer than the emergency requires. It must follow other duties under international law. It may not discriminate solely because of race, color, sex, language, religion, or social origin.

The state must immediately tell other ICCPR states which duties are affected and why, through the United Nations Secretary-General. It must also report when the suspension ends.

Some protections cannot be suspended under Article $4$. They include the right to life and the ban on torture. An emergency never permits **arbitrary killing**. This includes unlawful or unjust killing and denial of fair legal steps. It also includes unneeded force or force too great for the threat. A killing may still be arbitrary even if a national law allows it.

### The Commission on Human Rights

The **Commission on Human Rights (CHR)** is an independent national human-rights office. Article XIII of the $1987$ Constitution creates it. Independence lets it investigate government conduct without serving as an office under that department's control.

The CHR investigates violations involving civil and political rights. It may act on its own or on a complaint by any party. It can visit jails, prisons, and detention facilities.

Its constitutional powers include legal measures to protect rights, preventive measures, and legal aid for people in need. These powers support victims and help prevent further harm. They do not make the CHR a criminal court.

A victim, witness, relative, or organization may report a possible violation to the CHR. Approach a CHR office or use its official public-assistance channel. Give the known facts and ask what further information staff need.

Useful details include what happened, where, when, who was involved, and the help sought. Preserve available photos, messages, medical records, and witness details. Do not invent missing facts or wait to collect every possible record before seeking help.

The CHR may investigate and provide appropriate protection or legal aid within its powers. A matter needing prosecution may be referred to the Department of Justice or another proper office. A **referral** sends a matter to an office that can act on it.

The CHR does not convict people or impose criminal sentences. Courts decide criminal guilt. Reporting a violation does not guarantee a particular outcome.

### Learning from History

The United Nations adopted the UDHR in $1948$ after World War II. The war's abuses helped drive the effort to set a common standard for all people.

The **Holocaust** was Nazi persecution and mass murder, including the murder of Jews because they were Jews. It shows the danger of using government power to deny a group's equal human worth.

The UDHR's opening text links human dignity and equal rights with freedom, justice, and peace. This context helps explain its rejection of rights reserved for favored groups. The UDHR is an international declaration; Article III is part of the Philippine Constitution.

## Examples

### #1: Equal Access to a Public Service

A public office refuses an otherwise qualified applicant solely because of her religion. Which human-rights principle and constitutional protections are involved?

**Solution:**

1. Rights belong to everyone regardless of religion. The refusal violates nondiscrimination.
2. Unequal treatment without a valid reason involves equal protection. A religious test also conflicts with Section $5$.

### #2: Taking Property

An agency takes land for public use. It skips the required legal process and refuses fair payment. Which protections are involved?

**Solution:**

1. Skipping fair legal procedures involves due process under Section $1$.
2. Taking private property for public use without just compensation involves Section $9$.

### #3: A General Search Order

A judge allows police to search any house in a town for anything suspicious. No lawful warrantless-search exception applies. Does the order meet the Bill of Rights requirements?

**Solution:**

1. A warrant needs probable cause and specific descriptions of the place and things covered.
2. This order gives neither a specific place nor specific items. It cannot justify these searches.
3. Evidence from the unconstitutional search cannot be used in any proceeding under Section $3$.

### #4: Private Messages

Government officers secretly record private calls without a lawful court order. No public safety or order exception allowed by law applies. May the recording be used as evidence?

**Solution:**

1. Section $3$ protects private communications. Neither stated legal basis for an exception applies.
2. The recording violates this protection. It cannot be used as evidence in a proceeding.

### #5: Peaceful Criticism

Residents peacefully gather to criticize a mayor and request a policy change. Officials seek to stop them solely because their message is critical. Which protection applies?

**Solution:**

Section $4$ protects speech, peaceful assembly, and petitions. Criticism alone does not remove these protections.

### #6: A Travel Limit

A travel restriction is provided by law to protect public health during a disease outbreak. Does Section $6$ prohibit every such restriction?

**Solution:**

1. Section $6$ permits travel limits provided by law for national security, public safety, or public health.
2. The stated reason fits one allowed ground. The restriction must still meet the law's conditions and other applicable rights.

### #7: Home and Public Records

An official orders a resident to move homes without any lawful court order. The same official denies a citizen public records solely to hide official decisions. No lawful record limit applies. Which sections are involved?

**Solution:**

1. Section $6$ protects the choice of home. The official has no lawful court order supporting the move.
2. Section $7$ protects access to official records on public concerns. Personal embarrassment does not supply the missing lawful limit.

### #8: A Lawful Association and a Contract

Officials ban a peaceful workers' association whose purpose is lawful. A new law also erases payment duties in an existing contract. Which protections should be examined?

**Solution:**

1. The ban involves Section $8$, the right to form groups for lawful purposes.
2. The law erasing contract duties involves Section $10$, the protection against laws weakening contract obligations.

### #9: Questioning a Detained Suspect

Police hold a suspect for questioning. They deny his request for a lawyer and threaten him until he signs a confession. May that confession be used against him?

**Solution:**

1. Section $12$ protects a suspect's rights to silence and an independent lawyer during custodial investigation.
2. Denying a lawyer and using threats violate those protections.
3. The confession cannot be used against him.

### #10: Bail Before Conviction

An accused person seeks bail before conviction. The charge is punishable by reclusion perpetua, but evidence of guilt is not strong. Does the stated Section $13$ exception justify denying bail?

**Solution:**

1. The exception needs both the stated penalty and strong evidence of guilt.
2. Strong evidence is absent. That exception does not justify denying bail.

### #11: A Criminal Trial

A court treats an accused person as guilty before hearing evidence. It refuses to let her lawyer question prosecution witnesses. Which protection is involved?

**Solution:**

Section $14$ protects the presumption of innocence and the right to question witnesses. Both protections are denied here.

### #12: An Unjustified Absence

An accused person has been arraigned. He receives proper notice of trial but stays away without a valid excuse. Must his absence alone stop the trial?

**Solution:**

1. Section $14$ allows trial to continue after arraignment when notice is proper and the absence is unjustified.
2. Those conditions are present. His absence alone need not stop the trial.

### #13: Suspending Habeas Corpus

Officials claim an ordinary protest permits suspension of the privilege of habeas corpus. No invasion or rebellion exists. Is the stated basis sufficient?

**Solution:**

1. Section $15$ requires invasion or rebellion and a need to protect public safety.
2. The facts lack invasion or rebellion. The stated basis is insufficient.

### #14: Compelled Testimony and Delay

A witness is forced to answer a question that would expose her to criminal punishment. In a separate case, an agency leaves a matter unresolved through undue delay. Which protections apply?

**Solution:**

1. The forced self-incriminating answer involves Section $17$.
2. The undue delay involves Section $16$, which protects speedy handling of cases beyond criminal trials alone.

### #15: Debt and a Poll Tax

A borrower cannot repay an ordinary loan. Another person cannot pay a poll tax. Neither committed a separate crime. May either be jailed solely for that failure?

**Solution:**

Section $20$ bars imprisonment for debt or nonpayment of a poll tax. Neither may be jailed solely for the stated failure.

### #16: A Second Prosecution

A valid charge was read in a court with power to hear it. The accused pleaded not guilty and was validly acquitted after trial. May government retry the same offense simply because it dislikes the result?

**Solution:**

1. The valid charge, proper court, and valid plea placed the accused at legal risk of conviction.
2. The valid acquittal ended that case. Double jeopardy bars another prosecution for the same offense on these facts.

### #17: When a Dismissal Occurs

One charge is dismissed before a valid plea. Another case ends when the court grants the accused's request for acquittal because the prosecution's completed evidence is insufficient. Do both dismissals have the same effect?

**Solution:**

1. The first dismissal occurs before a valid plea. It alone does not create a double-jeopardy bar.
2. The second amounts to an acquittal after the prosecution presented its evidence. The accused's request does not remove double-jeopardy protection in that case.

### #18: A Later Criminal Law

An act was lawful when done. A later criminal law orders punishment for people who did it before the law took effect. Which protection applies?

**Solution:**

Section $22$ bars ex post facto punishment. A later criminal law cannot punish that earlier lawful act.

### #19: Matching Other Protections

Match each situation to a protection: court access denied solely for poverty; detention solely for political beliefs; forced labor for an official's private business without a criminal conviction; cruel prison punishment; legislative punishment without trial.

**Solution:**

1. Denial solely for poverty involves Section $11$.
2. Detention solely for political beliefs and the forced labor involve Section $18$.
3. Cruel prison punishment involves Section $19$.
4. Legislative punishment without trial is a bill of attainder under Section $22$.

### #20: Reporting to the CHR

A witness has photos and medical records supporting a report of abuse in detention. Can the witness approach the CHR even though the victim has not complained?

**Solution:**

1. The CHR may act on a complaint by any party or on its own.
2. The witness may report the known facts and available evidence to a CHR office.
3. The CHR may investigate and use its protective powers. A court decides criminal guilt.

### #21: An Emergency and Torture

Officials claim a serious national emergency permits suspension of the ICCPR torture ban. Is the claim valid?

**Solution:**

The ban cannot be suspended under ICCPR Article $4$. Even a qualifying national emergency does not permit torture.

### #22: Reading a Historical Source

The UDHR's opening text connects equal human dignity with peace. In the context of World War II and the Holocaust, what lesson does this support?

**Solution:**

1. Wartime persecution showed the harm of denying a group's equal human worth.
2. Universal rights do not depend on belonging to a favored group.
3. The text supports protecting every person from such abuse.

### #23: Rights and Community Duties

A speaker claims freedom of expression permits threats against neighbors who disagree with him. Does the UDHR support his claim?

**Solution:**

1. The UDHR recognizes duties to the community and respect for other people's rights.
2. Freedom does not permit destroying those rights. The claim is incorrect.

### #24: Constitutional Sources

Which articles of the $1987$ Constitution contain the Bill of Rights and create the CHR?

**Solution:**

Article III contains the Bill of Rights and its $22$ sections. Article XIII creates the CHR.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Match the act to the protection first. A search points to Section $2$; questioning a held suspect points to Section $12$; a criminal trial points to Section $14$.

Keep Article III, the Bill of Rights, separate from Article XIII, the CHR. For double jeopardy, check the charge, court, plea, and how the case ended. The word “dismissed” alone is not enough.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Rights Do Not Depend on Approval

> An unpopular person still has human rights.

Religion, criticism, or an accusation does not erase rights. The mistaken answer treats rights as a reward for approved behavior.

### #2: Limits Need a Legal Basis

> An emergency does not suspend every right.

Check each protection's conditions. A claim of public safety alone does not erase them, and Article $4$ never permits suspension of the torture ban.

### #3: A Warrant Is Not Unlimited Permission

> A judge's signature does not make a general search order valid.

Probable cause and specific descriptions are required. For a warrantless search, check for a recognized exception before deciding.

### #4: CHR Findings Are Not Convictions

> An investigation is not a criminal judgment.

The CHR investigates and helps protect rights. The mistaken answer gives it a court's power to convict and sentence.

### #5: Debt Is Different from a Separate Crime

> Failure to repay a loan alone does not justify imprisonment.

Section $20$ protects against imprisonment for debt. It does not prevent punishment for a separately proved crime involving money.

### #6: A Dismissal Is Not Always a Bar

> Check when and why the first case ended.

A dismissal before a valid plea generally does not support double jeopardy. A valid acquittal after a valid plea does. Dismissals requested by the accused have separate rules and exceptions.
