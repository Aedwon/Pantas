## Core Concept

### Rights Belong to Everyone

**Human rights** are basic rights people have because every person has worth. They do not depend on race, sex, religion, language, nationality, or where someone lives.

**Universality** means these rights belong to everyone. **Nondiscrimination** means people must not lose rights because of unfair treatment based on who they are.

Human rights law mainly places duties on governments and their offices. These duties continue in peace and during armed conflict. This does not mean every right has the same limits in every setting.

Having rights also brings duties to other people. The **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR)** recognizes duties to the community. No one may use a right as permission to destroy someone else's rights.

### The Bill of Rights

Article III of the $1987$ Constitution is the **Bill of Rights**. Its $22$ sections limit government power. **Civil and political rights** protect personal freedom, fair treatment, and taking part in public life.

**Due process**, in Section $1$, requires fair legal rules and fair procedures before government takes life, liberty, or property. **Equal protection**, in the same section, bars unequal treatment without a valid reason. A real, lawful difference may justify different treatment.

Section $2$ protects against unreasonable searches and seizures. A **search warrant** is a judge's order allowing a search. It needs **probable cause**, or facts giving a sound reason to believe evidence of a crime is in the place to be searched.

The judge must personally find probable cause based on statements given under oath. The warrant must describe the specific place and things covered. An order to search any home for anything suspicious is not enough.

Some searches are lawful without a warrant under recognized exceptions. A missing warrant alone does not settle every case. A search with neither a valid warrant nor a lawful exception violates this protection.

Section $3$ protects private messages. An exception needs a lawful court order or a public safety or order basis allowed by law. Evidence gained by violating Section $2$ or $3$ cannot be used in any proceeding.

Section $4$ protects speech, expression, the press, peaceful assembly, and petitions asking government to fix wrongs. Peaceful criticism of officials is protected. This freedom does not give permission to threaten violence against others.

Section $5$ protects religion. Government may not establish an official religion or block free religious practice. It may not require a religious test for civil or political rights.

### Other Everyday Protections

Section $6$ protects the choice of home and travel. The choice of home may be limited by a lawful court order. Travel limits must be allowed by law and serve national security, public safety, or public health.

Section $7$ protects access to information on public concerns, subject to lawful limits. Section $8$ protects groups formed for lawful purposes. Section $9$ requires fair payment when government takes private property for public use.

Section $10$ bars laws that weaken duties under contracts. Section $11$ says poverty must not block access to courts or adequate legal help.

### Investigation and Trial

Section $12$ protects a person under **custodial investigation**, such as a suspect held by police for questioning. Police must explain the right to remain silent and to have a competent, independent lawyer. A lawyer must be provided if the person cannot afford one.

Giving up these rights requires writing and the presence of a lawyer. Torture, threats, and forced confessions are forbidden. A confession gained by breaking these rules cannot be used against the person.

**Bail** is security given for release while a case proceeds. Section $13$ protects bail before conviction. Its stated exception concerns a charge punishable by **reclusion perpetua**, a severe prison penalty, when evidence of guilt is strong.

Bail must not be excessive. A serious accusation alone does not settle whether the exception applies. Both the stated penalty and the strength of the evidence matter.

Section $14$ treats an accused person as innocent until guilt is proved. It protects notice of the charge, a lawyer, a speedy and fair public trial, and the chance to question witnesses and present evidence. After the charge is formally read, trial may proceed without the accused if proper notice was given and the absence has no valid excuse.

Section $15$ concerns **habeas corpus**, a court process for checking whether detention is lawful. The right to obtain release through this process may be suspended only during invasion or rebellion when public safety requires it. This does not suspend every right.

Section $16$ requires cases to be resolved without undue delay. Section $17$ bars forcing a person to testify against himself or herself.

### Limits on Detention, Punishment, and Laws

Section $18$ bars detention solely for political beliefs. It also bars forced service, except as punishment after a lawful criminal conviction.

Section $19$ bars excessive fines and cruel, degrading, or inhuman punishment. It protects prisoners from abusive treatment and inhuman prison conditions.

Section $20$ bars imprisonment for debt or failure to pay a **poll tax**, a tax charged per person. It does not excuse a separate crime involving money.

Section $21$ protects against **double jeopardy**, or facing prosecution again for the same offense after a case has legally ended in a way this rule protects. A police complaint alone does not begin this protection.

First, there must be a valid criminal charge before a court with power to hear it. The charge must have been formally read to the accused, who must have entered a valid **plea**, such as guilty or not guilty. These steps put the accused at legal risk of conviction.

A valid acquittal or conviction then bars another prosecution for the same offense. A dismissal without the accused's express consent can also do so. A dismissal before a valid plea generally does not.

A dismissal requested or expressly agreed to by the accused generally does not bar another case. Exceptions include dismissal because the evidence is insufficient or the right to a speedy trial was denied. Check the stage of the case and the reason it ended, not just the word “dismissed.”

Section $22$ bars **ex post facto laws**. These later criminal laws punish an earlier act that was lawful when done, or increase the punishment for an earlier crime. The same section bars a **bill of attainder**, a law imposing punishment without a court trial.

### Ordinary Limits and Emergencies

Some rights allow limits under their own rules. Travel, for example, may face a lawful public-health limit. Such a limit does not suspend the whole Bill of Rights.

The **International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR)** is a human-rights treaty. Article $4$ lets a state temporarily set aside some treaty duties in a grave national emergency. The emergency must threaten the life of the nation, and government must officially announce it.

Each measure must be needed to meet that emergency. It must go no further and last no longer than needed. The state must still follow its other duties under international law.

Measures may not discriminate solely because of race, color, sex, language, religion, or social origin. The state must immediately tell other ICCPR states which duties are affected and why, through the United Nations Secretary-General. It must also report when the suspension ends.

Some protections cannot be suspended under Article $4$. These include the right to life and the ban on torture. An emergency never permits torture or **arbitrary killing**, meaning taking life without a lawful basis.

### The Commission on Human Rights

The **Commission on Human Rights (CHR)** is an independent national human-rights office. Article XIII of the $1987$ Constitution creates it. A government department cannot direct its findings.

The CHR investigates violations involving civil and political rights. It may act on its own or on a complaint by any party. It can visit jails and detention facilities and provide legal help and measures to protect victims.

A victim, witness, relative, or organization may report a possible violation to a CHR office or its official complaint channels. Give the known facts, people involved, place, date, available evidence, and help sought.

Keep available messages, photos, medical records, and witness details. Do not invent missing information. CHR staff assess the report and may ask for details, interview people, or examine evidence.

The process may lead to findings, referrals, legal help, counseling, or steps to protect victims. A **referral** sends a matter to another office that can act on it. The CHR does not convict people or replace courts.

### Learning from History

The United Nations adopted the UDHR in $1948$ after World War II. The war's abuses helped drive the effort to set a common standard for all people.

The **Holocaust** was Nazi persecution and mass murder, including the murder of Jews because they were Jews. It shows how government power can destroy rights when a group is denied equal human worth.

The UDHR's opening text connects human dignity and equal rights with freedom, justice, and peace. In this setting, it rejects the idea that government may decide some groups have no rights. The UDHR is an international declaration; Article III is part of the Philippine Constitution.

## Examples

### #1: Equal Access to a Public Service

A public office refuses an otherwise qualified applicant solely because of her religion. Which human-rights principle and constitutional protections are involved?

**Solution:**

1. Rights belong to everyone regardless of religion. The refusal violates nondiscrimination.
2. Unequal treatment without a valid reason involves equal protection. A religious test also conflicts with freedom of religion.

### #2: Taking Property

An agency takes land for public use. It skips the required legal process and refuses fair payment. Which protections are involved?

**Solution:**

1. Skipping the required fair process involves due process under Section $1$.
2. Taking private property for public use without fair payment involves Section $9$.

### #3: A General Search Order

A judge signs an order allowing police to search any house in a town for anything suspicious. No lawful warrantless-search exception applies. Does the order meet the Bill of Rights requirements?

**Solution:**

1. A search warrant must rest on probable cause and describe the specific place and things covered.
2. This order gives no specific place or items. It cannot justify these searches.
3. Evidence from the unconstitutional search cannot be used in court or another proceeding under Section $3$.

### #4: Private Messages

Government officers secretly record private calls without a lawful court order. No public safety or order exception allowed by law applies. May the recording be used as evidence?

**Solution:**

1. Section $3$ protects private communications. Neither stated legal basis for an exception applies here.
2. The recording was obtained by violating that protection. It cannot be used as evidence in a proceeding.

### #5: Peaceful Criticism

Residents peacefully gather to criticize a mayor and ask for a policy change. Officials seek to stop them only because their message is critical. Which protection applies?

**Solution:**

Section $4$ protects speech, peaceful assembly, and petitions to government. Criticism alone does not remove these protections.

### #6: Questioning a Detained Suspect

Police hold a suspect for questioning. They deny his request for a lawyer and threaten him until he signs a confession. May that confession be used against him?

**Solution:**

1. A suspect under custodial investigation has rights to silence and an independent lawyer.
2. Denying the lawyer and using threats breach Section $12$.
3. The confession cannot be used against him.

### #7: Bail Before Conviction

An accused person seeks bail before conviction. The charge is punishable by reclusion perpetua, but the court finds that evidence of guilt is not strong. Does the stated Section $13$ exception justify denying bail?

**Solution:**

1. The exception needs both the stated penalty and strong evidence of guilt.
2. Strong evidence is absent here. That exception does not justify denying bail.

### #8: A Criminal Trial

A court treats an accused person as guilty before hearing evidence. It also refuses to let her lawyer question prosecution witnesses. Which protection is involved?

**Solution:**

Section $14$ protects the presumption of innocence and the right to question witnesses in a fair trial. Both protections are denied here.

### #9: A Second Prosecution

A valid charge was read to an accused person in a court with power to hear it. He pleaded not guilty and was validly acquitted after trial. May the government retry the same offense simply because it dislikes the result?

**Solution:**

1. The valid charge, proper court, and valid plea placed him at legal risk of conviction.
2. The valid acquittal ended the case. Double jeopardy bars another prosecution for the same offense on these facts.

### #10: A Case That Ended Too Early

A criminal charge is dismissed before the accused enters any plea. Does that dismissal alone bar a later prosecution under double jeopardy?

**Solution:**

1. A valid plea is needed for the first legal risk of conviction to begin.
2. No plea was entered. The dismissal alone does not create the double-jeopardy bar.

### #11: A Later Criminal Law

An act was lawful when a person did it. A later law makes the act a crime and orders punishment for people who did it before the law took effect. Which protection applies?

**Solution:**

Section $22$ bars ex post facto punishment. A later criminal law cannot punish that earlier lawful act.

### #12: Matching Other Protections

Match each situation to its protection: a poor person is denied court access solely for lack of money; a person is jailed only for political beliefs; a legislature punishes a named person without trial.

**Solution:**

1. Denial solely because of poverty involves access to justice under Section $11$.
2. Detention solely for political beliefs violates Section $18$.
3. Legislative punishment without trial is a bill of attainder, barred by Section $22$.

### #13: Reporting to the CHR

A witness has photos and medical records supporting a report of abuse in detention. Can the witness approach the CHR even though the victim has not filed a complaint?

**Solution:**

1. The CHR may act on a complaint by any party or on its own.
2. The witness may report the facts and evidence through a CHR complaint channel.
3. The CHR may investigate and help protect the victim. A court decides criminal guilt.

### #14: An Emergency and Torture

Officials claim a serious national emergency allows them to suspend the ICCPR ban on torture. Is the claim valid?

**Solution:**

The ban on torture cannot be suspended under ICCPR Article $4$. Even a qualifying national emergency does not permit torture.

### #15: Reading a Historical Source

The UDHR's opening text connects equal human dignity with peace. In light of World War II and the Holocaust, what lesson does this idea support?

**Solution:**

1. Wartime persecution showed the harm of treating groups as if they had no rights.
2. Equal human dignity means rights cannot depend on belonging to a favored group.
3. The text supports protecting every person from such abuse.

### #16: Rights and Community Duties

A speaker claims freedom of expression permits threats against neighbors who disagree with him. Does the UDHR support his claim?

**Solution:**

1. The UDHR recognizes community duties and respect for other people's rights.
2. Freedom is not permission to destroy those rights. The claim is incorrect.

### #17: Constitutional Sources

Which articles of the $1987$ Constitution contain the Bill of Rights and create the CHR?

**Solution:**

Article III contains the Bill of Rights and its $22$ sections. Article XIII creates the CHR.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Match the act to the protection first. A search points to Section $2$; police questioning of a held suspect points to Section $12$; an unfair criminal trial points to Section $14$.

Separate Article III, the Bill of Rights, from Article XIII, the CHR. For double jeopardy, check the charge, court, plea, and how the case ended. The word “dismissed” alone is not enough.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Rights Do Not Depend on Approval

> An unpopular person still has human rights.

Religion, criticism of officials, or an accusation of crime does not erase basic rights. An accused person still has a right to fair process.

### #2: Limits Need a Legal Basis

> An emergency does not suspend every right.

Read the conditions of each protection. Ordinary limits may be lawful, but Article $4$ never permits suspension of the torture ban.

### #3: A Warrant Is Not Unlimited Permission

> A judge's signature does not make a general search order valid.

Probable cause and specific descriptions are required. For a warrantless search, check whether a recognized exception applies.

### #4: CHR Findings Are Not Convictions

> An investigation is not a criminal judgment.

The CHR gathers facts and helps protect rights. It does not convict people.

### #5: A Count Does Not Explain a Right

> The Bill of Rights has $22$ sections with different protections.

Knowing the count does not settle a case. Match the facts to the rule and its conditions.

### #6: A Dismissal Is Not Always a Bar

> Check when and why the first case ended.

A dismissal before a valid plea generally does not support double jeopardy. A valid acquittal after a valid plea does. A dismissal requested by the accused has separate rules and exceptions.
