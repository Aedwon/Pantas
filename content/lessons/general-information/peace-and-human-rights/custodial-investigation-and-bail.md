## Core Concept

### Section $12$: Custodial Investigation Safeguards

Article III, Section $12$ protects any person under **custodial investigation**, such as a suspect held by police for questioning. Police must inform the suspect of two rights:

1. The right to remain silent.
2. The right to a competent and independent lawyer, preferably of the suspect's own choice.

Police must provide a lawyer if the suspect cannot afford one. Waiving these rights requires **writing** and **the presence of counsel**.

Torture, threats, secret detention, and forced confessions are banned. Any confession taken by violating Section $12$ is inadmissible against the person.

Test an interview where police read the rights aloud, then obtain a signed waiver with no lawyer present:

1. Check writing: the suspect signed a written waiver.
2. Check counsel: no lawyer was present during the waiver.

Result: The waiver fails Section $12$, and any confession following it is inadmissible.

:::why
Why must a lawyer attend any Section $12$ waiver? Held suspects face heavy police pressure alone, so counsel ensures any waiver is free and informed.
:::

:::pair #waiver-needs-writing-and-counsel
Right: Require both a written waiver and counsel's presence during custodial investigation.
Wrong: Treating a suspect's written signature alone as a valid waiver without counsel.
Writing and counsel's presence must happen together for a valid Section $12$ waiver.
:::

:::check
Officers question an indigent detainee without providing a free lawyer and record an oral admission. May prosecutors use it?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Police failed to provide counsel, so Section $12$ excludes the admission.
:::

### Section $13$: Bail Before Conviction

**Bail** is security given for release while a case proceeds. Under Article III, Section $13$, all persons have a right to bail before conviction, and excessive bail is barred.

One constitutional exception exists. Bail may be denied before conviction when **both** conditions hold:

1. The offense is punishable by **reclusion perpetua**, a severe prison penalty.
2. The **evidence of guilt is strong**.

Match each stage to its section. A search points to Section $2$, custodial questioning to Section $12$, bail to Section $13$, and trial to Section $14$.

Test a bail request for theft carrying a $6$-year max term, even with strong eyewitness proof:

1. Check penalty: theft is not punishable by reclusion perpetua.
2. Check exception: one missing condition blocks the bail exception.

Result: The accused keeps the right to bail before conviction.

:::pair #bail-exception-needs-both-parts
Right: Require both reclusion perpetua and strong evidence of guilt before denying pre-conviction bail.
Wrong: Denying bail on a reclusion perpetua charge alone when evidence of guilt is weak.
Both severe penalty and strong proof must appear together to trigger the exception.
:::

:::pair #bail-has-constitutional-exception
Right: Recognize Section $13$'s exception when both reclusion perpetua and strong evidence exist.
Wrong: Claiming bail must always be granted before conviction with no exception.
Bail is the general rule before conviction, yet Section $13$ sets that narrow exception.
:::

:::check
At a murder bail hearing where the penalty is reclusion perpetua, prosecutors offer only weak hearsay rumor. May the judge deny bail under Section $13$?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Without strong evidence of guilt, the Section $13$ exception fails even for a reclusion perpetua charge.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Threatened Confession in Custody

Police question a held suspect, refuse his request for a lawyer, and threaten him until he signs a confession. May it be used against him?

**Solution:**

1. Check the setting: police questioning of a held suspect falls under Section $12$.
2. Check violations: denying counsel and using threats break Section $12$ safeguards.

Answer: No, the confession is inadmissible against him.

**Check:** Violating counsel, silence, or anti-threat rules bars the confession.

### #2: Strong Proof on a Non-Capital Charge

An accused seeks pre-trial bail on a fraud charge carrying $10$ years max prison, with strong bank proof. Does the Section $13$ exception bar bail?

**Solution:**

1. Check penalty: $10$ years in prison is not reclusion perpetua.
2. Apply Section $13$: strong evidence alone cannot trigger the exception without that penalty.

Answer: No, the accused keeps the right to reasonable bail before conviction.

**Check:** Match bail to Section $13$ and verify both reclusion perpetua and strong proof.

### #3: Oral Advice Waiver and Non-Capital Bail

Police read Section $12$ rights to an arrested clerk, who signs a typed waiver alone without counsel and admits theft. Prosecutors charge an $8$-year offense and ask to deny bail because proof is strong. Rule on both points.

**Solution:**

1. Check the custodial waiver: test verbal advisement plus signature against Section $12$ without counsel present.
2. Check bail: test the $8$-year penalty and strong proof against both Section $13$ conditions.

:::try
Finish the last step: rule on the signed waiver and the request to deny bail.
:::answer
The answer is **The waiver is invalid, and bail cannot be denied under Section $13$**. A waiver needs counsel present alongside writing, and an $8$-year charge lacks reclusion perpetua.

**Check:** Require writing plus counsel for Section $12$ waivers, and reclusion perpetua plus strong proof for Section $13$.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Suspect signs waiver alone: require both writing and counsel's presence under Section $12$. {#waiver-needs-writing-and-counsel}
- Bail hearing cites one factor: verify both reclusion perpetua and strong proof together. {#bail-exception-needs-both-parts}
- Pre-conviction bail rule: remember Section $13$ allows denial when both conditions hold. {#bail-has-constitutional-exception}
:::
