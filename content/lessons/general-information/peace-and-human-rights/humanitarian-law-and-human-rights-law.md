## Core Concept

### Human Rights Law

**Human Rights Law (HRL)** protects people's life, freedom, and dignity. It applies in peace and during armed conflict. It mainly places duties on governments toward people within their **jurisdiction**, meaning their territory or authority or control.

War does not turn off human rights. Some treaty duties allow a temporary suspension during a grave emergency. Other duties cannot be suspended in this way.

### International Humanitarian Law

**International Humanitarian Law (IHL)** limits harm during armed conflict. It protects people who are not fighting or have stopped fighting. It also limits weapons and the ways parties fight.

IHL binds every party to an armed conflict. These include government forces and organized armed groups outside government. Neither a claim to a just cause nor an enemy's abuse excuses breaking IHL.

An **international armed conflict (IAC)** exists when states use armed force against each other. No formal declaration of war is needed. Occupation can also trigger IHL even without armed resistance.

A **non-international armed conflict (NIAC)** involves sufficiently intense fighting between government forces and organized armed groups, or between such groups. Both the level of fighting and the groups' organization matter.

An organized group has features such as leaders who direct fighters and an ability to plan operations. The scale, length, and frequency of clashes help show how intense the fighting is. No single number of clashes settles every case.

A riot, brief disturbance, or isolated violent act does not automatically meet these conditions. The facts decide the classification. An official label alone does not.

### How the Laws Work Together

HRL and IHL are **complementary**, meaning they help protect people together. During armed conflict, IHL gives rules made for fighting. HRL still protects people against unlawful government action.

The right to life forbids **arbitrary killing**, or taking life without a lawful basis. During battlefield fighting, IHL's specific targeting rules help decide whether a killing breaks this protection. IHL does not cancel every human-rights rule.

An attack on an active opposing fighter during an IAC is not automatically unlawful because it takes life. The target's status and all applicable IHL rules matter. A person held in custody is not an active battlefield target just because war continues nearby.

### Emergency Suspension Under the ICCPR

The **International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR)** is a human-rights treaty. Article $4$ sets rules for temporarily setting aside some treaty duties during an emergency. The legal term for this is **derogation**.

The emergency must threaten the life of the nation. The government must officially announce that it exists. An ordinary protest alone does not meet this test.

Each measure must be needed to meet that emergency. It must go no further and last no longer than needed. The state must also follow its other duties under international law.

Measures must not discriminate solely because of race, color, sex, language, religion, or social origin. This means government cannot use those traits alone to choose who loses a protection.

The state must immediately notify other ICCPR states through the United Nations Secretary-General. It must say which treaty duties are affected and why. It must send another notice stating when the suspension ends.

**Non-derogable rights** are rights whose treaty protection cannot be suspended under Article $4$. The ICCPR includes the following protections:

- The right to life and the ban on torture or cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment. People must also freely agree before medical or scientific experiments are done on them.
- The bans on slavery and **servitude**, being forced to live and work under another person's control.
- Protection from prison merely because someone cannot meet a duty under a contract.
- Protection from **retroactive criminal punishment**. A later criminal law cannot punish an earlier act that was lawful when done or increase the penalty for an earlier crime.
- Recognition as a person before the law. Government must recognize every human being as someone who can hold legal rights.
- Freedom of thought, conscience, and religion. **Conscience** means a person's beliefs about right and wrong.

Non-derogable does not mean every part of a right has no limits. Some rights allow limits under their own rules. For example, certain necessary, lawful limits may apply to outward religious practice, but government may not suspend freedom of belief under Article $4$.

The right to life still forbids arbitrary killing in an emergency. The torture ban has no emergency exception. Article $4$ does not allow a state to suspend IHL either.

### People Protected from Attack

**Civilians** are people who are not members of the armed forces or other fighting forces covered by the relevant IHL rules. They must not be directly attacked. A civilian loses this protection for the time that the person directly takes part in fighting.

A person **hors de combat** is out of the fight. The person may be held by the opponent, clearly surrendering, or unable to defend himself or herself because of wounds or illness.

This protection from attack requires no hostile acts and no attempt to escape. Someone who starts fighting again cannot rely on an earlier surrender. Any force used must still follow the rules for that situation.

People in custody must be treated humanely, with respect for their basic needs and worth. They must not be tortured or killed as punishment without trial. Wounded and sick people must receive needed care without unfair discrimination.

### Weapons and Attack Methods

The **means of warfare** are weapons. The **methods of warfare** are the ways parties carry out their operations. Both have legal limits.

IHL bans weapons that are **indiscriminate by nature**. They cannot be aimed at a specific military target, or their effects cannot be kept within IHL's limits. Such weapons cannot reliably separate military targets from protected civilians.

Weapons must not cause **superfluous injury or unnecessary suffering**. In simple terms, a weapon must not cause extra injury or suffering beyond what a lawful military purpose can justify. This protection covers fighters too.

Specific treaties also ban particular weapons. The Geneva Gas Protocol prohibits the use of poison gas in war. A claimed military benefit does not cancel that ban.

**Distinction** means separating military targets from protected civilians and civilian things. Attacks must be aimed at military targets. A civilian home does not become a military target merely because destroying it would frighten the enemy.

For an object to be a **military objective**, it must effectively help military action. Destroying, capturing, or putting it out of use must offer a definite military gain in the circumstances. Both parts must be met.

**Proportionality** sets a separate limit on attacks against military targets. Planners must compare the civilian deaths, injuries, and damage they expect with the specific military gain they expect. They must not attack if that civilian harm would be too great compared with the gain.

This rule concerns **incidental harm**, meaning civilian harm that is not the attack's aim. A small military gain cannot justify very great civilian harm. Directly attacking protected civilians is forbidden regardless of a claimed gain.

**Precautions** are practical steps to avoid or reduce civilian harm. Planners must check the target and consider safer weapons or timing. They must give effective warning when circumstances allow it.

If new information shows the target is not lawful, the attack must be canceled or stopped. The same applies if expected civilian harm becomes too great compared with the expected military gain. Giving a warning does not make an unlawful attack lawful.

### Duties Before War and Work for Peace

Some IHL duties apply in peacetime. Article $127$ of the Third Geneva Convention requires teaching its rules in peace and war, including military instruction. Commanders responsible for prisoners of war need special instruction.

States must also pass laws needed to punish **grave breaches**, serious violations listed in the Geneva Conventions. Torture of a protected prisoner is an example. Training and laws prepare states to follow the treaties before fighting begins.

IHL limits harm once armed conflict exists. Peaceful conflict resolution seeks to settle disputes and prevent more violence. An agreement must still respect basic rights.

Start by identifying the issue and hearing each side. Ask what each side needs, not only what it demands. Find lawful options, agree on clear steps, and check whether people follow them.

## Examples

### #1: Comparing the Two Bodies of Law

Which statement correctly compares HRL and IHL?

- A. HRL applies only in peace, while IHL binds every party to armed conflict.
- B. HRL applies in peace and war, while IHL governs armed conflict and binds its parties.
- C. HRL applies in peace and war, while IHL binds only government forces.
- D. HRL applies only in war, while IHL governs both armed conflict and ordinary riots.

**Solution:**

1. HRL applies in peace and during armed conflict. Its main duties fall on states.
2. IHL governs armed conflict and binds all parties, including organized armed groups.
3. Choice B correctly states the difference.

### #2: Classifying Fighting

State A's forces attack State B's forces. Elsewhere, government troops fight a group with an organized command in prolonged, intense clashes. What classification fits each case?

**Solution:**

1. Armed force between states creates an IAC. No formal declaration of war is needed.
2. The other case has intense fighting and an organized armed group. Those facts support classification as a NIAC.

### #3: A Brief Riot

A small crowd causes a brief riot and disperses. There is no armed organization or continuing intense fighting. Does this establish a NIAC?

**Solution:**

1. A NIAC needs enough intensity and organization.
2. Neither requirement is established here. The riot does not establish a NIAC, although HRL still governs the state's response.

### #4: Protecting Civilians

A commander proposes attacking civilians who are not taking part in fighting to pressure the enemy. May the attack proceed?

**Solution:**

1. Distinction bars directly attacking protected civilians.
2. Pressure on the enemy does not turn civilians into military targets. The attack is prohibited.

### #5: Surrender and Wounds

An enemy fighter drops his weapon and clearly surrenders. Nearby, a wounded enemy cannot defend herself. Neither makes a hostile act or tries to escape. May either be attacked because of earlier fighting?

**Solution:**

1. The surrendered fighter is out of the fight under the stated conditions.
2. The wounded fighter is also unable to defend herself and meets the conditions for protection.
3. Neither may be attacked. Both must be treated humanely, and the wounded person must receive needed care.

### #6: Resuming Hostile Acts

A fighter surrenders, then picks up a weapon and starts firing again. Does the earlier surrender still protect him from attack?

**Solution:**

1. Protection as a person out of the fight requires no hostile acts.
2. Firing again breaks that condition. The earlier surrender no longer protects him from attack, but the applicable limits on force still apply.

### #7: A Prohibited Weapon

During war, an officer proposes using poison gas against enemy forces. He argues that the attack would achieve a clear military gain. Does that reason make the weapon lawful?

**Solution:**

1. The Geneva Gas Protocol prohibits the use of poison gas in war.
2. Military gain does not override the weapons ban. The proposed use is prohibited.

### #8: Civilian Harm and Military Gain

An ammunition depot is a military objective. A planned attack would offer only a small military gain but is expected to kill many nearby civilians and destroy much of their neighborhood. The expected civilian harm would be far greater than that gain could justify. Which rule bars the plan?

**Solution:**

1. The depot is a military target, but that is only the first check.
2. Proportionality bars an attack when expected civilian harm is too great compared with the expected military gain.
3. On these facts, the attack must not proceed as planned.

### #9: A Safer Attack Plan

A verified military objective can be attacked at either of two times. Waiting until nearby civilian workers leave is practical and gives the same military gain with less civilian danger. What should the planner do?

**Solution:**

1. Precautions require practical steps to avoid or reduce civilian harm.
2. The safer time is practical and preserves the military gain. The planner should use it and keep checking the situation.

### #10: HRL and Battlefield Targeting

During an IAC, an adviser argues that the right to life prohibits every attack on an active opposing combatant. Another says war removes the right to life entirely. How should the issue be assessed?

**Solution:**

1. HRL still protects the right to life. War does not remove it.
2. During battlefield fighting, IHL's targeting rules help decide whether taking life is arbitrary.
3. An attack on an active opposing combatant is not automatically prohibited. Weapons limits and civilian protections still apply.
4. Neither adviser's claim is correct.

### #11: An Invalid Emergency Claim

A government cites an ordinary protest as a reason to suspend ICCPR duties. It has not officially announced an emergency threatening the life of the nation. Is Article $4$ available on these facts?

**Solution:**

1. Article $4$ requires a qualifying national emergency and an official announcement.
2. Those conditions are absent. The protest alone does not justify derogation.

### #12: Torture During a Qualifying Emergency

A state officially announces an emergency threatening the life of the nation. It then suspends the torture ban to question detainees. Is that measure permitted under ICCPR Article $4$?

**Solution:**

1. A qualifying emergency does not permit suspension of every right.
2. The torture ban is non-derogable under Article $4$. The measure is forbidden.

### #13: Limits and Notice

During a qualifying emergency, a state suspends an ICCPR duty that allows derogation. Its measure lasts longer than needed, targets people solely by religion, and is never reported internationally. What must be corrected?

**Solution:**

1. The measure must last no longer and go no further than the emergency requires.
2. Choosing people solely by religion violates Article $4$'s nondiscrimination rule.
3. The state must immediately notify other ICCPR states through the UN Secretary-General. It must name the affected treaty duties and explain why it acted.
4. It must also report the date the suspension ends.

### #14: IHL Preparation in Peace

A state is at peace. It teaches soldiers the Third Geneva Convention and passes laws to punish grave breaches. Is this inconsistent with IHL's role in armed conflict?

**Solution:**

1. Some IHL duties require action before conflict begins.
2. Teaching treaty rules and preparing laws help meet those duties. These actions are consistent with IHL.

### #15: Resolving Competing Community Needs

One group wants a shared hall for evening study. Another wants it for evening meetings. Both reject giving up all evening access. What process could support a peaceful agreement?

**Solution:**

1. Hear both groups and clarify their needs, including time, quiet, and space.
2. Discuss lawful options, such as different evenings or separate rooms if available.
3. Agree on a clear schedule and a way to review problems later. This addresses both groups' needs without threats or violence.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

First ask whether the facts establish armed conflict. Force between states points to an IAC. For a NIAC, check both intensity and organization.

Then find the issue: a weapon, an attack plan, treatment in custody, or emergency suspension. Each has its own rules. One lawful step does not make every part of an action lawful.

For an attack, check the target, expected civilian harm, and practical safety steps separately. For an ICCPR emergency, check whether the emergency qualifies before testing each measure. Then check whether the affected protection can be suspended at all.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: War Does Not End HRL

> IHL and HRL can apply to the same event.

Use IHL's specific rules when judging battlefield conduct. That does not give permission to ignore other human-rights duties.

### #2: Labels Do Not Establish Armed Conflict

> Calling unrest a war does not settle its classification.

For a NIAC, the facts must show enough intensity and organization. An isolated riot does not automatically meet those requirements.

### #3: Surrender Has Continuing Conditions

> Starting to fight again ends protection based on an earlier surrender.

Protection as hors de combat requires no hostile acts and no escape attempt. People in custody still have protection against torture and unlawful punishment.

### #4: A Military Target Is Only One Check

> A lawful target does not make every weapon or attack plan lawful.

Weapons bans, proportionality, and precautions still apply. A warning does not excuse an otherwise prohibited attack.

### #5: An Official Announcement Is Not Enough

> A real emergency does not permit suspension of the torture ban.

Each emergency measure must meet Article $4$'s rules. Its need, scope, length, equal treatment, and required notices all matter.

### #6: Peacetime Still Requires Preparation

> States must prepare to follow IHL before war starts.

Treaty teaching, military training, and laws for grave breaches help meet IHL duties. They do not turn ordinary peacetime events into armed conflicts.
