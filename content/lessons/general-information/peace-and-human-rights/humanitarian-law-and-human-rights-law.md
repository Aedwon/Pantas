## Core Concept

### Human Rights Law

**Human Rights Law (HRL)** protects people's life, freedom, and dignity. It applies in peace and during armed conflict. It mainly places duties on governments toward people within their **jurisdiction**, their territory or authority or control.

A government's duties may also reach people it controls outside its borders. The exact reach depends on the right and the facts. War does not turn off human rights.

Some treaty duties allow temporary suspension during a grave emergency. Other duties cannot be suspended in this way. The ICCPR rules below explain this difference.

### International Humanitarian Law

**International Humanitarian Law (IHL)** limits harm during armed conflict. It protects people who are not fighting or have stopped fighting. It also limits weapons and the ways parties fight.

IHL binds all parties to an armed conflict. This includes government forces and organized armed groups outside government. Neither a claim to a just cause nor an enemy's abuse excuses breaking IHL.

An **international armed conflict (IAC)** exists when states use armed force against each other. A formal declaration of war is not needed. Occupation can also bring IHL into force even without armed resistance.

A **non-international armed conflict (NIAC)** involves sufficiently intense fighting between government forces and organized armed groups, or between such groups. Both the level of fighting and the groups' organization matter.

An organized group has features such as leaders who direct fighters and an ability to plan operations. The scale, length, and frequency of clashes help show the fighting's intensity. No single count of clashes settles every case.

A riot, brief disturbance, or isolated violent act does not automatically meet these conditions. Facts decide the classification. An official label alone does not.

### How the Laws Work Together

HRL and IHL are **complementary**, meaning they help protect people together. In armed conflict, IHL supplies rules made for fighting. HRL continues to protect people against unlawful government action.

The right to life forbids **arbitrary killing**, taking life without a lawful basis. During battlefield fighting, specific IHL rules help decide whether a killing violates that protection. This does not cancel other human-rights duties.

An attack on an active opposing combatant in an IAC is not automatically unlawful because it takes life. The target's status and the applicable attack rules matter. A person held in custody is not an active battlefield target merely because war continues nearby.

### Emergency Suspension Under the ICCPR

The **International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR)** is a human-rights treaty. Article $4$ sets rules for temporarily setting aside some treaty duties. The legal term is **derogation**.

The emergency must threaten the life of the nation. The government must officially announce it. An ordinary protest alone does not meet this test.

Each measure must be strictly needed to meet that emergency. It must go no further and last no longer than needed. The state must also follow its other duties under international law.

Measures must not discriminate solely because of race, color, sex, language, religion, or social origin. Government cannot use those traits alone to choose who loses a protection.

The state must immediately notify other ICCPR states through the United Nations Secretary-General. It must name the affected treaty duties and give its reasons. It must send another notice stating when the suspension ends.

**Non-derogable rights** have treaty protection that cannot be suspended under Article $4$. The ICCPR includes these protections:

- The right to life and the ban on torture or cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment. Medical or scientific experiments also require free consent.
- The bans on slavery and **servitude**, being forced to live and work under another person's control.
- Protection from prison merely because someone cannot meet a duty under a contract.
- Protection against **retroactive criminal punishment**. A later law cannot punish an earlier lawful act or increase punishment for an earlier crime.
- Recognition as a person before the law. Government must recognize each human being as someone who can hold legal rights.
- Freedom of thought, conscience, and religion. **Conscience** means beliefs about right and wrong.

Non-derogable does not mean every part of a right has no limits. For example, outward religious practice may face necessary limits under the treaty's own rules. Government cannot suspend freedom of belief under Article $4$.

The right to life still bars arbitrary killing in an emergency. The torture ban has no emergency exception. ICCPR Article $4$ does not authorize suspension of IHL duties.

### People Protected from Attack

**Civilians** are people outside the armed forces and other fighting forces covered by the relevant IHL rules. They must not be directly attacked. A civilian loses this protection for the time that the person directly takes part in fighting.

A person **hors de combat** is out of the fight. The person may be held by the opponent, clearly surrendering, or unable to defend himself or herself because of wounds or illness.

Protection from attack requires no hostile acts and no attempt to escape. Someone who starts fighting again cannot rely on an earlier surrender. Any force used must still follow the rules for that situation.

People in custody must receive humane treatment that respects their basic needs and worth. They must not be tortured or executed without a lawful trial. Wounded and sick people must receive needed care without unfair discrimination.

### Weapons and Attack Methods

The **means of warfare** are weapons. The **methods of warfare** are the ways parties fight. Both have legal limits.

IHL bans weapons that are **indiscriminate by nature**. They cannot be aimed at a specific military target, or their effects cannot be kept within IHL's limits. Such weapons cannot reliably separate military targets from protected people and places.

Weapons must not cause **superfluous injury or unnecessary suffering**. This means injury or suffering beyond what a lawful military purpose can justify. The protection covers fighters too.

Treaties also prohibit particular weapons. The Geneva Gas Protocol prohibits using poison gas in war. A claimed military benefit does not cancel that ban.

**Distinction** means separating military targets from protected civilians and civilian objects, such as homes. Attacks must aim at military targets. A home does not become a military target simply because destroying it would frighten the enemy.

A **military objective** is an object that effectively helps military action. Destroying, capturing, or putting it out of use must offer a definite military gain in the circumstances. Both conditions must be met.

**Proportionality** sets a separate limit on attacks against military targets. Planners must compare expected civilian deaths, injuries, and damage with the concrete, direct military gain expected. They must not attack if that civilian harm would be excessive compared with the gain.

This rule concerns **incidental harm**, civilian harm that is not the attack's aim. A small military gain cannot justify very great civilian harm. Directly attacking protected civilians is forbidden regardless of the claimed gain.

**Precautions** are practical safety steps to avoid or reduce civilian harm. Planners must check the target and consider safer weapons or timing. They must give an effective warning when circumstances allow it.

An attack must be canceled or stopped if new information shows the target is unlawful. The same applies if expected civilian harm becomes excessive compared with the expected military gain. A warning does not make an unlawful attack lawful.

### Duties Before War and Work for Peace

Some IHL duties require action in peacetime. Article $127$ of the Third Geneva Convention requires teaching its rules in peace and war, including military instruction. Authorities responsible for prisoners of war must have the text and receive special instruction.

States must also pass laws needed to punish **grave breaches**, serious violations listed in the Geneva Conventions. Torture of a protected prisoner is an example. Training and laws prepare states to meet treaty duties before fighting starts.

IHL limits harm during armed conflict. Peaceful conflict resolution seeks to settle disputes and prevent more violence. Any agreement must still respect basic rights.

For a community dispute, hear each side and define the issue. Ask what each side needs, then discuss lawful options. Agree on clear steps and a way to check whether people follow them.

These are practical steps for seeking agreement. They do not decide whether violence legally counts as armed conflict. That question requires the separate IHL tests.

## Examples

### #1: Comparing the Two Bodies of Law

Which statement correctly compares HRL and IHL?

- A. HRL applies only in peace, while IHL binds all parties to armed conflict.
- B. HRL applies in peace and war, while IHL governs armed conflict and binds its parties.
- C. HRL applies in peace and war, while IHL binds only government forces.
- D. HRL applies only in war, while IHL governs armed conflict and ordinary riots.

**Solution:**

1. HRL applies in peace and during armed conflict. Its main duties fall on states.
2. IHL governs armed conflict and binds all parties, including organized armed groups.
3. Choice B states the correct comparison.

### #2: Classifying Fighting

State A's forces attack State B's forces. Elsewhere, government troops fight a group with an organized command in prolonged, intense clashes. What classification fits each case?

**Solution:**

1. Armed force between states creates an IAC. No declaration of war is needed.
2. The other case has intense fighting and an organized armed group. It fits the NIAC conditions.

### #3: Occupation Without Resistance

One state's armed forces occupy another state's territory without armed resistance. Does the absence of fighting alone keep IHL from applying?

**Solution:**

No. Occupation can trigger the IHL rules for an IAC even without armed resistance.

### #4: A Brief Riot

A small crowd causes a brief riot and disperses. There is no armed organization or continuing intense fighting. Does this establish a NIAC?

**Solution:**

1. A NIAC requires enough intensity and organization.
2. Neither condition is established. The riot does not establish a NIAC, but HRL still governs the state's response.

### #5: Protecting Civilians

A commander proposes attacking civilians who are not taking part in fighting to pressure the enemy. May the attack proceed?

**Solution:**

1. Distinction bars directly attacking protected civilians.
2. Pressure on the enemy does not turn civilians into military targets. The attack is prohibited.

### #6: A Civilian Taking Part in Fighting

A civilian directly takes part in an attack during an armed conflict. Does civilian status give that person protection from attack throughout that act?

**Solution:**

1. A civilian loses protection from attack for the time of direct participation in fighting.
2. The act falls within that period. The applicable weapons and attack rules still limit any attack on the person.

### #7: Surrender and Wounds

A fighter drops his weapon and clearly surrenders. Nearby, a wounded enemy cannot defend herself. Neither makes a hostile act or tries to escape. May either be attacked because of earlier fighting?

**Solution:**

1. The surrendering fighter is out of the fight under the stated conditions.
2. The wounded fighter also meets the conditions for protection.
3. Neither may be attacked. Both need humane treatment, and the wounded person must receive needed care.

### #8: Resuming Hostile Acts

A fighter surrenders, then picks up a weapon and starts firing again. Does the earlier surrender still protect him from attack?

**Solution:**

1. Protection as a person out of the fight requires no hostile acts.
2. Firing again breaks that condition. The earlier surrender no longer protects him from attack, but limits on force still apply.

### #9: Treatment in Custody

Government soldiers hold an enemy fighter who is no longer resisting. An officer claims IHL replaces HRL, so torture is allowed. Is the claim correct?

**Solution:**

1. IHL protects people held by the opponent and requires humane treatment.
2. HRL continues to apply and also bars torture.
3. Both bodies of law protect this detainee. The officer's claim is incorrect.

### #10: A Prohibited Weapon

During war, an officer proposes poison gas against enemy forces. He claims a clear military gain. Does that reason make the weapon lawful?

**Solution:**

1. The Geneva Gas Protocol prohibits using poison gas in war.
2. Military gain does not override the ban. The proposed use is prohibited.

### #11: General Weapons Limits

A proposed weapon cannot be directed at a specific military target or have its effects kept within IHL limits. Another causes injury beyond what any lawful military purpose can justify. Which rules apply?

**Solution:**

1. The first weapon is indiscriminate by nature. IHL prohibits it.
2. The second violates the ban on superfluous injury or unnecessary suffering. A weapon can violate this rule even when aimed at fighters.

### #12: A Civilian Building

A building does not help military action. A commander wants to destroy it only to frighten civilians. Does that purpose make it a military objective?

**Solution:**

1. A military objective must effectively help military action, and its destruction must offer a definite military gain.
2. The building fails the first condition. Frightening civilians does not make it a lawful target.

### #13: Civilian Harm and Military Gain

An ammunition depot is a military objective. An attack offers a small military gain but is expected to kill many nearby civilians. The expected civilian harm would be excessive compared with that gain. Which rule bars the plan?

**Solution:**

1. The depot is a military target, but the attack needs a separate proportionality check.
2. Proportionality bars expected incidental civilian harm that is excessive compared with the concrete, direct military gain expected.
3. On these facts, the attack must not proceed as planned.

### #14: A Safer Attack Plan

A verified military objective can be attacked at either of two times. Waiting until civilian workers leave is practical and gives the same military gain with less civilian danger. What should the planner do?

**Solution:**

1. Precautions require practical steps to avoid or reduce civilian harm.
2. The safer time is practical and preserves the gain. The planner should use it and keep checking the situation.

### #15: New Information Before an Attack

After warning civilians, a planner learns that the intended target no longer serves any military purpose. May the attack proceed because a warning was given?

**Solution:**

1. New information shows that the object is no longer a lawful military target.
2. The attack must be canceled or stopped. A warning cannot correct an unlawful target.

### #16: HRL and Battlefield Targeting

During an IAC, an adviser says the right to life prohibits every attack on an active opposing combatant. Another says war removes the right to life entirely. How should these claims be assessed?

**Solution:**

1. HRL still protects the right to life during war.
2. During battlefield fighting, IHL targeting rules help decide whether taking life is arbitrary.
3. Attacking an active opposing combatant is not automatically prohibited. Weapons rules, proportionality, and precautions still apply.
4. Neither adviser's claim is correct.

### #17: An Invalid Emergency Claim

A government cites an ordinary protest to suspend ICCPR duties. It has not officially announced an emergency threatening the life of the nation. Is Article $4$ available on these facts?

**Solution:**

1. Article $4$ requires both a qualifying emergency and an official announcement.
2. Those conditions are absent. The protest alone does not justify derogation.

### #18: Torture During an Emergency

A state officially announces an emergency threatening the life of the nation. It suspends the torture ban to question detainees. Is this permitted under ICCPR Article $4$?

**Solution:**

1. A qualifying emergency does not permit suspension of every right.
2. The torture ban is non-derogable. The measure is forbidden.

### #19: Other Protected Rights

During a qualifying emergency, officials propose slavery, prison merely for inability to meet a contract duty, and punishment for acts lawful when done. Can Article $4$ authorize these steps?

**Solution:**

1. The bans on slavery, imprisonment merely for inability to meet contract duties, and retroactive criminal punishment are non-derogable.
2. Article $4$ cannot authorize any of these steps.

### #20: Belief and Legal Recognition

Officials claim an emergency lets them deny a group's members legal recognition as persons. They also claim power to force members to change their beliefs or undergo experiments without free consent. Is Article $4$ a valid basis?

**Solution:**

1. Recognition as a person before the law and freedom of thought, conscience, and religion are non-derogable.
2. The protection against medical or scientific experiments without free consent is also non-derogable.
3. Article $4$ cannot authorize the proposed measures.

### #21: Limits and Notice

During a qualifying emergency, a state suspends an ICCPR duty that permits derogation. Its measure lasts longer than needed, targets people solely by religion, and is never reported internationally. What must change?

**Solution:**

1. The measure must go no further and last no longer than strictly needed.
2. It cannot target people solely by religion.
3. The state must immediately notify other ICCPR states through the UN Secretary-General, naming affected duties and reasons.
4. It must also report when the suspension ends.

### #22: IHL Preparation in Peace

A state at peace teaches soldiers the Third Geneva Convention and passes laws to punish grave breaches. Is this inconsistent with IHL's role in armed conflict?

**Solution:**

1. Some IHL duties require action before conflict begins.
2. Article $127$ requires teaching the Convention in peace and war. Laws punishing grave breaches meet another preparation duty.
3. These actions are consistent with IHL.

### #23: Competing Community Needs

One group wants a shared hall for evening study. Another wants it for evening meetings. Both reject giving up all evening access. What process could support peaceful agreement?

**Solution:**

1. Hear both groups and clarify their needs, including time, quiet, and space.
2. Discuss lawful options, such as different evenings or separate rooms if available.
3. Agree on a clear schedule and a way to review problems. Each group can then check whether the agreement meets its needs.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

First check whether the facts establish armed conflict. Force between states points to an IAC. For a NIAC, check both intensity and organization.

Then identify the issue: a weapon, an attack plan, treatment in custody, or emergency suspension. Each has its own rules. One lawful step does not make the whole action lawful.

For attacks, check the target, expected civilian harm, and practical safety steps separately. For ICCPR emergencies, first check the emergency's conditions. Then check whether the affected protection can be suspended and whether each measure meets Article $4$.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: War Does Not End HRL

> IHL and HRL can apply to the same event.

Use IHL's specific rules for battlefield conduct. The mistaken answer treats IHL as permission to ignore every human-rights duty.

### #2: Labels Do Not Establish Armed Conflict

> Calling unrest a war does not decide its classification.

A NIAC requires enough intensity and organization. A brief riot alone does not meet those conditions.

### #3: Surrender Has Continuing Conditions

> Starting to fight again ends protection based on an earlier surrender.

Protection as hors de combat requires no hostile acts or escape attempt. This does not remove a detained person's protection against torture or unlawful punishment.

### #4: A Military Target Is Only One Check

> A lawful target does not make every weapon or attack plan lawful.

Weapons bans, proportionality, and precautions still apply. A warning does not excuse an otherwise prohibited attack.

### #5: An Announcement Is Not Enough

> An officially announced emergency does not permit suspension of the torture ban.

Each measure must meet Article $4$'s conditions. Its need, scope, length, nondiscrimination, and required notices all matter.

### #6: Peacetime Requires Preparation

> States must prepare to follow IHL before war starts.

Treaty teaching, military training, and grave-breach laws help meet IHL duties. These duties do not turn ordinary peacetime events into armed conflicts.
