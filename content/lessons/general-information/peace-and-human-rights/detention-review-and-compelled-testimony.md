## Core Concept

### Section $15$: Habeas Corpus and Its Narrow Suspension

Article III, Section $15$ protects the **privilege of the writ of habeas corpus**. **Habeas corpus** is a court process checking whether detention is lawful, letting detainees seek release.

Suspending this privilege needs **both** conditions together:

1. An **invasion or rebellion** exists.
2. **Public safety requires** the suspension.

Even during a valid suspension, the rest of the Bill of Rights remains active. Only detention review is affected, while bail and fair-trial rights continue.

Test a city bus strike where officials declare habeas corpus suspended with no rebellion or invasion:

1. Check condition one: a strike is neither invasion nor rebellion.
2. Apply Section $15$: without invasion or rebellion, suspension fails.

Result: Officials cannot suspend habeas corpus during an ordinary strike.

:::why
Why require both invasion or rebellion and public-safety need? Holding people without immediate court review is extreme, so unrest without public-safety necessity never justifies suspension.
:::

:::pair #suspension-needs-rebellion-and-safety
Right: Require both invasion or rebellion and public-safety need before suspending habeas corpus.
Wrong: Treating invasion or rebellion alone as enough without showing public safety requires it.
Both conditions are mandatory; neither works alone under Section $15$.
:::

:::pair #suspension-keeps-bill-of-rights
Right: Keep the rest of the Bill of Rights active during any habeas corpus suspension.
Wrong: Assuming suspending habeas corpus removes the entire Bill of Rights.
Section $15$ touches only detention review, never speech, counsel, or humane treatment.
:::

:::check
During an armed rebellion, public safety requires suspending habeas corpus in one province. Does that erase the right to bail under Section $13$?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Suspending habeas corpus never removes other Bill of Rights protections, including bail.
:::

### Sections $16$ and $17$: Speedy Disposition and Self-Incrimination

Article III, Section $16$ protects **speedy disposition of cases**. It covers all **judicial, quasi-judicial, and administrative bodies**, extending beyond criminal trials.

Article III, Section $17$ bars forcing anyone to be a witness against himself or herself. It shields answers exposing the person to **criminal punishment**.

Section $17$ is not a blanket excuse to refuse harmless questions. If an answer carries no criminal risk, Section $17$ does not apply.

Compare two agency hearing situations:

1. An inspector orders a clerk to admit whether she forged receipts, risking theft charges: Section $17$ protects her refusal.
2. A board sits on a pension claim for $6$ years: undue delay violates Section $16$.

Result: Match forced criminal-risk answers to Section $17$, and unreasonable agency delays to Section $16$.

:::pair #self-incrimination-needs-criminal-risk
Right: Apply Section $17$ only to compelled answers risking criminal punishment.
Wrong: Treating Section $17$ as a general right to refuse all questions on any subject.
Check whether the forced answer creates criminal risk before claiming self-incrimination.
:::

:::check
At a civil licensing hearing, a witness refuses to state her mailing address, carrying zero criminal risk. Does Section $17$ excuse her?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Section $17$ shields against criminal self-incrimination, not harmless background questions.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Protest March and Habeas Corpus

Officials say a loud tax protest causes disorder, so they suspend habeas corpus for $30$ days. No invasion or rebellion exists. Is that valid?

**Solution:**

1. Check condition one: a protest is not an invasion or rebellion.
2. Apply Section $15$: disorder without invasion or rebellion never permits suspension.

Answer: No, Section $15$ requires both invasion or rebellion and public-safety need.

**Check:** Local protests never satisfy the invasion-or-rebellion requirement.

### #2: Agency Delay Versus Forced Confession

A board leaves a retiree's benefit petition undecided for $5$ years without cause. In a tax audit, an official forces an owner to admit criminal tax fraud. Match both cases.

**Solution:**

1. Check the $5$-year wait: undue agency delay breaks Section $16$ speedy disposition.
2. Check the forced fraud admission: compelling criminal-risk testimony breaks Section $17$.

Answer: The board delay breaks Section $16$, and forcing the fraud admission breaks Section $17$.

**Check:** Section $16$ covers courts and agencies; Section $17$ blocks forced criminal self-incrimination.

### #3: Unrest Claims and Agency Speed

Officials suspend habeas corpus during a dockworkers' dispute to restore order, with no invasion or rebellion. Meanwhile, a civil-service board delays an appeal for $4$ years, claiming Section $16$ covers only criminal trials. Rule on both claims.

**Solution:**

1. Check the suspension: test both Section $15$ conditions against a labor dispute.
2. Check the $4$-year delay: test whether Section $16$ covers administrative and quasi-judicial bodies.

:::try
Finish the last step: rule on both the suspension and the board's Section $16$ claim.
:::answer
The answer is **Both official claims fail**. Section $15$ bars suspension without invasion or rebellion plus public-safety need, and Section $16$ covers administrative and quasi-judicial delays.

**Check:** Verify both Section $15$ prerequisites, and apply Section $16$ to all deciding bodies.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Invasion or rebellion occurs: verify public safety also requires suspending habeas corpus. {#suspension-needs-rebellion-and-safety}
- Habeas corpus is suspended: keep the rest of the Bill of Rights active. {#suspension-keeps-bill-of-rights}
- Witness invokes Section $17$: check whether the answer risks criminal punishment. {#self-incrimination-needs-criminal-risk}
:::
