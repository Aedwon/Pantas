## Core Concept

### How Clean Sorting Supports Recovery

Putting waste in a colored bin never recycles it alone. Sorting supports recovery only when items stay clean and reach a recovery step.

Food scraps and liquids soil clean paper, making recovery harder. Food contact spoils recyclables without turning paper into special waste. Keep accepted paper dry and separate until a collector takes it for processing.

Keep sound reusable items apart from dirty trash too. Wash intact jars for safe reuse, and never reuse containers that held paint or toxic chemicals for food or drinking water.

Compare $2$ paper-bin habits at a print shop:

1. Staff stack dry trimmings in a covered bin for weekly pickup by a paper mill.
2. Staff pour leftover soup into the paper bin, soaking clean trimmings.

Result: Dry sorting enables mill recovery, while food mixing contaminates paper and blocks recycling.

:::pair #mixing-contaminates-recyclables
Right: Keep food scraps out of clean paper because mixing contaminates materials for recovery.
Wrong: Treating food-soiled paper as automatically turned into special waste by food contact.
Food contact soils and spoils recyclables without turning paper into hazardous special waste.
:::

:::check
A canteen sets a blue recycling bin beside a sink and dumps wet fish bones onto clean folders inside. Does the blue bin guarantee recycling?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Wet food contaminates the paper, and a separate bin alone never proves recovery happened.
:::

### Reuse, Recycling, and Legal Overlap

In everyday terms, **reuse** and **recycling** work differently:

- **Reuse:** using an intact item again without changing its basic physical or chemical form, like storing bolts in a cleaned glass jar.
- **Recycling:** processing discarded material into useful material or a new product, like pulping newspapers into cardboard.

Watch **RA $9003$ Section $3$(bb)** closely. Its legal definition of **recycling** expressly includes collecting, separating, and reusing used packaging. Check whether a question tests everyday physical differences or RA $9003$'s broad statutory wording.

:::why
Why does RA $9003$ include reusing used packaging inside its definition of recycling? The law defines recycling broadly to support packaging recovery alongside industrial reprocessing.
:::

Classify $2$ everyday practices:

1. A bakery washes intact plastic bread crates and refills them daily.
2. A plant melts crushed aluminum cans to roll new metal sheets.

Result: Using intact bread crates again is reuse, while melting cans into new sheets is recycling.

:::pair #reuse-keeps-form-intact
Right: Classify using an intact item again without changing its basic form as reuse.
Wrong: Calling reuse of an intact container recycling into a different material.
Everyday reuse keeps the item's form, whereas reprocessing turns waste into new material.
:::

:::pair #processing-makes-new-products
Right: Classify processing waste into new products as recycling rather than direct reuse.
Wrong: Calling processing waste paper into new sheets direct reuse without processing.
Direct reuse uses old sheets as they stand, while recycling makes new products.
:::

:::check
A library cuts old one-sided flyers into scratch pads for notes without sending them to a paper mill. Everyday reuse or recycling?
:::answer
The answer is **Reuse**. Using the intact sheets again without changing their basic form is reuse.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Protecting Glass for Recovery

A beverage store keeps unbroken bottles clean, washes intact delivery crates for reuse, and sends cracked glass to a melting plant. Classify the crates and cracked glass.

**Solution:**

1. Intact crates are washed and used again in their same form, which is reuse.
2. Cracked glass is melted and processed into new glass products, which is recycling.

Answer: Reusing intact crates is reuse, and melting cracked glass is recycling.

**Check:** Check whether the item keeps its form or undergoes processing.

### #2: Storage Shed Without Recovery

A barangay collects dry tin cans away from fish scraps. Workers then lock the cans in a shed for $6$ months without sending them to any processor. Did recycling occur?

**Solution:**

1. Keeping cans clean and dry prepares them for recovery.
2. Leaving cans locked in a shed does not process them into new products.

Answer: No. Clean sorting prepares materials, while recycling requires reaching recovery processing.

**Check:** Follow sorted cans past storage to confirm actual processing.

### #3: Used Cartons and Shredded Drums

A soap factory washes returned shipping cartons and uses them intact again. Nearby, a firm shreds old plastic drums into pellets for park benches. An intern says reusing cartons never counts under RA $9003$'s recycling definition, and calls shredding drums reuse. Correct both errors.

**Solution:**

1. Check how RA $9003$ Section $3$(bb) treats reusing used packaging inside its legal definition of recycling.
2. Check whether shredding plastic drums into park benches keeps their form or processes them.

:::try
Finish the last step: correct the intern's legal claim on cartons and classify shredding drums into benches.
:::answer
The answer is **RA $9003$ Section $3$(bb) includes reusing used packaging within recycling, and shredding drums into benches is recycling**. Shredding and reforming plastic makes a new product, unlike everyday reuse.

**Check:** Separate RA $9003$'s packaging rule from the physical form test for everyday reuse.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Paper contamination: keep food scraps off clean paper instead of calling soiled paper special waste. {#mixing-contaminates-recyclables}
- Form unchanged: classify using intact items again as reuse rather than recycling into a new material. {#reuse-keeps-form-intact}
- Processing step: classify turning waste into new products as recycling rather than direct reuse. {#processing-makes-new-products}
:::
