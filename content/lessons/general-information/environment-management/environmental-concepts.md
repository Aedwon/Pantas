## Core Concept

Waste management starts where waste is made. Keeping useful materials separate can help people recover them before they reach a disposal site.

### Segregation at Source

**Segregation at source** means separating waste where it is produced. Homes, schools, offices, and businesses sort it before collection. Republic Act $9003$ requires this practice.

The law calls for separate, identified containers for compostable, recyclable, non-recyclable, and special waste. The category tells people how the material should be handled next.

**Compostable waste** can break down through composting. Suitable food scraps and garden leaves belong here. Composting uses tiny living things, such as bacteria and fungi, to turn organic matter into a soil-like product.

**Recyclable waste** can be recovered for useful use. Clean paper, glass, and metal are common examples. Keep them apart from wet food waste, and follow the receiving facility's rules on accepted materials.

**Non-recyclable waste** cannot be recovered through the available recycling process. Ordinary waste that is neither compostable nor special may belong here. A wrapper that the local facility cannot recycle is one example.

**Special waste** needs separate handling. It includes household hazardous waste, such as used batteries, paint, thinner, and spray canisters. Discarded consumer electronics, such as broken radios, are also included under RA $9003$.

Do not assume that everything made of metal belongs in an ordinary recycling bin. A used battery belongs with special waste. Follow local collection instructions for its safe handling.

### How Sorting Helps Recovery

Food and liquid can soil paper and make it harder to recycle. Keep accepted paper dry and separate, then send it to a collector or facility that recovers it. Sorting supports recycling only when the materials reach the proper next step.

Reusable items also benefit from separation. Keep a sound storage container apart from dirty waste, clean it for suitable use, and use it again. Do not reuse containers that held hazardous chemicals for food or drinking water.

### Waste Diversion

**Waste diversion** means keeping waste away from final disposal through recovery and similar actions. **Reduction** prevents waste at its source. Together, reduction, reuse, recycling, and composting can lower the amount needing disposal.

**Reuse** means using an item again without changing its basic physical or chemical form. Using a sound storage box again is reuse. **Recycling** can involve processing discarded material into useful material or another product.

These terms can overlap under the law. RA $9003$ also treats collecting, separating, and reusing used packaging as recycling. A question about the law's definition may therefore use “recycling” more broadly than a simple processing example.

Sorting alone does not prove diversion. If separated waste is mixed again and all sent for disposal, the sorting has not kept that waste out of disposal.

## Examples

### #1: Sorting Where Waste Is Made

A household separates food scraps, clean paper, used batteries, and non-recyclable wrappers before collection. What practice does this show?

**Solution:**

1. The household separates materials where the waste is made.
2. This is **segregation at source**.

### #2: Choosing the Waste Category

A school has garden leaves for composting, clean paper accepted by its recycler, an ordinary wrapper the recycler rejects, and a used household battery. How should these be sorted?

**Solution:**

1. Put the garden leaves with compostable waste.
2. Keep the accepted clean paper with recyclable waste.
3. Put the ordinary rejected wrapper with non-recyclable waste.
4. Keep the battery with special waste for separate handling.

### #3: Preventing Waste

An office stops ordering unnecessary disposable stirrers for its pantry. Which method best describes this action?

**Solution:**

1. The office avoids producing that waste in the first place.
2. The method is **reduction**.

### #4: Keeping an Item in Use

An office sets aside sound storage boxes during a cleanup and uses them to organize supplies. Which method is most directly shown?

**Solution:**

1. The boxes remain in their basic form.
2. Using them again is **reuse**. Keeping them separate made that reuse easier.

### #5: Completing the Recycling Process

A school keeps accepted paper dry and separate. A recycler collects it and processes it into new paper products. Which method keeps the paper out of disposal?

**Solution:**

1. Separating the paper supports recovery.
2. Processing it into new paper products is **recycling**.
3. The paper is diverted because it becomes useful material instead of going to final disposal.

### #6: Making Compost

A barangay separates suitable food scraps and leaves and manages their breakdown into compost. Which method is shown?

**Solution:**

1. The organic waste breaks down through a controlled process.
2. This is **composting**. It keeps those materials out of final disposal.

### #7: Sorting Without Diversion

An office uses separate waste bins. The collector later mixes all their contents and sends everything for disposal. Has the office's sorting alone demonstrated waste diversion?

**Solution:**

1. The office performed source segregation.
2. All the material still went to final disposal.
3. The facts do not show diversion through reuse, recycling, composting, or another recovery method.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Treating Every Separate Bin as Recycling

> A bin separates material, but its contents still need proper handling.

Clean paper must reach a recovery process to become recycled paper. Sorting helps that process. It does not prove that processing happened.

### #2: Making Reuse and Recycling Mutually Exclusive

> Check whether the question asks for an everyday action or the law's definition.

Using an item again without changing its basic form shows reuse. RA $9003$ also includes collecting, separating, and reusing used packaging within recycling. Do not reject that legal usage simply because no new product was made.

### #3: Sending Batteries With Ordinary Recyclables

> A used household battery is special waste.

Its metal parts do not make ordinary mixed collection the correct choice. Keep it separate and follow the special-waste collection instructions.
