## Core Concept

### Weather, Climate, and Greenhouse Warming

**Weather** is the condition on a single day or over a short period, such as afternoon drizzle or a cool morning. **Climate change** means a lasting, long-term shift in climate patterns over decades.

Human activities add **greenhouse gases** to the air, where they trap heat in the atmosphere. Added warming raises risks of rising seas, extreme heat, droughts, intense rain, and coastal flooding.

Compare $2$ observations in a coastal province to separate weather from climate:

1. A sudden Tuesday afternoon thunderstorm cools down $1$ town for $3$ hours.
2. Thirty-year weather records show dry seasons starting earlier and lasting $3$ weeks longer.

Result: The Tuesday storm is short-term weather, while the $30$-year shift reflects climate change.

:::why
Why can $1$ chilly week not disprove long-term warming? Daily weather rises and falls naturally, while climate change tracks average conditions over many decades.
:::

:::pair #weather-not-climate
Right: Weather describes short-term conditions like afternoon rain, while climate tracks long-term patterns.
Wrong: Treating a long-term shift in seasonal rainfall patterns as short-term daily weather.
Multi-year rainfall shifts describe climate trends rather than a single day's forecast.
:::

:::check
After $2$ unusually cool mornings in January, a radio caller claims the region's climate has permanently cooled. Is that claim sound?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Two cool mornings are short-term weather and cannot establish or disprove a long-term climate trend.
:::

### Economic, Social, and Policy Effects

Climate change disrupts **ecosystems** (living things plus surrounding soil, water, and air) and harms communities in $3$ distinct ways:

- **Economic effects:** damage to harvests, jobs, income, property, and repair budgets.
- **Social effects:** harm to health, housing, displacement, schooling, and access to public services.
- **Political and policy aspects:** government choices on budgets, laws, land-use rules, agency coordination, and international agreements.

One event often creates overlapping effects that stay distinct by what each measures. Notice how $1$ storm hits a seaside town:

1. Flooded piers stop ferry trips so boat crews lose daily wages (economic effect).
2. Ruined roofs force families into emergency shelters and halt classes (social effect).
3. The town council votes funds for seawall repairs and drainage rules (policy aspect).

Result: Lost income is economic, disrupted housing and classes are social, and public funding is policy.

:::pair #social-versus-economic-effects
Right: Economic effects track income, production, or repair bills, while social effects track schooling, health, and housing.
Wrong: Classifying missed school lessons during a flood as an economic effect rather than a social effect.
Lost class time affects education and community welfare rather than measuring money or production.
:::

:::check
A prolonged heat wave dries up tilapia fishponds, cutting fishers' sales and forcing nearby clinics to treat heat exhaustion. Identify the economic effect and the social effect.
:::answer
The answer is **Lost fish sales are economic, and heat-related illness is social**. Reduced income affects earnings, while illness affects residents' health.
:::

### Checking Local Causes of Floods

Climate change can worsen heavy rain, yet it does not explain every flood alone. Blocked drains, concreted yards, and hillside clearing also increase flood damage.

Conversely, finding clogged drains does not prove climate change played zero role in severe rainfall. Sound officials inspect local drainage and land use while seeking broader evidence on climate trends.

Check $2$ steps after flash flooding hits a growing town:

1. Engineers inspect street culverts and find plastic trash blocking drainage pipes.
2. Analysts compare multi-decade rainfall gauges to see if storm intensity is rising.

Result: Officials address local drainage problems alongside long-term rainfall evidence.

:::pair #single-flood-needs-multi-cause-check
Right: Investigate local causes like blocked drains alongside evidence of long-term climate change.
Wrong: Concluding from a single flood alone that climate change is its only cause.
Local drainage and land use shape flood damage, so $1$ event cannot settle attribution alone.
:::

:::check
A subdivision floods after heavy rain, and inspectors discover a builder filled in a natural creek nearby. Does the filled creek prove climate change had no role?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Local land changes worsen flooding, yet officials must still check long-term evidence to judge climate's role.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Two Effects on One Farm Town

A severe dry spell shrivels rice crops so farmers earn half their usual income. That same month, dried-up wells force families to haul water and skip clinic visits. Identify each effect.

**Solution:**

1. Shrinking rice harvests and lower farm earnings measure lost production and income, an economic effect.
2. Losing clean household water and missing health care affect daily welfare, a social effect.

Answer: Lower farm income is economic, and lost water and health access are social.

**Check:** Match income and output losses to economic effects and health or service disruptions to social effects.

### #2: A Town Council Votes on Seawalls

To handle rising tides, a town council passes a setback rule, funds dike repairs, and pairs $2$ offices to map floods. Which aspect is shown?

**Solution:**

1. Passing rules, allocating public money, and coordinating offices are government policy choices.
2. These actions reflect public decision-making even without any political party campaign.

Answer: These actions show the political and policy aspect of climate change.

**Check:** Link laws, budgets, and agency coordination to the policy aspect.

### #3: Mudslides After a Typhoon

Heavy typhoon rain triggers mudslides below a hillside where builders recently cleared trees and blocked natural gullies. One speaker blames climate change alone, while another says clearing explains everything and climate is irrelevant. Evaluate both claims.

**Solution:**

1. Test whether $1$ typhoon mudslide proves climate change is the sole cause.
2. Test whether hillside tree clearing and blocked gullies rule out any role for climate trends.

:::try
Finish the last step: evaluate both speakers' claims using evidence rules for single events.
:::answer
The answer is **Both speakers are wrong**. A single mudslide cannot prove climate change acted alone, and local hillside clearing does not rule out climate's role without broader evidence.

**Check:** Combine local site inspections with long-term climate data instead of jumping to single-cause claims.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Weather versus climate: separate single-day storms from multi-decade shifts in rainfall or temperature. {#weather-not-climate}
- Overlapping impacts: classify missed schooling and shelter loss as social, not economic. {#social-versus-economic-effects}
- Single-event blame: inspect local drains and land use while checking long-term climate evidence. {#single-flood-needs-multi-cause-check}
:::
