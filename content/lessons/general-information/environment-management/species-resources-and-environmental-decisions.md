## Core Concept

### Species, Habitats, and Ecosystems

A **species** is a group of living things of the same kind. A **habitat** is the place where a living thing gets the food, water, shelter, and space it needs.

An **ecosystem** is a community of living things and the nonliving parts around them, such as water, soil, and air. A change to one part can affect other parts.

People can affect ecosystems in several direct ways. Cutting or clearing habitat can remove food, shelter, or breeding places. Pollution can change water, soil, or air and make it harder for organisms to survive.

Taking wildlife or other resources too quickly can also cause harm. If a population or resource is removed faster than it can recover, less remains for the future.

### Protecting Species

Species conservation means helping wildlife populations survive and recover. The action should match the cause of the problem.

Useful actions include protecting or restoring habitat, limiting harmful collection or trade, and reducing pollution or other pressures. For a threatened species, protecting the place it depends on can be as important as protecting the animals or plants themselves.

Republic Act (RA) $9147$, the Wildlife Resources Conservation and Protection Act, protects wildlife resources and their habitats. It also provides for **critical habitats** outside protected areas where threatened species are found.

A critical habitat is chosen using scientific data, including the presence of threatened species and pressures on their survival. Once designated, it must be protected from exploitation or destruction that can harm the threatened species that depend on it.

RA $9147$ also allows conservation breeding or propagation of threatened species. This work goes together with rehabilitation or protection of the habitat where the species may be released.

### Protecting Natural Resources

A **natural resource** is something from nature that people use, such as water, forests, soil, minerals, or wildlife. Resource conservation means using and managing these resources so they are not wasted or damaged faster than they can recover.

Good resource actions depend on the resource. They may include reducing waste, reusing materials, recycling, protecting source areas, restoring damaged places, or limiting harvest so a renewable resource has time to recover.

Conservation does not always mean "never use." It means the use must fit the resource, the law, and the need to keep the resource available.

### Protected Areas

RA $7586$, as amended by RA $11038$, provides the National Integrated Protected Areas System (NIPAS). The system covers protected areas that need long-term management for conservation.

NIPAS has protected-area categories such as strict nature reserve, natural park, natural monument, wildlife sanctuary, protected landscape or seascape, resource reserve, and natural biotic area. The category helps describe what kind of area is being protected.

Each protected area has a management plan. The plan can use tools such as zoning, buffer-zone management, habitat conservation, and habitat rehabilitation.

A **critical habitat** under RA $9147$ and a **protected area** under NIPAS are not the same label. A critical habitat may be designated outside an existing protected area when threatened species need protection there.

### Choosing Among Environmental Options

**Evaluate** means judge the choices using evidence and reasons. Do not choose an option only because it sounds green or costs less.

First, identify the goal. Then compare how each option affects people, species, habitats, pollution, and natural resources.

Trace the cause and effect. Ask what each action changes, who or what may be harmed, and whether the harm can be avoided or reduced.

Check legal limits too. A choice that violates a protection rule is not a good option even if it is cheap or convenient.

A **tradeoff** happens when one choice gives a benefit but also has a cost. A strong answer explains both sides and chooses the option that meets the goal with the least avoidable environmental harm.

## Examples

### #1: Choosing a Species Conservation Action

A threatened native bird depends on one remaining forest patch for nesting. A proposed viewing trail can either cross the nesting area or follow a slightly longer route around it. Which action better protects the species while still allowing the trail?

**Solution:**

1. The conservation goal is to protect the bird population and the habitat it needs.
2. Crossing the nesting area adds direct pressure to the place the bird depends on.
3. The longer route still allows the trail but avoids the nesting area.
4. Therefore, the better choice is to route the trail around the nesting area.

### #2: Protecting a Renewable Resource

A town is using water faster than its local supply is recovering during a dry period. Which plan best supports resource conservation: repair major leaks and reduce avoidable use, or continue the same use and look for more water only after the supply becomes critical?

**Solution:**

1. Water is the resource being placed under pressure.
2. Repairing leaks and reducing avoidable use lowers demand now.
3. Continuing the same use does not address the cause of the shortage.
4. Therefore, reducing waste and avoidable use is the better conservation action.

### #3: Explaining an Ecosystem Impact

A riverside area loses much of its plant cover. During heavy rain, more loose soil is washed into the river. How can this human-caused change affect the ecosystem?

**Solution:**

1. Removing plant cover leaves more soil exposed to rain.
2. More soil can then enter the river as runoff.
3. The added sediment changes the river environment and can make conditions harder for organisms that depend on it.
4. The impact begins with habitat change and continues through a physical change in the water.

### #4: Applying RA $9147$ to a Threatened Species

A threatened species is found in an important habitat outside an existing NIPAS protected area. Scientific data show that human pressure in the area threatens the species. Which conservation tool under RA $9147$ directly fits this situation?

**Solution:**

1. The species is threatened and its important habitat is outside an existing protected area.
2. RA $9147$ provides for critical habitats outside protected areas where threatened species are found.
3. The designation uses scientific data and considers threats to the species.
4. Therefore, critical-habitat designation is the direct legal conservation tool described by RA $9147$.

### #5: Comparing Environmental Alternatives

A local government needs a new access road. Route A is shorter and cheaper but would cut through an intact wildlife habitat.

Route B costs more but follows an already cleared corridor and still provides the needed access. If the two routes are otherwise workable, which option is better supported by environmental decision-making?

**Solution:**

1. Both routes meet the access goal.
2. Route A has the lower cost, but it causes more new habitat loss.
3. Route B has a higher cost, but it uses an area that is already cleared.
4. The tradeoff is cost against added habitat damage.
5. If no other major factor changes the result, Route B is better because it meets the goal with less new environmental harm.

### #6: Managing a Protected Area

A NIPAS protected area has a damaged habitat beside a heavily used zone. The management team is choosing between ignoring the damage or using the management plan to zone activities and restore the habitat. Which choice better fits protected-area management?

**Solution:**

1. A protected-area management plan guides long-term protection and use.
2. Zoning can separate activities that need different levels of protection.
3. Habitat rehabilitation can repair a damaged area.
4. Therefore, using the management plan to zone activities and restore the habitat better fits NIPAS management.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Protecting a Species Without Protecting Its Habitat

> Saving individual animals is not enough when the habitat they need is still being damaged.

If the main threat is habitat loss, an answer that protects or restores habitat usually addresses the cause more directly than an action that helps only a few individual animals.

### #2: Treating Critical Habitat and Protected Area as the Same Thing

> A critical habitat under RA $9147$ does not have to be inside an existing NIPAS protected area.

RA $9147$ specifically provides for critical habitats outside protected areas where threatened species are found. Do not reject an answer only because the site is outside NIPAS.

### #3: Assuming Conservation Means No Use at All

> Resource conservation is not the same as banning every use of a resource.

Some resources can be used while they are protected and allowed to recover. The correct action depends on the resource, the level of pressure, and the law that applies.

### #4: Choosing the Cheapest Option Automatically

> Low cost is one factor, not the whole environmental decision.

A cheaper choice may cause greater pollution, habitat loss, or resource damage. Compare the full effects and explain the tradeoff before choosing.
