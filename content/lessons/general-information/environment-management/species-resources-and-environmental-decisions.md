## Core Concept

### Species, Habitats, and Ecosystems

A **species** is a group of living things of the same kind. A **habitat** is where a living thing gets food, water, shelter, and space. A breeding place is where it produces its young.

An **ecosystem** includes living things and nonliving parts around them, such as soil, water, and air. These parts affect one another. Changing water quality can change which plants and animals can live there.

Clearing a forest removes food, shelter, and breeding places. It can also separate wildlife groups that once shared a connected habitat. A species may decline even if people do not hunt it.

Plant roots help hold soil in place. Removing plants can let more soil wash into rivers during rain. This soil, called **sediment**, can cloud water, block light, and cover feeding or breeding places.

**Pollution** adds harmful substances or waste to the environment. It can make air, water, or soil unsafe for living things. Taking wildlife faster than it can reproduce can also reduce its population.

### Protecting Species

**Species conservation** means helping wild plants and animals survive and recover. A **threatened species** faces a risk of disappearing. Choose an action that addresses the threat described.

If habitat loss is the problem, protect or restore the habitat. If harmful collection or trade reduces the population, control that activity under the law. Reducing pollution helps when harmful waste threatens the species.

Republic Act (RA) $9147$, the Wildlife Resources Conservation and Protection Act, protects wildlife and habitats. It provides for **critical habitats** outside existing protected areas where threatened species are found. These places receive legal protection to help wildlife survive.

Scientific evidence guides the choice of these sites. Officials consider the species present and human activities that threaten them. Protection must prevent harmful use or damage that threatens the species there.

Section $24$ of RA $9147$ encourages **conservation breeding or propagation** of threatened species to increase their populations in natural habitats. This means producing more animals or plants with human help. The same section calls for protecting or repairing the habitat where they will be released.

Breeding is not permission for anyone to collect or release wildlife. Legal approval and scientific checks still apply. Breeding alone cannot replace a safe place to live.

### Protecting Natural Resources

A **natural resource** is something from nature that people use. Water, forests, soil, minerals, and wildlife are examples. **Resource conservation** means using and caring for resources so they are not wasted or damaged.

A **renewable resource** can be replaced through natural processes, given time and suitable conditions. It can still run short if people use it too quickly. Minerals do not return on a human time scale, so reducing waste and reusing materials help conserve them.

Match the action to the resource. Repairing leaks saves water. Protecting plant cover conserves soil, while harvest limits can give forests or fish populations time to recover.

Conservation does not always mean banning all use. The amount and kind of use must fit the resource and the law. Some species and places need strict protection.

### Protected Areas

RA $7586$, as amended by RA $11038$, provides the **National Integrated Protected Areas System (NIPAS)**. It organizes the protection and long-term care of important natural areas.

Its categories include strict nature reserves, natural parks, natural monuments, and wildlife sanctuaries. They also include protected landscapes and seascapes, resource reserves, and natural biotic areas. The categories reflect different features and protection needs.

A natural monument protects a notable natural feature. A wildlife sanctuary protects conditions wildlife needs. A protected landscape or seascape also recognizes how people and the land or sea have shaped one another over time.

A protected area's management plan guides its care. **Zoning** divides an area into parts with different activity rules. A **buffer zone** is outside and beside a protected area and needs special care to help prevent harm to it.

**Habitat rehabilitation** means repairing a damaged habitat so it can support living things again. Plans can combine zoning, buffer zones, protection, and rehabilitation. Activities must follow the area's rules and required permits.

A critical habitat under RA $9147$ and a NIPAS protected area are different legal tools. A place outside NIPAS can still receive critical-habitat protection. Being outside a protected area does not make harmful wildlife use lawful.

### Choosing Among Environmental Options

**Evaluate** means judge choices using evidence and reasons. Start with the goal, such as providing a road or protecting water supplies. Check whether each option can meet it.

Compare effects on people, species, habitats, pollution, and resources. Trace how each action changes the environment and may cause harm. Check immediate effects and those that may last.

A **tradeoff** is a benefit gained with a cost or disadvantage. A longer road may cost more but avoid clearing wildlife habitat. State both the added cost and the avoided harm.

Legal limits still apply. A cheap option is unacceptable if it breaks a protection rule. Among lawful, workable choices, prefer one that meets the goal with less avoidable harm.

More evidence may be needed before deciding. A road beside a river needs checks on runoff and bank damage. Words such as “green” or “natural” do not prove that an option is safe.

## Examples

### #1: Protecting a Nesting Area

A threatened native bird nests in a forest patch. A viewing trail can cross the nesting area or take a slightly longer route around it. Both routes are safe and lawful. Which better protects the bird while allowing visitors?

**Solution:**

1. The bird needs the habitat to reproduce.
2. A trail through the nesting site adds disturbance there.
3. Routing the trail around it allows access with less direct pressure on the nesting area.

### #2: Conserving a Water Supply

During a dry period, a town uses water faster than its supply recovers. Should it repair major leaks and reduce avoidable use now, or wait until the supply becomes critical?

**Solution:**

1. Water use is greater than replacement.
2. Repairs and less waste lower demand now.
3. Acting now addresses the pressure before it becomes more severe.

### #3: Explaining a River Impact

A riverside area loses its plant cover. Heavy rain then washes more loose soil into the river. How can the clearing harm the ecosystem?

**Solution:**

1. Removing plants and roots leaves more soil exposed to washing away.
2. Sediment enters the river and clouds the water.
3. Less light reaches underwater plants, and sediment can cover feeding or breeding places.
4. Clearing land can therefore harm living things in the river.

### #4: Applying Critical-Habitat Protection

Studies find a threatened species in an important habitat outside an existing NIPAS protected area. Human activities threaten its survival there. Which conservation tool under RA $9147$ directly fits this case?

**Solution:**

1. The threatened species needs habitat protection outside an existing protected area.
2. RA $9147$ provides for critical habitats in such places, based on scientific evidence.
3. Critical-habitat designation supports protection against harmful use or damage that threatens the species.

### #5: Linking Breeding with Habitat Protection

An approved program breeds a threatened animal for release. Its planned release site is still losing food plants and shelter. Is breeding alone enough to support recovery there?

**Solution:**

1. Released animals need food, shelter, and places to reproduce.
2. Continuing habitat damage leaves those needs unmet.
3. Breeding must go with habitat protection or rehabilitation, as Section $24$ of RA $9147$ provides.

### #6: Comparing Road Routes

A local government needs an access road. Route A is cheaper but clears intact wildlife habitat. Route B costs more and follows an already cleared corridor. Studies show both are lawful, safe, and provide the same access, with no other major difference in environmental effects. Which better supports conservation?

**Solution:**

1. Both routes meet the access goal.
2. Route A saves money but adds habitat loss.
3. Route B costs more but avoids that new clearing.
4. The tradeoff is added cost against added habitat damage.
5. Route B better supports conservation under the stated conditions.

### #7: Managing a Protected Area

A NIPAS wildlife sanctuary has damaged breeding habitat beside a visitor zone. Its team can leave the area as it is or limit access near the breeding site and restore native plants under its approved plan. Which better fits the area's purpose?

**Solution:**

1. A wildlife sanctuary protects conditions wildlife needs.
2. Limiting access near the breeding site reduces disturbance.
3. Restoring native plants helps repair the habitat.
4. Combining these actions under the plan better supports the sanctuary's purpose.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Saving Animals While Losing Habitat

> Protect the place the species needs as well as the species itself.

Breeding or rescuing a few animals will not solve continuing habitat loss. Address the main threat described.

### #2: Giving All Protected Places One Legal Label

> Critical habitats and NIPAS protected areas are different tools.

RA $9147$ provides for critical habitats outside existing protected areas. Do not reject protection simply because a site is outside NIPAS.

### #3: Treating Renewable as Unlimited

> A renewable resource needs time and suitable conditions to recover.

A forest, fish population, or water supply can be used faster than it recovers. Check the rate of use and the pressure described.

### #4: Choosing by Price Alone

> Cost is only part of the decision.

Compare legal limits, resource loss, pollution, and lasting damage. A choice that costs less at first may cause more harm later.

### #5: Ignoring the Question's Conditions

> A good option must be lawful and workable in the stated place.

An already cleared route may reduce habitat loss, but other risks still need checking. Use the evidence given before choosing.
