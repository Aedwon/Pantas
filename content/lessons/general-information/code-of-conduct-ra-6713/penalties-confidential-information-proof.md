## Core Concept

A **criminal case** asks a court to decide guilt. **Prosecution** means bringing and pursuing that case.

### Section $7(c)$ Protected Information and Purpose

Under Section $7(c)$, workers must not disclose or misuse **confidential** or **classified** office information not yet made public.

Check two elements together:

1. Protected status: the information was confidential or classified and not publicly released.
2. Prohibited purpose: the disclosure aimed to further private interests, give undue advantage, or harm public interest.

Section $7$ carries criminal penalties under Section $11(a)$. Proven crimes carry up to $5$ years in prison, ₱$5{,}000$ fine, or both.

Compare two emails sent by a procurement clerk:

1. Sending a posted agency fee list to a caller: public data, so Section $7(c)$ does not apply.
2. Texting unannounced inspection scores to help a supplier pass early: confidential plus undue advantage.

Result: Sharing public files is lawful, whereas leaking confidential scores violates Section $7(c)$.

:::pair #section-seven-covers-confidential-data
Right: Misusing confidential work information violates Section $7(c)$ and triggers Section $11(a)$ criminal penalties.
Wrong: Treating confidential-information misuse as a Section $3$ violation outside Section $7$'s criminal penalty.
Section $3$ defines terms, whereas Section $7(c)$ bars confidential-information misuse with criminal penalties.
:::

:::check
A customs clerk forwards a published tariff table to an importer. A colleague leaks sealed bid estimates to a relative's firm before bidding closes. Which act meets Section $7(c)$?
:::answer
The answer is **Only the sealed bid-estimate leak**. The tariff table is public, whereas sealed estimates are confidential and shared for undue advantage.
:::

### Criminal Proof Versus Administrative Proof

Different cases require different proof levels under **Rule $133$** of the Rules of Court.

Under Rule $133$, Section $2$, criminal conviction needs **proof beyond reasonable doubt**. The evidence must leave no reasonable doubt of guilt in a fair judge's mind.

Under Rule $133$, Section $6$, administrative findings need **substantial evidence**. That is relevant evidence a reasonable person would accept as enough.

Apply both standards when an officer's login sends sealed route maps at midnight, and a co-worker shared that password:

1. Administrative hearing: access logs and replies satisfy substantial evidence for discipline.
2. Criminal trial: shared password access leaves reasonable doubt about who sent them, forcing acquittal.

Result: The officer is acquitted in court while administrative discipline stands.

:::why
Why demand proof beyond reasonable doubt in court while agencies use substantial evidence? Criminal trials can take away liberty through prison, whereas administrative discipline governs fitness for office.
:::

:::pair #substantial-evidence-cannot-convict
Right: Criminal conviction needs proof beyond reasonable doubt, so substantial evidence alone cannot convict.
Wrong: Requiring a criminal court to convict once an administrative body accepts substantial evidence.
A higher criminal proof threshold protects the accused even after an agency finds substantial evidence.
:::

:::pair #acquittal-does-not-cancel-discipline
Right: Criminal acquittal on reasonable doubt leaves an independent administrative finding intact.
Wrong: Treating reasonable doubt in a criminal case as automatically canceling the separate administrative finding.
Criminal acquittal means guilt lacked proof beyond reasonable doubt, which never erases substantial evidence.
:::

:::check
An agency dismisses a land examiner on substantial evidence for altering survey files. Later, a court acquits because another clerk held a desk key. Does acquittal reinstate the examiner?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Acquittal for reasonable doubt never cancels an independent administrative dismissal backed by substantial evidence.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Public Ordinance Versus Sealed Audit

A city clerk emails a posted ordinance PDF to a civic group. Later, a colleague sends an unapproved internal fraud audit to an investigated contractor. Which act violates Section $7(c)$?

**Solution:**

1. Check the ordinance: it was publicly released, lacking confidential status.
2. Check the audit: it is confidential office data sent to give undue advantage.

Answer: Only sending the unapproved internal fraud audit violates Section $7(c)$.

**Check:** Require both confidential status and a prohibited purpose for Section $7(c)$.

### #2: Substantial Evidence Cannot Bind a Criminal Court

An agency board finds substantial evidence that a clerk leaked sealed bid estimates to a supplier and dismisses him. The board then demands a Section $7(c)$ criminal conviction without extra proof. Must the criminal court convict?

**Solution:**

1. Administrative standard: substantial evidence under Rule $133$, Section $6$ supports dismissal after notice and hearing.
2. Criminal standard: Rule $133$, Section $2$ requires proof beyond reasonable doubt, so substantial evidence alone cannot convict.

Answer: No, the criminal court must acquit unless the evidence removes reasonable doubt.

**Check:** Match substantial evidence to agency discipline and proof beyond reasonable doubt to criminal conviction.

### #3: Disputed Intent in Dual Proceedings

A mining officer attaches an unredacted inspection memo in an email to a trade reporter, claiming a clerical attachment mistake. After notice and hearing, the board removes the officer on substantial evidence of careless disclosure. The criminal court retains reasonable doubt on intent to give undue advantage. Rule on both cases.

**Solution:**

1. Check Section $7(c)$ elements: confirm the memo was non-public and check disputed intent under prohibited purposes.
2. Check proof standards: compare Rule $133$, Section $6$ removal against Rule $133$, Section $2$ trial proof.

:::try
Finish the last step: state the criminal verdict, the effect on administrative removal, and whether Section $7$ carries criminal penalties.
:::answer
The answer is **Acquittal in court, removal stands, and Section $7$ carries criminal penalties**. Reasonable doubt forces acquittal without canceling substantial-evidence removal. Section $7$ crimes carry up to $5$ years prison, ₱$5{,}000$ fine, or both.

**Check:** Separate the two Rule $133$ proof standards while keeping Section $7$ in the criminal list.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Confidential-information section: place confidential-information misuse under Section $7(c)$, not Section $3$. {#section-seven-covers-confidential-data}
- Criminal conviction threshold: require proof beyond reasonable doubt in court instead of administrative substantial evidence. {#substantial-evidence-cannot-convict}
- Acquittal vs. discipline: keep an independent substantial-evidence finding intact after criminal acquittal on reasonable doubt. {#acquittal-does-not-cancel-discipline}
:::
