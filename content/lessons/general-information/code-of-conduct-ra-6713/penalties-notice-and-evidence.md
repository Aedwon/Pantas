## Core Concept

### Notice, Answer, and Governing Rules

An administrative case begins when an authority checks a complaint and supporting facts. If a charge proceeds, the worker receives **due notice** stating the accusation.

The worker then gets a fair chance to answer with records and evidence. A formal investigation follows when the governing rules require one.

The **handling authority's rules** govern the case, such as Civil Service Commission or Ombudsman rules under Section $12$. Written submissions can give a fair hearing without full criminal courtroom steps.

Follow the steps when a regional examiner receives a gift complaint:

1. Check the charge: the agency reviews initial facts and sends written notice of the accusation.
2. Check the reply: under Civil Service rules, the examiner submits receipts and a sworn answer before any penalty.

Result: The handling authority's rules protect notice and a fair reply without requiring a criminal trial.

:::why
Why do administrative cases allow written answers instead of criminal courtroom trials? Agencies decide work-rule discipline faster when records and sworn replies answer the charge fairly.
:::

:::pair #handling-authority-rules
Right: Follow the handling authority's administrative rules, which need not mirror criminal trial steps.
Wrong: Applying criminal trial rules in every administrative discipline case under RA $6713$.
Administrative cases follow the handling body's lawful procedure rather than criminal courtroom rules.
:::

:::pair #notice-before-dismissal
Right: Give the worker written notice of the charge and a chance to answer before dismissal.
Wrong: Dismissing the official before giving notice of the accusation.
Section $11(a)$ requires due notice and hearing before any removal takes effect.
:::

:::check
A complainant demands that an agency hear a Code case under private corporate arbitration rules. Which procedural rules actually govern?
:::answer
The answer is **The handling authority's rules**. Administrative cases follow the agency, Civil Service Commission, or Ombudsman rules, never private arbitration codes.
:::

### Substantial Evidence Under Rule $133$

**Level of proof** means how strong the evidence must be to support a finding.

Under Rule $133$, Section $6$ of the Rules of Court, an administrative finding requires **substantial evidence**. That is relevant evidence a reasonable person would accept as enough to support the conclusion.

An unsupported accusation never meets this standard. The deciding body must connect real evidence to the charged acts and explain its findings.

Compare two records in a conflict review:

1. An unsigned note says a clerk favored a cousin, with zero dates or files attached.
2. Signed permit logs and payroll records show the clerk approved a cousin's firm.

Result: Only the signed logs and records can meet the substantial-evidence test after notice and hearing.

:::pair #complaint-not-proof
Right: Treat a filed complaint as an unproved accusation that must be proved in a proper proceeding.
Wrong: Treating any filed complaint as automatically proving an ethics code violation without examining evidence.
A complaint opens the review, whereas Rule $133$ requires substantial evidence connected to the acts charged.
:::

:::check
A citizen files a sworn complaint accusing a treasurer of taking a bribe, with no receipts, logs, or witnesses. The treasurer denies it. Can the complaint alone justify dismissal?
:::answer
The answer is **No.** A complaint is only an allegation, so dismissal requires substantial evidence proved after due notice and hearing.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Summary Suspension Without Notice

A department head reads a walk-in complaint accusing a cashier of rudeness and favoritism. The head suspends the cashier immediately without stating the charge or asking for a reply. Has the head met Section $11(a)$?

**Solution:**

1. Check the notice rule: Section $11(a)$ requires due notice stating the accusation before any penalty.
2. Check the hearing rule: the cashier never received a fair chance to offer records or an answer.

Answer: No, the suspension fails due notice and hearing.

**Check:** Verify both notice of the charge and a chance to reply before imposing any sanction.

### #2: Demanding Criminal Courtroom Procedure

A clerk faces a Civil Service administrative charge for delayed service. The clerk demands full criminal trial cross-examination before answering. Meanwhile, the complainant insists on using the complainant's own hearing timeline. Who sets the procedure?

**Solution:**

1. Reject private timelines: a complainant starts the review but never chooses the governing rules.
2. Apply administrative rules: the Civil Service Commission handles the case under its own administrative rules, including written submissions.

Answer: The handling authority's administrative rules govern, not criminal trial rules or the complainant's preference.

**Check:** Match administrative discipline to the handling authority's rules rather than courtroom or private rules.

### #3: Anonymous Tip Versus Connected Records

An agency receives a complaint claiming a supply officer steered contracts to a relative's company. Investigators gather signed award sheets and corporate registry pages naming the officer's spouse as owner. The officer receives written notice and files a sworn reply. Check the process and proof.

**Solution:**

1. Check fair process: inspect whether the officer received notice of the charge and a fair chance to answer.
2. Check the evidence: compare the bare complaint against the signed award sheets and registry records under Rule $133$, Section $6$.

:::try
Finish the last step: state whether the agency met notice requirements and whether the records can meet substantial evidence.
:::answer
The answer is **Yes to both**. Notice and reply were given. Signed award sheets plus registry records give evidence a reasonable person can accept.

**Check:** Separate an unproved complaint from connected records evaluated after notice and reply.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Governing procedure: follow the handling authority's rules instead of criminal trial rules in administrative cases. {#handling-authority-rules}
- Notice before penalty: give written notice and a chance to answer before any dismissal. {#notice-before-dismissal}
- Complaint vs. proof: prove the violation with substantial evidence instead of treating a complaint as proof. {#complaint-not-proof}
:::
