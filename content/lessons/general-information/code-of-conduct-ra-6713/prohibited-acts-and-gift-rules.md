## Core Concept

Section $7$ of RA $6713$ lists acts public officials and employees must avoid. These rules keep private gain from affecting public duties. Check how the act connects to the worker's own office.

### Financial or Material Interest

A **financial or material interest** is a money or property stake. It lets someone gain or lose money or property from a transaction. A worker must not hold such a stake in a transaction needing the worker's office to approve it.

The interest can be direct, such as personally owning shares. It can be indirect, such as holding the interest through another person. A private investment alone does not show that an office-approved transaction is involved.

### Outside Work in a Private Business

A worker must not own, control, or manage a private business regulated, supervised, or licensed by the worker's own office. The ban also covers jobs and roles listed below. It applies unless a law expressly allows the arrangement.

**Regulates or supervises** means the office has legal power to enforce rules for the business or oversee its work. **Licenses** means it gives permission the business needs to operate. The link to the worker's own office matters.

An **officer** helps lead the business. An **employee** works for it. A **consultant** gives expert advice, while **counsel** gives legal advice or represents the business as a lawyer.

A **broker** arranges deals. An **agent** acts for another person or business. A **trustee** holds or manages property for someone else, while a **nominee** holds a named role or interest for someone else.

All these roles are covered. Express permission means a law clearly allows the arrangement. A supervisor's verbal approval alone does not meet this exception.

### Private Professional Practice

**Private professional practice** means serving private clients in a profession, such as law or medicine. During public service, the Act requires authority under the Constitution or law. The implementing rules also refer to authority under regulation.

**Implementing rules** explain how to carry out a law. Private work must also not conflict, or tend to conflict, with public duties. Permission alone is not enough.

A **conflict** occurs when private work pulls against public duty. A government lawyer cannot rely only on permission if a client's matter opposes the lawyer's public work.

### Recommendations and Leaving Office

A worker generally must not recommend someone for a job in a private business with regular or pending official dealings with the worker's office. Regular dealings are ongoing. A pending transaction still awaits action or completion.

The implementing rules allow referrals required by law, international agreements or commitments, or assigned public duties. Using office influence to get a friend a job does not meet that exception by itself.

The outside-business and recommendation bans continue for $1$ year after leaving office. **Separation** means leaving government service through resignation, retirement, or another kind of departure. These bans do not end at once.

Private practice has a different limit. For $1$ year after leaving, the former worker cannot practice on a matter before the former office. This does not ban every professional job everywhere during that year.

### Protected Work Information

**Confidential or classified information** is protected work information not made public. A worker must not use or share it for private gain, an unfair advantage, or harm to the public interest.

The ban covers using information as well as sharing it. Check that it came through office and was not made public. Information already lawfully made public is different.

### Gifts and Valuable Benefits

A worker must not ask for or accept a gift, favor, entertainment, loan, or other valuable benefit tied to public duties. The rule also covers benefits linked to a business the office oversees or a transaction it may affect. Receiving a benefit through another person is covered too.

**Solicitation** means asking for a benefit. Asking can break the rule even when nothing is delivered. Calling the benefit a token does not make a trade for special treatment proper.

### Separate Gift Exceptions

Each exception has its own conditions. First check the giver and purpose. Then check every condition of that particular exception.

An **unsolicited nominal gift** was not requested and has small or insignificant value. It must not be given for an expected favor or in exchange for one. Giving it after service does not make a reward for an official favor proper.

There is no single peso cutoff in this exception. Circumstances include the worker's salary, how often gifts are given, and the expected benefit. Low price alone is not enough.

A family member's or relative's gift at a family celebration has a separate exception. No **pecuniary gain or benefit**, meaning money or another financial benefit, may be expected. A parent's birthday gift differs from a gift seeking government business.

The Code defines family as the spouse and unmarried children under $18$. Relatives include people within the fourth civil degree by blood or marriage. The Code expressly includes **bilas**, **inso**, and **balae**.

Bilas means a brother-in-law's wife or a sister-in-law's husband. Inso means the wife of an elder brother or male cousin. Balae means the parent of one's son-in-law or daughter-in-law.

A civil degree counts steps in a family relationship. For blood relatives, count each birth up to the shared ancestor and then down. Parents are in the first degree, siblings in the second, aunts and uncles in the third, and first cousins in the fourth.

Marriage connects a person to the spouse's blood relatives in the corresponding degrees. The named relationships above are expressly included too. A family friend is not a relative merely because the friendship is close.

A nominal donation from a person has a different test. The donor must have no regular, pending, or expected transaction with the department, office, or agency. No money or financial benefit may be expected.

Another exception covers donations from local or foreign private organizations. These must be considered and accepted as **humanitarian and altruistic** in purpose and mission. Humanitarian means helping people in need, while altruistic means helping without seeking personal gain.

Check who receives the aid and how it is used. Food for flood victims can meet that purpose. A personal reward for an approval does not become relief aid because of its label.

This organization-donation exception does not state the no-office-transaction condition used for nominal donations from persons. A donor's pending license does not alone settle the question. The donation must meet its own purpose and acceptance conditions, and office decisions must remain fair.

Donations from one government entity to another are also listed. A personal gift to an official does not automatically fit this exception.

### Benefits From Foreign Governments

A worker may accept and keep a foreign-government gift of nominal value received as a souvenir or courtesy. This exception requires nominal value.

Foreign-government scholarships, fellowship grants, and medical treatment are separately listed. A **fellowship** is a grant for study, training, or research. These types are separate from courtesy souvenirs.

Foreign-government travel must take place entirely outside the Philippines. Acceptance must be appropriate or consistent with Philippine interests. The head of the person's office, branch, or agency must permit it.

Travel benefits can include allowances, transportation, food, and lodging worth more than a nominal amount. Reporting or disclosure requirements may still apply. Educational, scientific, and cultural exchanges remain subject to national-security requirements.

## Examples

### #1: Shares and Office Approval

An employee owns shares in a company whose transaction needs approval from the employee's office. Does the financial-interest ban apply?

**Solution:**

1. Shares give the employee a money stake.
2. The transaction needs office approval. The ban applies to this direct interest.

### #2: Consultant for a Licensed Company

An agency employee accepts a consultant job with a company licensed by that agency. No law expressly permits it. Is the job allowed?

**Solution:**

1. Consultant is a covered role, and the employee's own agency licenses the company.
2. No law clearly permits it. The employee may not accept the job.

### #3: Permission in a Law

A public employee takes a covered role in a business supervised by the employee's office. A law expressly allows that exact arrangement. Does this outside-work ban itself bar the role?

**Solution:**

1. The role and office connection normally fall under the ban.
2. Express permission in law meets this rule's exception.

### #4: Conflicting Private Practice

A government lawyer has legal authority to take private clients. A proposed client's matter directly conflicts with public duties. May the lawyer accept it?

**Solution:**

1. Legal authority is only one condition.
2. The conflict fails the other condition. The lawyer may not accept the matter.

### #5: Work After Leaving

An official left government $6$ months ago. The person wants to consult for a company regulated by the former office and handle a client's matter before that office. No legal exception applies. Are these activities allowed?

**Solution:**

1. The outside-business ban continues for $1$ year. It still covers the consultant job.
2. The separate practice rule bars the matter before the former office during that year.
3. Neither activity is allowed on these facts. Other professional work is not automatically banned.

### #6: Required Referral

An officer's assigned duties require referrals of qualified job seekers. One referral goes to a business with a pending agency transaction. Is it automatically prohibited?

**Solution:**

1. The pending transaction brings the general recommendation rule into view.
2. A referral required by assigned duties has an exception. The pending transaction alone does not defeat it.

### #7: Recommendation After Retirement

An officer retired $4$ months ago and recommends a friend to a business with a pending transaction before the former office. No law, agreement, or assigned duty requires the referral. Does retirement end the ban?

**Solution:**

1. The recommendation ban continues for $1$ year. Only $4$ months have passed.
2. The pending transaction and lack of an exception make the recommendation prohibited during that period.

### #8: Protected Information

An employee gives a friend confidential work information not made public to gain an unfair advantage in a private deal. Is this allowed?

**Solution:**

1. The information is protected and came through work.
2. Sharing it for an unfair private advantage violates the rule.

### #9: Asking Without Receiving

An employee asks a business regulated by the agency for an expensive personal item. The business refuses. Does refusal make the request proper?

**Solution:**

1. The request seeks a valuable benefit from a regulated business.
2. Asking is covered even without delivery. Refusal does not make it proper.

### #10: Small Gift Without a Favor

A visitor gives a worker a low-value souvenir without being asked. Its value is insignificant in the circumstances. No official favor is expected or being rewarded. Which exception fits?

**Solution:**

1. The gift is unsolicited and nominal.
2. No favor is expected or exchanged. It fits that gift exception.

### #11: Cheap Gift for Priority

A permit applicant gives an employee a cheap souvenir for priority over other applicants. Does low value establish an exception?

**Solution:**

1. The gift is exchanged for special treatment.
2. It fails the no-favor condition despite its low price.

### #12: Family Celebration

A parent gives an employee a birthday gift at a family celebration. No money, financial benefit, or official favor is expected. Which exception fits?

**Solution:**

1. A parent is a relative, and the gift is for a family celebration.
2. No financial benefit is expected. It fits the family-celebration exception.

### #13: Different Donation Conditions

Two people offer nominal donations without expected financial benefit. The first has no regular, pending, or expected office transaction. The second has a pending transaction. Do both meet the nominal-donation exception?

**Solution:**

1. The first meets the stated transaction and financial-benefit conditions.
2. The second fails the no-office-transaction condition. Small value does not remove it.

### #14: Relief With Office Dealings

An organization awaiting license renewal offers food through the agency's flood-relief program. The donation is reviewed and accepted for that mission. All food goes to victims, with no personal benefit or request for favorable license action.

Does the renewal alone defeat the organization-donation exception?

**Solution:**

1. The food helps people in need without a sought private gain.
2. It is considered and accepted for that mission. These facts support the humanitarian and altruistic exception.
3. This exception does not state the no-office-transaction condition for nominal donations from persons. The renewal alone does not defeat it.
4. The license decision must still be fair. The donation does not buy approval.

### #15: A Donation Label

An organization awaiting approval calls its offer humanitarian aid. It actually offers the deciding officer a personal vacation for approval. Does the label establish an exception?

**Solution:**

1. The offer rewards the officer personally for official action.
2. It does not meet the helping purpose. The donation label does not establish the exception.

### #16: Equipment Transfer

A foreign public agency donates equipment directly to a Philippine government agency. Which listed donation exception fits the transfer?

**Solution:**
The government-to-government exception fits because the donor and recipient are government entities.

### #17: Foreign Courtesy Souvenir

A foreign government gives an official a nominal souvenir as a courtesy during an official visit. May the official accept and keep it under the stated exception?

**Solution:**

1. The giver is a foreign government, and the gift is nominal.
2. Its courtesy purpose meets the souvenir exception.

### #18: Study and Treatment

A foreign government offers a worker a scholarship. In separate cases it offers a fellowship grant and medical treatment. Are these benefit types listed exceptions?

**Solution:**

1. Foreign-government scholarships and fellowship grants are listed.
2. Medical treatment is separately listed too. Each stated type is included.

### #19: Foreign Travel With Permission

A foreign government offers travel, food, and lodging for an official. The trip is entirely outside the Philippines. Acceptance fits Philippine interests, and the agency head permits it. Are the travel conditions met?

**Solution:**

1. The whole trip is outside the Philippines.
2. Acceptance fits Philippine interests and has the required permission.
3. The stated conditions are met. This exception can cover benefits above nominal value.

### #20: Missing Permission

A foreign government offers travel entirely outside the Philippines. Acceptance fits Philippine interests, but office-head permission is missing. May the official rely on the travel exception now?

**Solution:**

1. Location and Philippine interests meet only part of the test.
2. Required permission is missing. The official cannot rely on the exception on these facts.

### #21: A Named Relative

At a family celebration, an employee receives a gift from the parent of the employee's daughter-in-law. No money, financial benefit, or official favor is expected. Does the lack of a blood relationship exclude this gift?

**Solution:**

1. The donor is the employee's balae, expressly included among relatives.
2. The celebration and lack of expected financial benefit meet the stated conditions. Lack of a blood relationship alone does not exclude it.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For gifts, check the giver and purpose first. Then apply that exception's conditions. Do not move conditions from one exception into another.

Nominal gifts need the no-favor test. Nominal donations from persons add the no-office-transaction test. Organization donations need their own genuine helping purpose and acceptance for that mission.

For foreign travel, check location, Philippine interests, and permission together. After a worker leaves office, identify the activity before applying the $1$-year rule.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Every Small Gift Is Allowed

> Low value does not cancel an expected favor.

A cheap gift for priority fails the no-favor condition. Check purpose as well as price.

### #2: Believing the Label

> Relief aid must serve its stated helping purpose.

Check who receives the benefit and what the donor seeks. A personal vacation for approval does not help disaster victims.

### #3: Mixing Donation Exceptions

> Each exception keeps its own conditions.

Nominal donations require the stated absence of office transactions. Organization donations require their helping purpose and acceptance. Office dealings call for care in checking the real purpose.

### #4: Forgetting Recommendations

> The recommendation ban continues for $1$ year after leaving.

The continuing rule covers more than outside jobs. Private practice has a different limit on matters before the former office.

### #5: Permission Alone

> Private practice also requires no conflict with public duties.

Legal authority does not permit a conflicting matter. Covered outside-business work needs express permission in law.

### #6: Mixing Foreign Benefits

> Souvenirs and travel grants have different tests.

Courtesy souvenirs must be nominal. Travel has location, Philippine-interest, and permission conditions. Scholarships, fellowships, and treatment are separate types.

### #7: Friends and Relatives

> The Code gives family and relatives specific meanings.

Check blood or marriage degrees and the named relationships. Friendship alone does not meet that definition.
