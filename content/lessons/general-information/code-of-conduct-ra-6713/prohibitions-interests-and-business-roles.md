## Core Concept

### Financial or Material Interest

Section $7$ of RA $6713$ bars acts that let private gain interfere with public duty. Always trace how the act connects to the worker's own office.

Section $7(a)$ bans holding any direct or indirect **financial or material interest** in a transaction requiring office approval. That stake lets someone gain or lose money or property from the deal.

Direct stakes include owning company shares yourself. Indirect stakes include holding shares through another person or expecting future job pay tied to the deal. A private investment alone breaks no Section $7(a)$ rule without a transaction needing office approval.

Evaluate an analyst whose sibling holds silent shares for him in a printer vendor bidding on an office-approved contract:

1. Check the stake: holding profits through a sibling is an indirect financial interest.
2. Check approval: the contract needs approval from the analyst's office, so Section $7(a)$ bars the interest.

:::pair #office-approval-interest
Right: Section $7(a)$ bans any direct or indirect money or property stake in a transaction requiring office approval.
Wrong: Focusing on document signatory limits while ignoring a worker's private financial stake.
Trace who gains or loses money from the transaction and whether the worker's office must approve it.
:::

:::check
A city health inspector holds preferred shares in a bakery chain whose sanitary permit renewal awaits her division's approval. Does Section $7(a)$ bar that stake?
:::answer
The answer is **Yes**. Shares give a direct financial interest in a transaction requiring her office's approval.
:::

### Outside Work in a Regulated Business

Section $7(b)(1)$ bars owning, controlling, managing, or taking covered roles in a private enterprise regulated, supervised, or licensed by the worker's office. It applies unless expressly allowed by law. **Regulates or supervises** means enforcing rules or overseeing work, while **licenses** means granting operating permission.

The ban lists $8$ covered roles:

1. **Officer** (leads the firm) or **employee** (works for it).
2. **Consultant** (gives expert advice) or **counsel** (serves as lawyer).
3. **Broker** (arranges deals) or **agent** (acts for another).
4. **Trustee** (holds property for another) or **nominee** (holds a role for another).

Express permission means a clear law allows that role. A supervisor's verbal consent, office custom, or promising not to sign papers never counts.

Review a telecom engineer invited to consult part-time for a radio station licensed by his bureau, with his chief's verbal blessing:

1. Check link and role: consultant is a covered role, and his bureau licenses the station.
2. Check exception: verbal consent is not express permission by law, so Section $7(b)(1)$ bars the role.

:::why
Why require express law instead of supervisor consent? A statute sets a public rule approved by lawmakers, while informal consent or custom invites hidden conflicts.
:::

:::pair #express-law-only
Right: Only express authorization in a law lifts the Section $7(b)(1)$ ban on roles in office-regulated businesses.
Wrong: Treating a supervisor's informal verbal approval as express legal authority under Section $7(b)(1)$.
Check whether a statute expressly allows the role; office customs and verbal assurances never count.
:::

:::check
A maritime examiner agrees to act as unpaid trustee for a shipping firm licensed by his bureau, promising never to handle its files. Is that allowed without a law?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Trustee is a covered role in a bureau-licensed firm, and recusal promises never replace express law.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Private Shares Versus Office-Approved Deals

A revenue officer buys listed supermarket shares with no transaction before her tax district. Separately, her district must approve a tax-credit claim for a factory where she owns shares. Which holding violates Section $7(a)$?

**Solution:**

1. Check supermarket shares: no transaction needs her office's approval, so private share ownership alone is lawful.
2. Check factory shares: the tax-credit claim needs her office's approval, creating a direct interest under Section $7(a)$.

Answer: Only the factory shareholding violates Section $7(a)$.

**Check:** Both a financial stake and a transaction needing office approval must appear together.

### #2: Covered Roles in Supervised Firms

An energy regulator accepts an evening post as corporate secretary and legal counsel for a power distributor supervised by her commission. No law authorizes staff posts there, though her boss orally agreed. Does Section $7(b)(1)$ bar the post?

**Solution:**

1. Check role and link: officer and counsel are covered roles, and her commission supervises the distributor.
2. Check authority: oral approval fails the express-permission-by-law requirement.

Answer: Yes, Section $7(b)(1)$ prohibits the corporate post.

**Check:** Spot any of the $8$ listed roles in a firm regulated, supervised, or licensed by the worker's own office.

### #3: Indirect Gain Versus Statutory Board Seats

An airport planner accepts a supplier's promise of a post-award consulting payout if his authority approves its baggage-scanner bid. Meanwhile, a transport director sits on a ferry operator's board because the ferry charter law expressly assigns an agency seat. How does Section $7$ treat each official?

**Solution:**

1. Check the planner: test whether a promised post-award consulting payout on an authority-approved bid triggers Section $7(a)$.
2. Analyze the board seat: managing a regulated firm triggers Section $7(b)(1)$, so check whether the charter law gives express permission.

:::try
Finish the last step: rule on both the planner and the director under Section $7(a)$ and Section $7(b)(1)$.
:::answer
The answer is **The planner violates Section $7(a)$, while the director meets Section $7(b)(1)$'s express-law exception.** A promised payout tied to an office-approved bid is an indirect financial interest, while a statute expressly allows the board seat.

**Check:** Separate Section $7(a)$ office-approved financial interests from Section $7(b)(1)$ roles permitted expressly by law.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Office-approved financial stake: check for direct or indirect money interests instead of document signatory limits. {#office-approval-interest}
- Regulated business exceptions: require express permission in a law, never informal verbal consent from a supervisor. {#express-law-only}
:::
