## Core Concept

### Criminal Penalties for Sections $7$, $8$, and $9$

Under Section $11(a)$, criminal penalties apply to violations of **Sections $7$, $8$, or $9$**. Those cover prohibited acts, financial disclosures, and divestment.

A court may impose imprisonment up to **$5$ years**, a fixed peso fine up to **₱$5{,}000$**, or **both**. This ₱$5{,}000$ peso fine differs from the $6$-month salary administrative fine and the ₱$25{,}000$ statement-misuse penalty.

The court may also impose **disqualification**, barring the person from public office. That sanction rests on the court's discretion and is never automatic.

Check a court sentence for a buyer convicted under Section $7(a)$ earning ₱$35{,}000$ monthly:

1. Prison ceiling: up to $5$ years.
2. Criminal fine ceiling: up to ₱$5{,}000$, not $6 \times 35{,}000$.
3. Disqualification: the judge decides whether to bar the buyer from office.

Result: The court may impose prison up to $5$ years, ₱$5{,}000$ fine, or both, plus discretionary disqualification.

:::pair #sections-seven-eight-nine-ceilings
Right: Cap the Section $11(a)$ criminal fine for Sections $7$, $8$, or $9$ at ₱$5{,}000$.
Wrong: Using $6$ months' salary as the criminal fine ceiling for a Section $7$ conviction.
Match each limit to its track: salary-based for administrative discipline, and fixed ₱$5{,}000$ for criminal cases.
:::

:::check
A director fails to divest conflicting shares within $60$ days under Section $9$. The court imposes $2$ years in prison and a ₱$4{,}000$ fine, without disqualification. Does that fit Section $11(a)$?
:::answer
The answer is **Yes**. Section $9$ is covered, both sanctions sit below the ceilings, and disqualification is discretionary.
:::

### Heavier-Penalty Laws and Independent Removal

Section $11(a)$ includes a **heavier-penalty rule**. If another law imposes a heavier penalty for the act, prosecution proceeds under that statute.

Section $11(b)$ makes a violation proved in a proper administrative case enough cause for removal or dismissal. No criminal prosecution needs to come first.

A proper administrative proceeding still requires substantial evidence, notice, and a fair hearing.

Apply both rules when a licensing chief's kickback violates RA $6713$ and an anti-graft law carrying $10$ years in prison:

1. Criminal track: prosecute under the anti-graft law because $10$ years exceeds $5$ years.
2. Administrative track: the agency may remove the chief after notice and hearing without awaiting a trial verdict.

Result: The heavier law governs prosecution, while administrative removal proceeds independently.

:::why
Why let administrative removal proceed before any criminal trial finishes? Public offices need prompt removal of unfit workers once an administrative hearing proves the violation.
:::

:::pair #heavier-penalty-statute-governs
Right: Prosecute under the other statute whenever it imposes a heavier penalty than RA $6713$.
Wrong: Using RA $6713$ exclusively even when another statute sets a heavier penalty for the same act.
Compare penalties across applicable laws because Section $11(a)$ yields to the heavier penalty.
:::

:::pair #removal-needs-no-criminal-case
Right: Section $11(b)$ allows administrative removal on a proved violation with no criminal case filed.
Wrong: Requiring a prior criminal conviction in court before an agency can order removal.
Administrative removal stands on proper administrative proof rather than waiting for a criminal conviction.
:::

:::check
A clerk omits property from a SALN, violating Section $8$ and a falsification law carrying $6$ years in prison. Must prosecutors use RA $6713$, and must the agency await trial to dismiss?
:::answer
The answer is **No to both**. Prosecutors use the heavier $6$-year law, and Section $11(b)$ lets dismissal follow proper administrative proof.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Sections $7$, $8$, and $9$ Sentencing Options

A judge convicts a procurement officer under Section $7(d)$. The officer claims a court may impose prison only, never a fine, and must disqualify automatically. Is the officer right?

**Solution:**

1. Check options: Section $11(a)$ allows up to $5$ years imprisonment, up to ₱$5{,}000$ fine, or both.
2. Check disqualification: barring public office lies in the court's discretion, never automatic mandate.

Answer: No, the court may impose imprisonment, a fine, or both, plus discretionary disqualification.

**Check:** Confirm "imprisonment, fine, or both" and "discretion of the court" in Section $11(a)$.

### #2: Comparing Two Statutes on the Same Act

An auditor's act violates Section $7(a)$ and another criminal statute carrying $8$ years in prison and a ₱$40{,}000$ fine. Counsel asks the court to average both penalties. How does Section $11(a)$ resolve this?

**Solution:**

1. Compare ceilings: $8$ years and ₱$40{,}000$ exceed RA $6713$'s $5$-year and ₱$5{,}000$ limits.
2. Apply Section $11(a)$: prosecute under the heavier statute, with no averaging.

Answer: Prosecute under the heavier-penalty statute instead of averaging or capping at RA $6713$.

**Check:** Never average two statutory penalties; apply the heavier statute directly.

### #3: Pending Probe and Administrative Dismissal

An agency proves through notice, hearing, and bank records that an inspector violated Section $7(d)$. No criminal case is filed yet. Separately, a prosecutor checks whether RA $6713$'s criminal fine matches a $6$-month salary fine on a ₱$30{,}000$ monthly salary. Check both points.

**Solution:**

1. Check Section $11(b)$: test whether administrative removal requires an active criminal case after proper administrative proof.
2. Check the fine track: compare the Section $11(a)$ criminal fine ceiling for Section $7$ against the $6$-month salary fine.

:::try
Finish the last step: rule on whether the agency may remove the inspector now and state the criminal fine ceiling.
:::answer
The answer is **Removal is allowed now, and the criminal fine ceiling is ₱$5{,}000$**. Section $11(b)$ needs no criminal case, and Section $7$ criminal fines cap at ₱$5{,}000$.

**Check:** Separate independent removal under Section $11(b)$ from Section $11(a)$'s ₱$5{,}000$ criminal fine.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Criminal vs. administrative fine: cap Sections $7$, $8$, or $9$ criminal fines at ₱$5{,}000$, not $6$ months' salary. {#sections-seven-eight-nine-ceilings}
- Competing laws: prosecute under the statute with the heavier penalty instead of RA $6713$ alone. {#heavier-penalty-statute-governs}
- Removal timing: act on a proved administrative violation without waiting for a criminal conviction. {#removal-needs-no-criminal-case}
:::
