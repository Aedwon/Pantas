## Core Concept

### Financial Stakes in Office Transactions

Under Section $7(a)$ of RA $6713$, a **financial or material interest** is any money or property stake.

Public workers must not hold that stake, directly or **indirectly**, in any transaction requiring the approval of their office.

An **indirect** interest includes shares or profits held through a spouse, relative, or **nominee** (someone holding an interest on another person's behalf).

The ban turns on whether the worker's **office** approves the deal. It applies even if the worker never signs the approval and before any profit is earned.

Test Section $7(a)$ on a procurement clerk whose cousin holds silent shares for her in a gravel vendor bidding at city hall:

1. Check the stake: shares held through a cousin give the clerk an indirect financial interest.
2. Check office approval: city hall, her own office, must approve the gravel bid.

Result: The indirect stake violates Section $7(a)$ even if another official signs the award.

:::pair #indirect-stake-still-barred
Right: Section $7(a)$ bars direct stakes and indirect stakes held through a nominee or another person.
Wrong: Treating a stake held through a nominee as allowed because the worker's name is absent from records.
Section $7(a)$ explicitly covers direct and indirect financial interests.
:::

:::pair #office-approval-covers-non-signers
Right: Section $7(a)$ bars a stake in any office-approved deal even when someone else signs the approval.
Wrong: Thinking a financial stake is allowed when the worker personally skips signing the approval.
The ban follows approval by the worker's office, even without the worker's own signature.
:::

:::check
A budget analyst owns shares in a caterer bidding for her bureau's conference. The director signs the award, and no profit is paid yet. Does Section $7(a)$ bar her stake?
:::answer
The answer is **Yes**. Section $7(a)$ bars direct or indirect stakes in bureau-approved deals without requiring her signature or earned profit.
:::

### Outside Roles in Regulated Firms

Under Section $7(b)(1)$, workers cannot own, control, manage, or work for a private firm **regulated, supervised, or licensed** by their office. Only express law allows an exception.

**Regulated, supervised, or licensed** means the office oversees the firm, enforces its rules, or grants its permit.

Covered roles include **officer**, **employee**, **consultant** (expert adviser), **counsel** (legal adviser), **broker** (deal arranger), **agent**, **trustee** (property manager), and **nominee**.

A supervisor's consent never replaces statutory authority.

Apply Section $7(b)(1)$ to an inspector hired as a **broker** for a shipping line licensed by his agency, holding his chief's memo:

1. Check the firm: his agency licenses the shipping line.
2. Check role and law: broker is covered, and a chief's memo is not express law.

Result: Accepting the broker role violates Section $7(b)(1)$.

:::why
Why bar all listed roles even with a supervisor's nod? Regulated firms could buy influence through retainers or trustee seats approved quietly by a friendly boss.
:::

:::pair #supervisor-consent-not-law
Right: Only express permission in law permits a covered role in an office-regulated, supervised, or licensed firm.
Wrong: Treating a supervisor's approval as valid legal permission for a covered outside business role.
Section $7(b)(1)$ requires "unless expressly allowed by law," which internal approval never replaces.
:::

:::check
A pharmacy inspector is offered an unpaid **trustee** seat at a clinic supervised by her health department. No law allows it. Is the seat allowed?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Section $7(b)(1)$ names trustees, and supervised firms are covered unless a law expressly allows it.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Nominee Shares Without a Signature

A port clerk asks her brother-in-law to hold warehouse shares as her nominee. The warehouse needs a port-board lease approval, though the clerk has no signing power. Does Section $7(a)$ apply?

**Solution:**

1. Check indirect interest: nominee shares held for the clerk remain an indirect stake under Section $7(a)$.
2. Check office approval: her port authority approves the lease, so lacking signing power never clears her.

Answer: Yes, Section $7(a)$ prohibits the clerk's indirect stake in the port lease.

**Check:** Verify whether the stake is direct or indirect and whether the worker's office approves the deal.

### #2: Legal Counsel for a Supervised Carrier

A telecom agency's staff lawyer takes a part-time **counsel** retainer from a phone carrier supervised by the agency. Her director emails approval, though no statute permits it. Is the retainer lawful?

**Solution:**

1. Check firm and role: the agency supervises the carrier, and counsel is a listed role under Section $7(b)(1)$.
2. Check authority: a director's email is not express statutory permission.

Answer: No, the counsel retainer violates Section $7(b)(1)$ because no law expressly permits it.

**Check:** Covered roles include counsel and trustee, and only express law creates an exception.

### #3: Supply Broker With Director Note

A hospital supply chief works as a **broker** for a device firm licensed by his department, holding his hospital director's note. Meanwhile, his wife holds shares for him in a film vendor needing his hospital's approval. Rule on both acts.

**Solution:**

1. Check the broker role: test the licensed firm, the broker role, and the director's note against Section $7(b)(1)$.
2. Check the film shares: test whether shares held by his wife for him violate Section $7(a)$.

:::try
Finish the last step: rule on both the broker role and the film shares under Sections $7(a)$ and $7(b)(1)$.
:::answer
The answer is **Both acts violate Section $7$.** A director's note cannot authorize a broker role in a licensed firm under Section $7(b)(1)$, and indirect shares in a hospital-approved supplier violate Section $7(a)$.

**Check:** Match office-approved stakes to Section $7(a)$ and regulated-firm roles to Section $7(b)(1)$.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Nominee holdings: treat stakes held through another person as barred indirect interests under Section $7(a)$. {#indirect-stake-still-barred}
- Non-signing employees: bar financial stakes whenever the worker's office must approve the deal. {#office-approval-covers-non-signers}
- Internal permission: require express permission in law rather than a supervisor's approval for regulated-firm roles. {#supervisor-consent-not-law}
:::
