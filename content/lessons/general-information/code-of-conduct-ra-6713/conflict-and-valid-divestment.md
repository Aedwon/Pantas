## Core Concept

### Conflict of Interest and Substantial Stockholder

Under Section $3$(i) of RA $6713$, a **conflict of interest** arises when a public official holds a private business link that clashes with official duty. The official must hold one of these roles:

1. A board member, officer, or **substantial stockholder** of a private corporation.
2. An owner or holder of a substantial interest in a business.

A conflict exists whenever faithful official duty may oppose or affect that business interest or the official's rights in it. Actual financial loss need not occur first.

Under Section $3$(f), a **substantial stockholder** owns, **directly or indirectly**, enough shares to elect a corporate director.

Evaluate a procurement director whose family trust holds indirect shares enough to elect a board director in a medical supplier his office contracts:

1. Check share count: indirect shares able to elect a director make him a substantial stockholder under Section $3$(f).
2. Check duty link: approving supply contracts lets official duty affect that firm, creating a conflict of interest.

:::why
Why count indirect share ownership? Otherwise officials could hide controlling shares in trusts or holding firms while ruling on that business.
:::

:::pair #indirect-shares-and-potential-clash
Right: Indirect shares enough to elect a director create a substantial stockholding once official duty may affect the business.
Wrong: Assuming indirect share ownership cannot create a substantial stockholding under Section $3$(f).
Section $3$(f) expressly covers both direct and indirect shares sufficient to elect a director.
:::

:::check
A port commissioner owns a trucking firm whose trucks apply for terminal permits from her office. She has approved zero permits yet. Does a conflict of interest exist?
:::answer
The answer is **Yes, a conflict of interest exists.** Her permitting duties may affect the firm she owns, even without a prior permit decision.
:::

### Valid Divestment Requirements

Under Section $3$(j), **divestment** means transferring title or disposing of an interest **voluntarily, completely, and actually**.

Two strict rules govern valid divestment:

1. **Disallowed recipients**: never transfer to a **spouse** or covered **relatives** (within the fourth civil degree of consanguinity or affinity, plus bilas, inso, and balae).
2. **No retained control**: give up actual control, not just paper title or a board seat alone.

Compare selling $100\%$ of conflicting telecom shares to a first cousin versus selling $100\%$ outright to an unrelated buyer with zero retained control:

1. Selling to a first cousin fails Section $3$(j) because a first cousin is a fourth-degree relative.
2. Selling completely to an unrelated buyer with zero retained control satisfies Section $3$(j).

:::pair #no-transfer-to-spouse-or-relatives
Right: Valid divestment must transfer full title to an unrelated buyer, never to a spouse or fourth-degree relative.
Wrong: Treating a full transfer of shares to the official's spouse as valid statutory divestment.
Even a total title transfer fails Section $3$(j) when the recipient is a spouse or covered relative.
:::

:::pair #must-give-up-control-and-shares
Right: Divestment requires actually giving up both ownership and control of the conflicting shares.
Wrong: Keeping control over transferred shares under a side agreement with an unrelated buyer.
Section $3$(j) demands complete, actual disposal without any retained control.
:::

:::check
A customs collector transfers all his shares in a brokerage firm to his sister-in-law's husband (his bilas), leaving zero shares in his own name. Is that valid divestment?
:::answer
The answer is **No.** Section $3$(k) includes bilas as a covered relative, so transferring shares to him fails Section $3$(j).
:::

## Examples

### #1: Board Seat Without Share Ownership

An energy regulator owns zero shares in a power distributor, yet sits as a paid board member. His agency sets the distributor's power rates. Does a conflict of interest exist?

**Solution:**

1. Check Section $3$(i): serving on a private corporate board is a listed private role even with zero shares owned.
2. Check duty link: rate-setting decisions directly affect the distributor's business interest.

Answer: Yes, a conflict of interest exists under Section $3$(i).

**Check:** Corporate board seats trigger Section $3$(i) independently of share ownership.

### #2: Resigning Only From the Board

A mining regulator sits on a quarry firm's board and owns $40\%$ of its voting shares, enough to elect two directors. He resigns from the board but keeps his $40\%$ stake. Has he cured the conflict?

**Solution:**

1. Check board resignation: stepping down ends his corporate board role.
2. Check remaining shares: keeping $40\%$ voting stock leaves him a substantial stockholder under Section $3$(f).

Answer: No, resigning from the board does not divest conflicting shares.

**Check:** Separate the corporate role from share ownership; clear both when both conflict.

### #3: Comparing Two Share Transfers

A maritime regulator must divest voting shares in a shipping line. Proposal A transfers title to an unrelated investor with a side contract letting the regulator direct board votes. Proposal B sells all shares outright to an unrelated buyer with zero retained rights. Which passes Section $3$(j)?

**Solution:**

1. Proposal A: although the investor is unrelated, the voting side contract keeps the regulator in control.
2. Rule check: Section $3$(j) requires complete, actual disposal with zero retained control.

:::try
Finish the last step: rule on Proposal B and explain why it passes while Proposal A fails.
:::answer
The answer is **Only Proposal B satisfies Section $3$(j).** Proposal B transfers both title and control completely to an unrelated buyer, whereas Proposal A leaves control with the regulator.

**Check:** Verify voluntary complete disposal, loss of control, and an unrelated recipient.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Indirect stockholding: count shares held indirectly if they can elect a corporate director. {#indirect-shares-and-potential-clash}
- Spouse and relative transfers: never count a transfer to a spouse or fourth-degree relative as valid divestment. {#no-transfer-to-spouse-or-relatives}
- Retained control: give up both title and control, not just paper title or a board seat alone. {#must-give-up-control-and-shares}
:::
