## Core Concept

Section $11$ of RA $6713$ gives penalties for Code violations. Section $12$ assigns enforcement duties. First check what happened and which rule applies.

### General Administrative Penalties

**Administrative discipline** decides whether a public worker broke a work rule and should receive a penalty. A general Code violation may lead to a fine of up to $6$ months' salary, suspension of up to $1$ year, or removal from office. Suspension keeps the worker from work for a set period.

The proper body chooses the penalty based on how serious the offense is. A maximum is a ceiling. It is not the required penalty for every violation.

To find the fine ceiling, multiply the monthly salary by $6$:

$$
\text{Fine ceiling} = 6 \times \text{monthly salary}
$$

**Due notice and hearing** must come before the sanction. Notice tells the worker the charge to answer. A hearing gives a fair chance to answer and offer evidence.

The authority first checks the complaint and supporting facts. If a charge proceeds, the worker receives notice and may answer with records and other evidence. A formal investigation follows when required by the governing rules.

The deciding body considers both sides and issues a decision explaining its findings. The chance to answer may include written submissions. It does not always require the same courtroom steps as a criminal trial.

**Level of proof** means how strong the evidence must be. Under Rule $133$, Section $6$ of the Rules of Court, an administrative finding needs **substantial evidence**. This is relevant evidence a reasonable person would accept as enough to support the finding.

An unsupported accusation is not enough. The authority must connect the evidence to the acts charged.

### Criminal Penalties and Separate Cases

A **criminal case** asks a court to decide whether someone committed a crime. **Prosecution** means bringing and pursuing that case.

A criminal conviction needs **proof beyond reasonable doubt**, under Rule $133$, Section $2$. The evidence must leave no reasonable doubt about guilt in a fair judge's mind. This is a higher level of proof than substantial evidence.

Violations of Sections $7$, $8$, or $9$ may lead to imprisonment of up to $5$ years, a fine of up to ₱$5{,}000$, or both. The court may also order **disqualification**, which bars the person from holding public office. That added penalty is up to the court.

Section $7(c)$ concerns confidential or classified work information. This is protected information learned through office and not made public. A worker must not use or share it to serve private interests, give anyone an unfair advantage, or harm the public interest.

Check both the information and the purpose. Sending a lawfully public file does not by itself prove this offense. Sending a confidential file to give a business an unfair advantage presents a different case.

If another law gives a heavier penalty for the violation, prosecution proceeds under that law. Check this before choosing the Code's penalty.

Section $11(b)$ makes a Code violation proved in a proper administrative case enough cause for removal or dismissal. No criminal case must be filed first. A proper administrative case still requires evidence and a fair chance to answer.

### Private People Who Take Part

Section $11(c)$ covers private people who participate in conspiracy with public workers as co-principals, accomplices, or accessories. It gives them the same **penal liabilities**, meaning criminal responsibility and penalties. They are tried with the public workers.

Under Revised Penal Code Article $8$, **conspiracy** means that at least $2$ people agree to commit a crime and decide to commit it. Knowing about a plan alone is not enough.

The participation terms describe different roles. Articles $17$ to $19$ explain them:

- A **co-principal** takes a principal role with another offender. A principal directly commits the act, directly forces or induces it, or supplies help without which it could not happen.
- An **accomplice** knowingly agrees with the criminal purpose and gives effective help before or during the crime. The help must relate to the crime. The person does not meet the principal test.
- An **accessory** knows the crime occurred and takes part afterward, without having been a principal or accomplice. Covered acts include profiting from its proceeds or hiding or destroying evidence to prevent discovery.

Article $19$ also covers helping the principal escape under specific conditions. That ground requires abuse of public functions or one of the listed crimes or offender conditions. Merely helping someone afterward does not always fit.

Check the person's knowledge, acts, and timing. Then check whether the help was essential. A title such as “consultant” does not settle the role.

These role meanings do not erase Section $11(c)$'s conspiracy requirement. Do not assume that every helper joins a conspiracy. The court must apply the special law to the proved facts.

Revised Penal Code Article $20$ also exempts certain close relatives from ordinary accessory penalties, except for profiting from the crime. Check applicable exceptions and the special law before assigning liability.

### Misuse of Filed Statements

A **SALN** is a Statement of Assets, Liabilities and Net Worth. Assets are things owned, liabilities are debts, and net worth is assets minus debts.

Section $8(D)$ bans obtaining or using filed statements for purposes contrary to **morals or public policy**. These mean accepted standards of right conduct or the public good protected by law. The section also bans commercial use, such as selling information for marketing.

News and communications media have an exception to the commercial-use ban when they use statements to inform the general public. Public access does not allow every use.

Under Section $11(d)$, the official or employee concerned may bring an action over prohibited statement use. The court may assess up to ₱$25{,}000$. If another applicable sanction is heavier, that heavier sanction applies.

### Enforcement, Referrals, and Review

The **Civil Service Commission (CSC)** has primary responsibility for administering and enforcing the Code. It sends cases requiring prosecution to the proper authorities. It may also start administrative action allowed by law.

For example, a complaint about a public officer's suspected criminal misuse of office may go to the **Ombudsman** for investigation. RA $6770$, Section $15(1)$ authorizes it to investigate and prosecute. It has primary authority over cases that the Sandiganbayan can hear.

Under Section $11(4)$ of RA $6770$, the Special Prosecutor conducts preliminary investigations and prosecutes cases within the Sandiganbayan's authority, under the Ombudsman's control and authorization. A preliminary investigation checks whether there is enough basis to bring a criminal case. It does not decide guilt.

The **Sandiganbayan** is a court. It tries cases within its legal authority. Other cases go to the proper courts; not every Code complaint belongs in the Sandiganbayan.

Each House of Congress keeps its power to discipline its members for disorderly behavior. Section $12$ also directs the Ombudsman to take steps to protect citizens who report Code violations.

This duty is not a promise of automatic immunity from every legal claim. A reporter may raise threats or retaliation with the Ombudsman and request protection.

An **appeal** asks a higher body to review a decision. A **motion for reconsideration** asks the body that decided the case to review its own ruling. The available route depends on the body and decision.

For a CSC decision in a disciplinary case, the $2025$ civil service administrative rules provide review by the Court of Appeals under Rule $43$. The party files a verified petition for review, meaning a written request confirmed under oath.

The ordinary Rule $43$ period is $15$ days from notice of the decision, or of the denial of a timely motion for reconsideration. When a lawyer represents the party, notice to that lawyer matters. Check the applicable filing requirements and any extension rules.

This route does not make every agency decision directly appealable to that court. Follow any required administrative review first. Criminal cases and Ombudsman decisions have their own review rules.

## Examples

### #1: General Violation and Salary-Based Fine

After notice and a fair chance to answer, an employee is found liable for a general Code violation. The monthly salary is ₱$30{,}000$. What are the fine and suspension ceilings, and may removal be imposed?

**Solution:**
1. Multiply the monthly salary by $6$.

$$
6 \times 30{,}000 = 180{,}000
$$

2. The fine may reach ₱$180{,}000$. Suspension may reach $1$ year.
3. Removal is possible based on the offense's seriousness and the required process. No maximum is automatic.

### #2: No Chance to Answer

An agency suspends an employee based only on a complaint. It never states the charge or allows an answer. Has it met the general sanction rule?

**Solution:**
1. A sanction requires due notice and hearing.
2. The employee had neither notice nor a chance to answer. The agency has not met the required process.

### #3: Criminal Penalties

A court finds an officer guilty of a Section $7$ violation. What maximum criminal penalties does Section $11$ provide?

**Solution:**
1. The court may impose up to $5$ years in prison, a fine of up to ₱$5{,}000$, or both.
2. It may also bar the officer from public office. That added penalty is the court's choice.

### #4: Direct Participation

An officer and a private consultant agree to sell confidential agency records for private gain. Both decide to carry out the plan. The consultant directly takes part in the sale. What participation role do these acts show?

**Solution:**
1. Their agreement and decision show conspiracy.
2. Direct participation in the sale fits a co-principal's role.
3. If the Code violation and participation are proved, Section $11(c)$ gives the consultant the same penal liabilities. They are tried together.

### #5: Help Before the Crime

For purposes of distinguishing participation roles, a person learns of an already decided criminal plan and knowingly supports it. Before the crime, the person supplies useful equipment. The equipment helps carry out the crime but is not essential, and the person does not directly commit, force, or induce it. Which role fits?

**Solution:**
1. The person knowingly supports the criminal purpose and gives related help before the crime.
2. The facts exclude the principal roles. They fit an accomplice's role.
3. Applying Section $11(c)$ still requires checking its conspiracy condition. The role alone does not prove that condition.

### #6: Help After the Crime

A person had no part in committing a crime. After learning of it, the person destroys its instruments to prevent discovery. Which participation role does this conduct illustrate?

**Solution:**
1. The person knew about the completed crime and acted afterward.
2. Destroying its instruments to prevent discovery fits an accessory act under Article $19$.
3. This does not alone prove Section $11(c)$ conspiracy. Applicable exceptions and all requirements still need checking.

### #7: Knowledge Without Agreement

A private person learns that an officer plans to sell confidential records. The person does not agree to the plan or take part. Does this knowledge alone show conspiracy?

**Solution:**
1. Knowledge does not show an agreement and decision to commit the crime.
2. These facts do not establish conspiracy under Section $11(c)$.

### #8: Disputed Disclosure

An officer admits sending a work file to a business owner. The officer says it was public, but the release log shows it was still confidential. Messages from the officer's account describe a plan to give the owner an unfair advantage.

The officer offers evidence that a coworker shared the account and wrote the messages. After a fair hearing, the administrative body accepts the log and messages as reliable evidence against the officer. The criminal court still has reasonable doubt about who wrote them.

How do the proof rules affect the cases?

**Solution:**
1. The log supports the protected-information requirement of Section $7(c)$. The messages support a prohibited purpose. Both bodies must consider the officer's reply.
2. The administrative body finds the evidence reliable despite that reply. If a reasonable person would accept it as enough, it meets the substantial-evidence standard and can support discipline.
3. The criminal court has reasonable doubt about proof of the officer's purpose. It must acquit on this charge, meaning find the officer not guilty.
4. Criminal punishment requires proof beyond reasonable doubt. A proven Section $7$ offense can bring up to $5$ years in prison, a fine of up to ₱$5{,}000$, or both, with disqualification at the court's choice.
5. The acquittal for insufficient criminal proof does not by itself cancel the separate administrative finding. That case uses a different proof standard.

### #9: Marketing From a SALN

A company uses an employee's filed SALN to build and sell a marketing list. It is not media informing the public. Is this allowed, and what remedy does Section $11$ give?

**Solution:**
1. This commercial use lacks the media exception. Section $8(D)$ prohibits it.
2. The employee may bring an action under Section $11(d)$. The court may assess up to ₱$25{,}000$, subject to the heavier-sanction rule.

### #10: A Heavier Penalty

The same violation is punishable under RA $6713$ and another law. The other law gives a heavier penalty. Under which law should prosecution proceed?

**Solution:**
Section $11$ directs prosecution under the other law because it gives the heavier penalty.

### #11: Removal Without a Criminal Case

A proper administrative case proves an officer's Code violation. No criminal case has been filed. May the officer still be removed?

**Solution:**
1. Section $11(b)$ makes the proved violation enough cause for removal or dismissal.
2. A criminal case need not come first. The required administrative process still applies.

### #12: Routing a Complaint

The CSC receives evidence of a suspected criminal Code violation by an officer. The case falls within the Sandiganbayan's authority. Where can investigation and prosecution proceed?

**Solution:**
1. The CSC refers cases requiring prosecution. It may also take administrative action within its powers.
2. The Ombudsman has primary investigative authority over this kind of case. Its authorized Special Prosecutor handles investigation and prosecution within the stated powers.
3. The Sandiganbayan tries the case. Referral and investigation do not establish guilt.

### #13: Protection for a Reporter

A citizen reports a possible Code violation and fears retaliation. Which institution does Section $12$ direct to take protective steps?

**Solution:**
The Ombudsman must take steps to protect citizens who report Code violations by public officials or employees.

### #14: Review of a CSC Decision

The CSC denies a timely motion to reconsider its disciplinary decision. The employee wants court review. Which route applies?

**Solution:**
1. File a verified petition for review in the Court of Appeals under Rule $43$.
2. The ordinary period is $15$ days from notice of the denial. Follow the applicable filing and service requirements.
3. This route concerns the stated CSC decision. It is not a rule that every Code complaint goes directly to the Court of Appeals.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Making Every Penalty Automatic

> A maximum is a ceiling.

The general sanction depends on the offense's seriousness. Notice and hearing must come first. Court-ordered disqualification is also not automatic.

### #2: Waiting for a Criminal Case

> Administrative removal does not require criminal prosecution first.

A proper administrative case can support removal. It still needs evidence and a fair chance to answer.

### #3: Mixing Fine Ceilings

> Match the amount to the kind of case.

The general fine uses months of salary. The special criminal fine is up to ₱$5{,}000$. Prohibited statement use has a separate court-assessed ceiling of ₱$25{,}000$.

### #4: Confusing Agency Roles

> Enforcement, prosecution, trial, and protection are different tasks.

The CSC administers the Code and refers cases needing prosecution. The Ombudsman investigates and has the stated protection duty. Courts decide criminal guilt.

### #5: Giving Every Helper the Same Role

> Check acts, knowledge, timing, and the law's conditions.

Direct participation differs from nonessential help and later concealment. Mere knowledge is not conspiracy. A participation label does not prove every condition of Section $11(c)$.

### #6: Treating Public Access as Unlimited Use

> A public statement can still have use restrictions.

Section $8(D)$ bans specified purposes. The media exception concerns informing the public. It does not allow every business to sell SALN information.
