## Core Concept

### Reasonable Time for Completing Documents

Under Section $5(c)$ of RA $6713$, official documents must be processed and completed within a **reasonable time** from preparation.

Follow any deadline already set by law or valid rule. Under Rule VI, Section $4(b)$, when none exists, the **agency head** must issue rules prescribing a reasonable period.

Those rules weigh work complexity and paper completeness. Valid delay causes include:

1. Resource shortages beyond the office's control.
2. A court order halting action, or a disaster.
3. Missing papers owed by the applicant.

Being busy never lets a worker choose a personal deadline.

Test Rule VI on a zoning office with no statutory deadline where a typhoon destroys survey maps:

1. Check rule authority: the agency head prescribes the reasonable period by office rules.
2. Check delay cause: typhoon damage beyond the office's control justifies extra time.

Result: Agency-head rules govern the period, and storm damage counts as a valid delay factor.

:::pair #agency-head-sets-period
Right: When no law sets a deadline, the agency head must prescribe a reasonable period by rules.
Wrong: Letting each employee choose a personal completion deadline when no statute names one.
Rule VI assigns deadline rules to the agency head based on work complexity, not worker preference.
:::

:::check
A bureau has no statutory deadline for boat permits, so each examiner picks her own turnaround target. Does that meet Rule VI?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. When no law sets a deadline, the agency head must prescribe a reasonable period by rules.
:::

### The Three-Signatory Cap and Substitutes

Section $5(c)$ requires documents to contain **at most $3$ initials or signatures**, **as far as practicable** (whenever reasonably possible). A law or regulation can require more.

If an authorized signer is absent, Section $5(c)$ directs the **official next-in-rank** or the **officer-in-charge (OIC)** to sign on that official's behalf.

Under Rule VI, any other substitute needs proper **written authorization**. Oral permission is not enough.

Apply Section $5(c)$ when a division chief travels abroad and a routine voucher awaits her signature:

1. Check the cap: keep total initials or signatures at $3$ or fewer where practicable.
2. Check the substitute: let the assistant chief next-in-rank or designated OIC sign immediately.

Result: The next-in-rank official or OIC signs without delay.

:::why
Why cap routine papers at $3$ signatories and name substitutes? Long sign-off chains stall simple requests whenever one official goes on leave.
:::

:::pair #three-signatory-limit
Right: Written actions should carry at most $3$ initials or signatures as far as practicable.
Wrong: Routing routine papers through $4$ or $5$ approving officers as a general office habit.
Section $5(c)$ caps signatories at $3$ as far as practicable unless law requires more.
:::

:::pair #designated-substitute-signer
Right: When a signer is absent, the next-in-rank official, OIC, or a written substitute signs.
Wrong: Letting the first available staff member sign without regard to rank, OIC status, or written authority.
Availability alone never grants signing authority under Section $5(c)$ and Rule VI.
:::

:::check
A director is on sick leave. Her secretary offers to sign a supplier check after a phone call saying, "Go ahead." Is that valid?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Only the next-in-rank official or OIC can sign automatically, and others need written authorization.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Court Injunction and Special Sign-Off Rule

No statute sets a land-clearance timeline. A court injunction halts release during a boundary suit, and a regulation requires $4$ technical sign-offs. Must the office ignore both to meet the $3$-signature cap?

**Solution:**

1. Check the delay: a court order halting action is a valid reason delaying completion.
2. Check signatures: Section $5(c)$'s $3$-signature cap applies as far as practicable, so a regulation requiring $4$ controls.

Answer: No, the office must obey the court injunction and obtain all $4$ required signatures.

**Check:** Test both external legal barriers and whether a specific regulation overrides the $3$-signature cap.

### #2: Extra Sign-Off Layers

A city permit office requires $5$ supervisors to initial every routine renewal even though no law or ordinance requires more than $2$. Does this follow Section $5(c)$?

**Solution:**

1. Check legal mandate: no law or regulation requires $5$ signatures here.
2. Apply Section $5(c)$: routine actions carry at most $3$ initials or signatures as far as practicable.

Answer: No, requiring $5$ unnecessary signatories violates Section $5(c)$.

**Check:** Keep routine documents at $3$ signatories or fewer unless law or regulation requires more.

### #3: Absent Chief and Unfixed Deadline

A supply office has no statutory deadline for clearing bonds. When the chief travels, an aide who is neither next-in-rank nor OIC offers to sign a bond on a verbal promise. The aide also claims clerks pick their own deadlines. Rule on both claims.

**Solution:**

1. Check the deadline claim: Rule VI requires the agency head, not clerks, to set a reasonable period by rules.
2. Check the signature: test whether verbal permission lets a non-OIC aide sign under Section $5(c)$.

:::try
Finish the last step: rule on both the aide's signature and the clerk's deadline claim under Section $5(c)$ and Rule VI.
:::answer
The answer is **Both claims violate the rules.** The aide lacks next-in-rank, OIC, or written authority, and the agency head must prescribe the reasonable period by rules.

**Check:** Require next-in-rank, OIC, or written authority for substitutes, and agency-head rules for unfixed timelines.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Unfixed completion deadlines: require agency-head rules, never personal worker choice. {#agency-head-sets-period}
- Routine signatory count: limit papers to $3$ signatories as far as practicable unless law requires more. {#three-signatory-limit}
- Absent signer substitutes: use the next-in-rank official, OIC, or a substitute with written authorization. {#designated-substitute-signer}
:::
