## Core Concept

Section $5$ of RA $6713$ states duties of public officials and employees. Section $7$ lists acts they must avoid. Both protect the public from poor service and misuse of office.

### Who the Code Covers

**Public officials and employees** include people elected or appointed to government posts. Voters choose elected officials. An authorized person appoints a worker to a government post.

The Code covers permanent and temporary workers. It covers **career service**, which generally uses merit and fitness to select workers and offers a path for advancement. **Non-career service** includes elected posts and posts tied to a term or project.

Military and police personnel are included. Government includes national and local offices, other government bodies, and government-owned or controlled corporations and their subsidiaries. A subsidiary is a company controlled by another company.

Pay does not decide coverage. **Free voluntary service** means government service given without pay. A volunteer needs a proper document assigning the work and must be fit for it.

Rules against improper appointments of relatives also apply. Volunteers remain subject to conduct rules, duties, bans, and penalties. Serving for free does not allow improper gifts.

The **SALN**, or Statement of Assets, Liabilities and Net Worth, has separate filing rules. Assets are things owned, such as money and property. Liabilities are debts, and net worth is assets minus debts.

Rule XII of the Code's implementing rules exempts free-service volunteers from SALN filing and financial disclosures. **Implementing rules** explain how to carry out a law. This filing exemption does not remove the Code's duties and bans.

### Letters, Reports, and People at the Counter

A public worker must answer letters, telegrams, and similar messages from the public within $15$ working days after receipt. The reply must state the action taken. Working days are counted workdays, not every calendar day.

A routine request that can be settled at once should be handled without delay. A complex matter still needs a timely reply. The reply must explain the action taken, such as starting a review, and why more time is needed.

If more information is needed, the office should name the missing requirements. It should give a reasonable period to submit them and identify the worker in charge. A bare receipt does not explain what action the office took.

If another office handles the matter, the receiving office should refer it there and inform the sender. A request sent to the wrong office is not a reason to ignore it.

Heads or other responsible officers must submit an annual performance report within $45$ working days after the year ends. It shows the work done by the office, agency, or government corporation. The public must be able to inspect it during regular office hours.

Workers must also attend promptly to people seeking services in person. The written-reply deadline does not allow a counter worker to leave a visitor waiting without a proper reason.

### Completing Documents Within a Reasonable Time

**Reasonable time** means a period justified by the work and its conditions. If a law or valid rule sets a deadline, follow it. When none exists, the agency head must issue rules setting a reasonable period.

The rules must consider the kind of work and how simple or complex it is. They must also consider whether the needed papers and information are complete.

Other factors include a lack of resources caused by events outside the office's control. A court order stopping action may matter. So may the requesting person's failure to supply needed papers, or an unavoidable event such as a disaster.

These factors help explain a delay. They do not give every worker a free choice of deadline. Being busy does not by itself justify every delay.

As far as practicable, written actions or decisions should carry no more than $3$ initials or signatures. This means following the limit when it can reasonably be done. A law or regulation may require otherwise.

If the proper signer is absent, the next-in-rank official or officer in charge signs on that person's behalf. Another substitute needs proper written authorization. An absent signer should not cause needless delay.

### Public Access and Lawful Limits

Public documents must be ready for inspection during reasonable working hours. A record is not secret merely because an office wants to avoid public attention.

Some records have lawful protection. Release may expose national-defense, security, or foreign-affairs secrets. It may put someone's life in immediate danger or improperly expose private details.

**Privileged information** is information that a law or recognized legal rule protects from disclosure. Certain private communications between a lawyer and client are an example.

The rules also protect draft decisions, orders, and similar records. Some investigation records may be withheld if release would harm enforcement or deny a fair trial. Other grounds include exposing a confidential source or unjustifiably revealing investigation methods.

Early release may also be restricted when it would seriously harm financial markets or defeat a planned official action. These grounds have conditions. They do not make every unfinished matter secret.

### Failure to Respond and a Fair Process

Failure to act promptly may lead to **administrative disciplinary action**. This is a process to decide whether a worker broke a work rule and should receive a penalty. A complaint does not make the worker guilty.

The proper authority examines the complaint and records under the applicable Civil Service rules. The worker must receive notice of the charge and a fair chance to answer and present evidence. A penalty requires the proper process.

Dated proof of a timely reply may answer a claim that no reply was sent. For a complex request, the reply should show the action taken and explain the work still needed.

A timely referral and notice to the sender may also matter. A court order or missing required paper may explain a delay in completing a document. Neither automatically excuses silence about a written request.

### Private Interests and Prohibited Acts

A **financial or material interest** is a money or property stake. A worker must not hold that stake, directly or through another person, in a transaction needing the worker's office to approve it.

Outside work can also be prohibited. The ban concerns a private business the worker's office regulates, supervises, or licenses. This means the office has legal power to oversee the business, enforce its rules, or issue its operating permission.

Unless a law expressly allows it, the worker must not own, control, or manage that business. The ban also covers serving as an officer, employee, consultant, counsel, broker, agent, trustee, or nominee.

An officer helps lead the business, and an employee works for it. A consultant gives expert advice, while counsel gives legal advice. A broker arranges deals, and an agent acts for another person.

A trustee holds or manages property for someone else. A nominee holds a named role or interest for someone else. A supervisor's approval alone does not replace express permission in law.

**Private professional practice** means serving private clients in a profession, such as law or medicine. The Act requires authority under the Constitution or law. The implementing rules also refer to authority under regulation.

The private work must not conflict, or tend to conflict, with public duties. This means it must not pull against the worker's duty to the public. Legal authority alone does not allow conflicting work.

A worker generally must not recommend someone for a job in a private business with regular or pending official dealings with the office. Pending means still awaiting action or completion. Referrals required by law, international commitments, or assigned public duties have an exception under the rules.

The outside-business and recommendation bans continue for $1$ year after leaving office. Private practice has a different limit: for $1$ year, the former worker cannot practice on a matter before the former office. This does not ban every professional job everywhere.

**Confidential or classified information** is protected work information not made public. A worker must not use or share it for private gain, an unfair advantage, or harm to the public.

Workers must not ask for or accept gifts, favors, entertainment, loans, or other valuable benefits tied to their duties. The ban also covers a business their office oversees or a transaction it can affect. Gift exceptions have conditions, so low value alone does not establish permission.

## Examples

### #1: Written Reply Deadline

A citizen sends a written request to a government office. What response does Section $5$ require, and within what period?

**Solution:**

1. The office must reply within $15$ working days after receiving the request.
2. Its reply must say what action it took. Receiving the letter without answering is not enough.

### #2: A Complex Request and a Complaint

A request needs a review of old files. Within $15$ working days, the office explains the review underway and why more time is needed.

The citizen later claims there was no response because the review remains unfinished. What evidence matters?

**Solution:**

1. Check the dated reply and what it says about the action taken.
2. A review may take longer to finish, but the office still needs to answer within the reply period.
3. The unfinished review alone does not prove there was no reply. The worker must have a fair chance to answer any charge.

### #3: A Request for Another Office

Agency A receives a request belonging to Agency B. It knows which office can act. What should Agency A do?

**Solution:**

1. Agency A should refer the request to Agency B.
2. It should inform the sender about the referral. It should not leave the request unanswered.

### #4: A Visitor at the Counter

During office hours, a worker has no waiting tasks but tells a visitor to return another day. The visitor has all papers needed for routine service. Does the written-reply period justify this delay?

**Solution:**

1. The visitor seeks service in person. The worker must attend promptly.
2. The $15$-working-day reply rule does not permit an unjustified delay at the counter.

### #5: Court Order and Required Signatures

No law sets a document's completion deadline. A court order prevents action for now, and another regulation requires $4$ signatures. Must the office ignore these conditions to follow the usual $3$-signature guideline?

**Solution:**

1. The court order matters when judging reasonable time because it legally prevents action.
2. A law or regulation can require more signatures than the usual guideline.
3. The office must obey the order and obtain the required signatures.

### #6: An Absent Signer

A document is ready, but its usual signer is absent. Someone who is neither next in rank nor officer in charge offers to sign based only on oral permission. Is that enough?

**Solution:**

1. The next-in-rank official or officer in charge can sign for the absent signer.
2. Another substitute needs proper written authorization. Oral permission alone is not enough.

### #7: Late Annual Report

An agency submits its annual performance report $60$ working days after the year ends. It also refuses all public inspection during regular office hours. Do these actions meet Section $5$?

**Solution:**

1. The report is due within $45$ working days. Submission after $60$ working days is late.
2. The report must be available for inspection during regular office hours.
3. Both actions fail to meet these duties.

### #8: Private Details in a Record

Someone requests a government file containing another person's private medical details. There is no proper reason to expose them. Must the office release those details merely because it holds the file?

**Solution:**

1. Public access has lawful limits protecting personal privacy.
2. The office may withhold details whose release would improperly invade that privacy.

### #9: An Unpaid Government Volunteer

A properly designated free-service volunteer claims that exemption from SALN filing also allows gifts for official favors. Is that correct?

**Solution:**

1. Rule XII exempts free-service volunteers from SALN filing and financial disclosures.
2. It keeps them covered by the Code's duties and bans. The filing exemption does not allow gifts for favors.

### #10: Ownership and Office Approval

An employee owns shares in a company. A transaction involving that company needs the employee's office to approve it. Which ban applies?

**Solution:**

1. Shares give the employee a money stake.
2. The transaction needs the office's approval. The ban on financial or material interests applies.

### #11: Work for a Licensed Business

A licensing officer accepts a consultant job with a company licensed by the officer's agency. No law expressly allows it. May the officer accept?

**Solution:**

1. The company is licensed by the officer's own agency, and consultant is a covered role.
2. No law permits this arrangement. The officer may not accept it.

### #12: Two Tests for Private Practice

A government lawyer has legal authority to take private clients but proposes work that conflicts with public duties. Another lacks legal authority but claims there would be no conflict. May either rely on the stated reason alone?

**Solution:**

1. The first lawyer has authority, but the conflicting work is barred.
2. The second lacks legal authority. Absence of conflict alone is not enough.
3. Both legal authority and absence of conflict are required.

### #13: Sharing a Protected File

An employee shares a confidential work file to give a friend an unfair advantage in a private deal. Is this allowed?

**Solution:**

1. The employee learned the protected information through work.
2. Sharing it gives an unfair private advantage. The Code prohibits the act.

### #14: A Required Job Referral

An officer's assigned duties require referring qualified job seekers to employers. A referral goes to a company with a pending agency transaction. Does that fact alone make the referral unlawful?

**Solution:**

1. The general ban covers recommendations to companies with regular or pending office dealings.
2. Referrals required by assigned public duties have an exception. The pending transaction alone does not defeat it.

### #15: A Gift for Favorable Treatment

A regulated business offers an employee an expensive gift for favorable treatment. May the employee accept?

**Solution:**

1. The gift is tied to work the agency can affect.
2. It is offered for an official favor. No exception is shown, so the employee may not accept.

### #16: Outside Work After Resignation

A former officer resigned $6$ months ago. A company licensed by the former office offers a consultant job, and no law expressly permits the arrangement. Does resignation make the job allowed now?

**Solution:**

1. The outside-business ban continues for $1$ year after leaving office.
2. Only $6$ months have passed. The covered consultant job remains prohibited during that period.

### #17: A Recommendation After Retirement

An officer retired $4$ months ago and recommends a friend for a job at a business with a pending transaction before the former office. No law, agreement, or assigned duty requires it. Does retirement end the ban?

**Solution:**

1. The recommendation ban continues for $1$ year after retirement.
2. The business has a pending transaction, and no exception applies. The recommendation remains prohibited during that period.

### #18: Private Practice Before the Former Office

A lawyer left an agency $6$ months ago and proposes handling a private client's matter before that agency. The lawyer also claims that all private legal work is banned during this period. Are both positions correct?

**Solution:**

1. The lawyer cannot practice on a matter before the former office during the $1$-year period.
2. That specific limit does not ban every professional job everywhere. The claim about all private legal work is too broad.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Confusing a Reply With Final Completion

> A complex matter still needs a timely reply.

The office may need time to finish a review. It must explain its action within the reply period. Do not treat an unfinished review as proof that no reply was sent.

### #2: Treating the Signature Guideline as Absolute

> Read the conditions attached to the $3$-signature guideline.

It applies as far as practicable. A law or regulation may require more signatures. Another substitute for an absent signer needs written authority if not next in rank or officer in charge.

### #3: Assuming Every Record Is Open Without Limits

> A refusal needs a lawful ground.

Privacy, safety, security, and protected investigations can limit access. A wish to avoid public attention is not enough.

### #4: Assuming a Complaint Proves Guilt

> The worker must have a fair chance to answer.

Check receipt dates, replies, referrals, and reasons for delay. An accusation alone does not settle whether a duty was broken.

### #5: Mixing SALN Exemptions With Code Coverage

> A filing exemption does not erase conduct rules.

A free-service volunteer remains subject to duties and gift bans. Check which specific rule an exemption concerns.

### #6: Using the Same Rule for Every Former Worker

> Identify the activity before applying the $1$-year period.

The outside-business and recommendation bans continue after departure. The private-practice limit concerns matters before the former office. It does not forbid every private client.
