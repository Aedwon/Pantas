## Core Concept

### Amendment vs. Revision

Article XVII sets rules for changing the Constitution so no group can change it alone.

An **amendment** changes specific provisions without altering a basic principle. A **revision** changes the basic structure or a large part of the Constitution. These meanings come from Supreme Court case law, not Article XVII definitions.

Both amendments and revisions need voter ratification in a plebiscite. Congress and a convention may also propose amendments alongside voters.

Compare Proposal A, lowering the voting age by $1$ year in Article V, with Proposal B, rewriting Articles VI through VIII to merge branches:

1. Check Proposal A: it edits one specific rule while leaving basic principles intact, so it is an **amendment**.
2. Check Proposal B: it overhauls core structure across three branches, so it is a **revision**.

:::why
Why distinguish an amendment from a revision? People's initiative can propose amendments only, whereas Congress and a convention can propose either.
:::

:::pair #amendment-vs-revision-meaning
Right: An amendment changes specific provisions while keeping basic principles intact; a revision alters the basic plan or a large part of the Constitution.
Wrong: Defining an amendment as replacing the basic plan of government.
Replacing the basic plan of government is a revision, not an amendment.
:::

:::check
A proposal moves Congress's opening date from late July to early August while keeping every branch's powers intact. Is this an amendment or a revision?
:::answer
The answer is **An amendment**. Shifting one calendar date edits a specific provision without changing basic principles or governmental structure.
:::

### The Quantitative and Qualitative Tests

In *Lambino v. COMELEC*, the Supreme Court used two tests to classify constitutional proposals:

1. The **quantitative test** checks how many provisions change and how widely they reach.
2. The **qualitative test** asks whether basic principles or the basic plan of government change.

Count and effect both matter. Even a short proposal is a revision if it alters the basic form or structure of government. Classify a proposal before ratification.

Test a $1$-sentence proposal abolishing the Supreme Court and giving all judicial power to Congress:

1. Apply the quantitative test: only one sentence changes, so the count looks small.
2. Apply the qualitative test: removing a co-equal branch alters the basic plan of government, making it a **revision**.

:::pair #qualitative-test-short-proposal
Right: Apply both *Lambino* tests; even a short proposal is a revision if it changes the basic form or structure of government.
Wrong: Treating a change to the form of government as an amendment because it alters only a few provisions.
Changing the form of government fails the qualitative test and makes the proposal a revision.
:::

:::pair #classify-before-ratification
Right: Classify a proposal before ratification to verify that its proposal route is valid.
Wrong: Waiting until voters approve a proposal in a plebiscite before classifying it as an amendment or a revision.
Classification controls which proposal routes are valid before any plebiscite occurs.
:::

:::check
A $3$-line proposal replaces the elected presidency and Congress with an appointed council holding all lawmaking and executive power. Does its short length make it an amendment?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Under the qualitative test in *Lambino v. COMELEC*, replacing the basic plan of government makes it a **revision** despite its short text.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Compare the Changes

Proposal $1$ updates a local reporting deadline in one section without changing basic principles. Proposal $2$ merges the Supreme Court and Congress into one governing assembly. Classify each proposal.

**Solution:**

1. Check Proposal $1$: updating one reporting deadline edits a specific rule while keeping basic principles intact, so it is an **amendment**.
2. Check Proposal $2$: merging two co-equal branches alters the basic plan of government, so it is a **revision**.

Answer: Proposal $1$ is an amendment; Proposal $2$ is a revision because it changes the basic plan of government.

**Check:** Under the qualitative test, altering the form of government is a revision even when few provisions change.

### #2: Broad Multi-Article Rewrite

A draft proposal rewrites $95$ sections across six articles, altering economic rules, local autonomy, and executive powers. Classify it under *Lambino v. COMELEC*.

**Solution:**

1. Apply the quantitative test: rewriting $95$ sections across six articles alters a large part of the Constitution.
2. Apply the qualitative test: changing local autonomy and executive powers alters core structure too.

Answer: The proposal is a revision under both *Lambino* tests.

**Check:** Broad multi-article rewrites and structural shifts both classify a proposal as a revision.

### #3: Short Structural Shift Before Voting

A draft edits only $2$ sections of Article VI to replace elected district seats with appointed regional governors. Supporters call it an amendment because only $2$ sections change and voters have not voted yet. How should it be classified?

**Solution:**

1. Test the count argument: weigh the $2$-section count against the qualitative effect of replacing elected seats with appointed governors.
2. Test the timing argument: check whether classification waits for a plebiscite or occurs before ratification.

:::try
Finish the last step: state what effect the appointment change has under *Lambino v. COMELEC*, classify the draft, and say whether either argument holds.
:::answer
The answer is **The draft is a revision, and neither argument holds.** Replacing elected representatives with appointed governors alters the basic structure of government under the qualitative test, even across $2$ sections. Classification also occurs before ratification to verify the proposal route, not after voters vote.

**Check:** Test qualitative structural effect alongside section count, and classify the draft before any plebiscite.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Defining amendment vs. revision: amendments preserve basic principles, while revisions replace the basic plan. {#amendment-vs-revision-meaning}
- Short structural proposal: apply the qualitative test since form-of-government shifts are revisions. {#qualitative-test-short-proposal}
- Classification timing: classify before ratification to verify the proposal route. {#classify-before-ratification}
