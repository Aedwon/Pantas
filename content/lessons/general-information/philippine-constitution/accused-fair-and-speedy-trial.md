## Core Concept

### Section $14(2)$: Speedy Trial and the Four Delay Factors

Under Article III, Section $14(2)$, a criminal trial must be **speedy, impartial, and public**. **Speedy** means moving forward without unreasonable delay. A justified postponement does not break this right.

To judge a delay, weigh four factors together:

1. Length of the delay,
2. Reasons for the delay,
3. Whether the accused asked for a timely trial, and
4. Harm caused to the accused.

Never decide from elapsed days alone. Even if trial later resumes, earlier unexplained delay still needs review.

Test a two-week pause for typhoon flooding when the defense agreed to reset the date:

1. Check length and reason: a short flood pause has a sound reason.
2. Check assertion and harm: no unheeded demand or harm appears, so the pause is reasonable.

:::why
Why check four factors instead of days alone? Storm pauses differ from repeated, neglectful delays that hurt the defense.
:::

:::pair #speedy-not-instant
Right: Judge trial delay using all four factors: length, reason, assertion, and harm.
Wrong: Treating any postponement as a violation, or deciding solely by elapsed time.
Sound scheduling reasons and lack of harm separate valid pauses from unreasonable delay.
:::

:::check
After years of unexplained stalls, a court resumes trial and says no speedy-trial issue remains. Is it right?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Resuming trial does not justify earlier delay, which must still be weighed under all four factors.
:::

### Section $14(2)$: Impartial and Public Trial

An **impartial** trial means the judge favors neither side. Promising to favor the prosecution breaks impartiality even in a fast, open courtroom.

A **public** trial keeps the trial open to public observation, subject to lawful limits. Under Rule $119$, Section $21$ of the Rules of Court, courts may exclude spectators during testimony offensive to decency or morals. Closing a room on prosecutorial preference, or opening only the verdict, breaks Section $14(2)$.

Test a prompt, open courtroom where a judge privately coaches one side's witness during recess:

1. Check access and speed: spectators watched and the hearing moved quickly.
2. Check neutrality: helping one side build its case breaks impartiality.

:::pair #public-trial-lawful-limits
Right: Public trial keeps the trial open to observation, subject only to lawful limits.
Wrong: Opening only the verdict, or closing the room on a prosecutor's preference.
Lawful rules allow narrow exclusions for sensitive testimony while protecting open proceedings overall.
:::

:::pair #impartiality-independent-from-speed
Right: Impartiality requires an unbiased judge even when the trial is fast and open.
Wrong: Confusing judicial bias toward one side with speed, notice, or public access.
A fast, open hearing cannot cure a judge who takes sides.
:::

:::check
A prosecutor asks a judge to bar all spectators simply because cameras make him nervous. Does Section $14(2)$ allow closing the trial?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. A trial may be closed only on lawful grounds, never on prosecutorial preference.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Impartial and Public Trial

A criminal trial is closed to the public without a lawful reason. The judge also openly favors the prosecution. Which trial safeguards are involved?

**Solution:**

1. Check the closure: barring the public without a lawful basis raises the right to a public trial.
2. Check judicial bias: favoring the prosecution raises the right to an impartial trial.

Answer: Both the right to a public trial and the right to an impartial trial under Article III, Section $14(2)$.

**Check:** Section $14(2)$ requires open observation subject to lawful limits plus a neutral judge.

### #2: Repeated Trial Delays

A criminal trial is repeatedly postponed without a sound reason. The accused has asked for the trial to proceed, and the delay has caused a defense witness to become unavailable. Which right is most directly at issue?

**Solution:**

1. Check the forum: delay during a criminal trial falls under Section $14(2)$'s speedy-trial right.
2. Check the four factors: repeated unexplained delays, requests to proceed, and witness loss weigh against reasonableness.

Answer: The right to a speedy trial under Article III, Section $14(2)$.

**Check:** All four delay factors point to unreasonable delay, unlike a justified single postponement.

### #3: Closed Hearing for Sensitive Testimony

During a criminal trial, a judge excludes spectators only while a young victim gives sensitive testimony. The judge cites Rule $119$, Section $21$ of the Rules of Court, keeps the rest of the trial open, and treats both sides fairly. Does closing that witness session violate Section $14(2)$'s public-trial rule?

**Solution:**

1. Check the rule: Section $14(2)$ guarantees a public trial, subject to lawful limits.
2. Check the basis: Rule $119$, Section $21$ permits excluding spectators for testimony offensive to decency or morals.

:::try
Finish the last step: rule on whether the partial closure breaks Section $14(2)$ and explain why.
:::answer
The answer is **No, the partial closure does not violate Section $14(2)$**. Public trial allows narrow exclusions authorized by law and court rules. Here, the judge applied Rule $119$, Section $21$ while keeping the rest of the trial open.

**Check:** Lawful closure of one sensitive testimony differs from arbitrary or prosecutor-driven exclusion.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Postponement occurs in a criminal trial: check all four factors instead of counting days alone. {#speedy-not-instant}
- Spectators are restricted at trial: verify a lawful rule rather than treating every limit as unlawful or allowing prosecutor preference. {#public-trial-lawful-limits}
- Trial is fast and open yet the judge takes sides: identify a breach of impartiality. {#impartiality-independent-from-speed}
:::
