## Core Concept

### Sections $2$ and $3$: Searches and Privacy

Section $2$ protects people, homes, papers, and belongings against unreasonable searches and seizures. A **search** looks for evidence. A **seizure** takes control of a person or thing.

A **warrant** is a court order authorizing a search or arrest. Search warrants and arrest warrants require **probable cause**, reasonable grounds supported by facts. A judge must personally decide whether it exists.

For a search warrant, the judge personally examines the complainant and any witnesses under oath or affirmation. An **oath or affirmation** is a formal promise to tell the truth. The judge asks questions to check the facts supporting the requested search.

For an arrest warrant, the judge may personally evaluate the prosecutor's report and supporting evidence. Personal questioning of the complainant and witnesses is not always needed when those records are enough. The judge must decide independently, not simply accept the prosecutor's conclusion.

A search warrant must specifically describe the place to be searched and the things to be seized. An arrest warrant must identify the person to be arrested. A general command to search anywhere or take anything fails this requirement.

## Examples

### #1: A General Search Warrant

A search warrant names a house but permits officers to take any object they wish. Which warrant requirement is missing?

**Solution:**

1. Section $2$ requires a specific description of the things to be seized.
2. Permission to take anything does not supply that description.
3. The warrant lacks the required specific description.

### #2: An Arrest Warrant Based on Records

A judge personally reviews a prosecutor's report and supporting evidence and finds probable cause for an arrest warrant. Must the judge also personally question the complainant and witnesses merely because the warrant concerns an arrest?

**Solution:**

1. The judge must personally determine probable cause.
2. For an arrest warrant, personal review of sufficient supporting evidence can meet that duty.
3. Personal questioning of the complainant and witnesses is not always required.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Warrant Procedures Differ

> Personal determination does not always require personal questioning.

A judge personally examines the complainant and witnesses for a search warrant. For an arrest warrant, the judge may decide probable cause through personal review of sufficient supporting evidence.
