## Core Concept

### Betrayal of Public Trust

**Betrayal of public trust** is the $6$th named ground in Article XI, Section $2$. It can cover serious abuse of power or favoritism even when no criminal law is broken.

In *Gonzales III v. Office of the President*, the Supreme Court explained that non-criminal conduct needs **bad faith** and gravity matching the other grounds. Ordinary negligence is not a named ground, though inexcusable neglect can support betrayal of public trust with bad faith and comparable gravity. An honest mistake or ordinary policy disagreement is never enough. One set of facts may also raise more than one ground.

Compare two non-criminal acts by an impeachable official:

1. Repeatedly rigging agency rules in bad faith to shield close friends from public audit shows serious abuse, raising betrayal of public trust.
2. Supporting an unpopular tax rate after open public hearings shows a policy choice, not bad-faith abuse.

:::pair #betrayal-is-named-ground
Right: Section $2$ expressly names betrayal of public trust, not general negligence. Inexcusable neglect can support betrayal of public trust only with bad faith and comparable gravity.
Wrong: Treating "negligence in public duties" as one of Section $2$'s six expressly named grounds.
Ordinary negligence is unlisted; inexcusable neglect reaches betrayal of public trust only when bad faith and comparable gravity are shown.
:::

:::check
During a busy budget week, a commissioner makes an honest arithmetic error in a forecast and corrects it openly the next morning. Does that mistake show betrayal of public trust?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Betrayal of public trust requires bad faith and gravity comparable to the other grounds, not an honest error.
:::

### Accusation vs. Proof and Conviction

Naming a valid ground in a complaint only passes the initial list check. It never proves the facts, proves legal sufficiency, or convicts the officer.

Article XI, Section $2$, requires impeachment **and conviction** before removal. Under Section $3$, the House initiates and approves charges, while the Senate holds the trial and votes on guilt.

:::why
Why separate naming a ground from proving guilt? If naming a serious phrase removed an official at once, rivals could oust leaders before any evidence was tested.
:::

Trace what happens after a sworn complaint names betrayal of public trust against an impeachable officer:

1. Naming betrayal of public trust matches an allowed constitutional ground in Section $2$.
2. The House must still process and vote on the complaint, and the Senate must hear evidence and vote before any conviction.

:::pair #ground-not-conviction
Right: Naming a listed ground still requires evidence, House initiation, and a Senate conviction decision.
Wrong: Treating the mere naming of a listed ground or House receipt of a complaint as conviction and removal.
Accusations are claims to test at trial, while removal happens only after Senate conviction.
:::

:::check
A radio host claims an impeachable official lost office the moment a verified complaint citing betrayal of public trust reached the House Speaker's desk. Is that claim accurate?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Receiving a complaint only starts House review; evidence, formal charges, and Senate conviction are still required.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Bad-Faith Abuse Without a Criminal Charge

Without breaking a specific penal statute, an impeachable official repeatedly cancels clinic supply deliveries to rival towns in bad faith at public expense. Explain whether betrayal of public trust may apply and what remains before removal.

**Solution:**

1. Test the official's repeated bad-faith delivery cancellations against Supreme Court guidance on non-criminal betrayal of public trust.
2. Repeatedly blocking clinic aid out of bad faith shows the grave harm and bad faith required to raise that ground.
3. The House must still initiate charges and the Senate must hear proof before any conviction.

Answer: Betrayal of public trust may apply, and House action plus Senate proof and decision are still needed.

**Check:** Check both tests: bad-faith gravity makes the ground usable, and only Senate conviction removes the officer.

### #2: Policy Disagreement vs. Named Ground

A civic group dislikes an official's lawful decision to adjust ferry routes. They draft a complaint citing "dissatisfaction with policy." Test whether that draft names a Section $2$ ground.

**Solution:**

1. Compare "dissatisfaction with policy" with Section $2$'s $6$ named grounds.
2. Section $2$ names betrayal of public trust, not policy dissatisfaction, insubordination, or ordinary negligence.
3. Inexcusable neglect can support betrayal of public trust only with bad faith and comparable gravity, whereas lawful policy disagreement states no ground.

Answer: The draft fails because Section $2$ does not list policy dissatisfaction as an impeachment ground.

**Check:** Separate an unpopular lawful policy (not listed) from serious bad-faith abuse (betrayal of public trust).

### #3: Multiple Grounds and House Charges

An impeachable official secretly takes cash to rig agency bid rules in bad faith for a friend's company. One reviewer says one act raises only $1$ ground. Another says the official loses office once the House approves charges. Evaluate both claims.

**Solution:**

1. Check the first claim: test whether one set of facts may raise more than one Section $2$ ground.
2. Check the second claim: compare House initiation of charges with the Senate's role under Article XI, Sections $2$ and $3$.

:::try
Finish the last step: rule on both claims and explain what must still occur before removal.
:::answer
The answer is **Both claims are wrong: one set of facts may raise multiple grounds, and House charges alone never remove an official.** The facts point to both bribery and betrayal of public trust. Under Sections $2$ and $3$, the Senate must still hold a trial, weigh evidence, and vote to convict.

**Check:** Separate House initiation from Senate trial and conviction, and remember one act may raise more than one ground.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Betrayal of public trust: ordinary negligence is unlisted, while inexcusable neglect needs bad faith and comparable gravity to support betrayal. {#betrayal-is-named-ground}
- Ground vs. verdict: naming a valid ground still requires evidence, House charges, and a Senate conviction. {#ground-not-conviction}
:::
