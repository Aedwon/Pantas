## Core Concept

### Section $12(2)$: Banned Force and Secret Detention

Article III, Section $12(2)$ forbids torture, force, violence, threat, intimidation, or any means that destroy free choice.

Secret, solitary, **incommunicado** detention (cutting off all communication), and similar forms are banned outright. They are prohibited even without physical blows or a confession. Section $12(4)$ directs laws to punish violations and compensate victims.

Test Section $12(2)$ when officers hold a suspect in an unmarked basement room without outside contact or blows:

1. Check the setting: hiding the detainee and cutting off contact is secret, incommunicado detention.
2. Apply Section $12(2)$: the detention violates the Constitution even with no blows or confession.

:::pair #secret-detention-always-barred
Right: Secret, solitary, and incommunicado detention are prohibited even when no physical violence occurs.
Wrong: Allowing secret or cut-off detention because no physical force was used or no confession came out.
Section $12(2)$ bans hidden and cut-off detention directly, regardless of violence or questioning results.
:::

:::check
Officers keep a detainee in a hidden room and promise to log his location next week. Does that promise satisfy Section $12(2)$?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Secret detention is forbidden at once, and a promise to reveal the location later cannot cure it.
:::

### Section $17$ and Section $12(3)$: Forced Testimony and Inadmissible Statements

Article III, Section $17$ bars **compelled self-incrimination**. No person may be forced to give testimony that helps prove their own guilt.

The statement need not confess the whole crime, and protection applies before conviction. It guards against forced answers showing one's own guilt, not voluntary answers or another person's guilt.

Under Section $12(3)$, any **confession** or **admission** taken against Section $12$ or Section $17$ is **inadmissible** against the person. A confession admits guilt, while an admission acknowledges a helpful fact. Calling a statement voluntary never cures threats or denied rights, yet lawful admissions remain usable.

Test an officer threatening to arrest a clerk's brother unless the clerk admits holding the store key at midnight:

1. Classify the statement: admitting possession of the key is an incriminating admission, not a full confession.
2. Apply Sections $12(2)$, $12(3)$, and $17$: threats broke free choice, so the forced admission cannot be used in evidence.

:::why
Why exclude forced admissions as well as full confessions? A single forced fact, like being at the scene, can help convict a person just like a confession.
:::

:::pair #admissions-excluded-too
Right: Violations of Section $12$ or Section $17$ exclude both confessions and admissions against the person.
Wrong: Allowing a forced statement in evidence because it is only a factual admission, not a full confession.
Section $12(3)$ expressly bars any confession or admission obtained in violation of Section $12$ or Section $17$.
:::

:::pair #section-17-before-conviction
Right: Section $17$ protects against compelled self-incriminating testimony before conviction, including during questioning.
Wrong: Claiming Section $17$ applies only after the person has been convicted.
Forced testimony helping prove personal guilt is barred during questioning and trial, before any conviction occurs.
:::

:::check
After clear warnings and a written waiver in counsel's presence, a suspect freely admits owning a receipt. Does Section $12(3)$ exclude that admission?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Section $12(3)$ excludes confessions and admissions obtained through violations of Section $12$ or Section $17$, not lawful voluntary statements.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Threatened Confession

Investigators threaten a person until the person signs a confession. May that confession be used against the person under Section $12$?

**Solution:**

1. Check the method: threats that destroy free choice violate Article III, Section $12(2)$.
2. Apply Section $12(3)$: any confession obtained in violation of Section $12$ is inadmissible against the person.

Answer: No, the threatened confession cannot be used as evidence against the person.

**Check:** Threats vitiate free will under Section $12(2)$, triggering automatic exclusion under Section $12(3)$.

### #2: Hidden Room Without Violence

Police hold a theft suspect for two days inside an unmarked warehouse room and forbid phone calls or family visits. A supervisor says no rule was broken because nobody struck the suspect or questioned him yet. Does Section $12(2)$ allow that detention?

**Solution:**

1. Classify the custody: holding someone in an unmarked room without outside contact is secret and incommunicado detention.
2. Apply Section $12(2)$: secret and incommunicado detention are prohibited even with no physical violence or interrogation.

Answer: No, the detention violates Article III, Section $12(2)$.

**Check:** Section $12(2)$ bans secret and cut-off detention on its own, without needing physical blows or a confession.

### #3: Forced Partial Admission

During questioning without counsel, officers intimidate a warehouse guard until he admits turning off the alarm at closing time. He denies taking any goods. Officers argue the statement is usable because he never confessed to theft. How do Sections $12$ and $17$ apply?

**Solution:**

1. Classify the statement: turning off the alarm helps prove guilt, making it an **admission** rather than a confession.
2. Check Section $17$: forcing an incriminating fact violates protection against compelled self-incrimination.

:::try
Finish the last step: rule on whether the admission is usable and explain why avoiding a full confession does not save it.
:::answer
The answer is **No, the admission is inadmissible under Article III, Section $12(3)$**. Section $12(3)$ excludes both confessions and admissions. Even without admitting the whole theft, the intimidated factual statement violated Section $12$ and Section $17$.

**Check:** Section $12(3)$ excludes any confession or admission obtained through intimidation, missing counsel, or forced self-incrimination.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Secret or cut-off detention with no blows: Section $12(2)$ still prohibits it outright. {#secret-detention-always-barred}
- Intimidated factual admission short of full guilt: Section $12(3)$ excludes admissions as well as confessions. {#admissions-excluded-too}
- Investigator demands an incriminating fact before trial: Section $17$ protects before conviction too. {#section-17-before-conviction}
:::
