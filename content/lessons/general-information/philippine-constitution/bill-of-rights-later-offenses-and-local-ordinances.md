## Core Concept

### Section $21$ and Rule $117$, Section $7(a)$: Graver Offense Developing Later

Double jeopardy under Article III, Section $21$ has stated exceptions. Under Rules of Court, **Rule $117$, Section $7(a)$**, a conviction does not bar a later trial for a **graver offense**. That exception applies when **supervening facts** from the same act cause the graver offense after conviction.

For instance, an assault victim may die after the attacker's conviction for physical injuries. Prosecutors may then file homicide in specified circumstances, yet guilt still requires proof in court.

Test Rule $117$, Section $7(a)$ when an injured pedestrian dies after the driver's injury conviction:

1. Check timing: death from the same act happened after the first conviction.
2. Apply Rule $117$, Section $7(a)$: the supervening death allows a later case, where guilt needs proof.

:::why
Why allow a second trial for a later graver result? When the first trial ended, the graver crime did not yet exist.
:::

:::pair #supervening-graver-offense
Right: Under Rule $117$, Section $7(a)$, a graver offense developing after conviction permits a later trial.
Wrong: Claiming a prior conviction permanently bars prosecution for every later result of the same act.
Check whether a new graver harm developed after the first conviction, and still require full proof at trial.
:::

:::check
After a valid conviction for physical injuries, the victim recovers fully. May prosecutors retry the attacker for a graver crime on those facts?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. No graver offense developed after conviction, so Section $21$ bars a second trial.
:::

### Section $21$: One Act Under a National Law and a Local Ordinance

Article III, Section $21$ sets a special rule for an act punished by both a **national law** and a local **ordinance**. An **ordinance** is a local law. Conviction or acquittal under either bars another prosecution under the other for the same act.

National rank never overrides this rule. Seeking a different penalty also fails to allow a second trial.

Test Section $21$ when a vendor is validly convicted and fined under a city littering ordinance, and national prosecutors charge that same act:

1. Check the act: both measures punish the exact same littering act.
2. Apply Section $21$: the ordinance conviction bars the national prosecution.

:::pair #ordinance-and-national-same-act
Right: Under Section $21$, conviction or acquittal under an ordinance bars a national trial for the same act.
Wrong: Allowing a second trial because national law outranks an ordinance or seeks another penalty.
Section $21$'s second sentence treats national laws and ordinances equally for one act.
:::

:::pair #conviction-and-acquittal-both-bar
Right: Either a conviction or an acquittal under the law or ordinance bars prosecuting the same act under the other.
Wrong: Claiming only an acquittal under an ordinance bars a later national prosecution.
Section $21$ explicitly states that "conviction or acquittal under either" bars another case.
:::

:::check
A motorist is acquitted under a national traffic law for running a red light. Can a city prosecute that exact act under an ordinance?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Under Article III, Section $21$, acquittal under the national law bars prosecution under the ordinance.
:::

## Examples

### #1: A Graver Offense Develops Later

An accused is convicted of physical injuries after a valid first case. The victim later dies from those injuries, so a graver offense develops after conviction. Does the first conviction always bar prosecution for that graver offense?

**Solution:**

1. Check timing: the victim's death happened after conviction, creating a graver offense from the same act.
2. Apply Rule $117$, Section $7(a)$: supervening facts allow a later trial, though guilt still needs proof.

Answer: No, the first conviction does not automatically bar the later prosecution.

**Check:** Because the graver crime arose from supervening facts after conviction, Rule $117$, Section $7(a)$ creates an exception.

### #2: One Act Under a Law and an Ordinance

A person is acquitted under a national law. A local ordinance punishes the same act. May another prosecution proceed under the ordinance for that act?

**Solution:**

1. Identify the two rules: a national law and a local ordinance punish the same act.
2. Apply Article III, Section $21$: acquittal under either bars prosecution under the other for that act.

Answer: No, the second prosecution under the ordinance is barred.

**Check:** Section $21$ protects one act against successive trials under national and local measures.

### #3: City Smoke Conviction and a National Charge

A driver is validly convicted and fined under a city smoke-belching ordinance for one exhaust incident. Next month, a prosecutor files a national clean-air charge for that same incident. The prosecutor cites national rank, a prison penalty, and the prior conviction. May the national trial proceed?

**Solution:**

1. Check the act: both the ordinance and the national law target the exact same exhaust act.
2. Check rank, penalty, and outcome: Section $21$ bars retrial despite national rank, another penalty, or conviction.

:::try
Finish the last step: rule on the national case and answer the prosecutor's rank, penalty, and conviction claims.
:::answer
The answer is **No, the national trial is barred under Article III, Section $21$.** Neither national rank nor a different penalty overrides Section $21$'s same-act rule. A conviction under the city ordinance bars another case just like an acquittal.

**Check:** Both measures punish one act, so the first valid conviction closes off a second trial.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Graver harm happens after conviction: apply Rule $117$'s supervening-fact exception without assuming guilt. {#supervening-graver-offense}
- Act violates both a law and an ordinance: block retrial despite national rank or penalty differences. {#ordinance-and-national-same-act}
- First ordinance case ended in conviction: bar retrial because conviction and acquittal both trigger Section $21$. {#conviction-and-acquittal-both-bar}
:::
