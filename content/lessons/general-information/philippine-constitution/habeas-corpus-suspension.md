## Core Concept

### Section $15$: Purpose of Habeas Corpus

The **writ of *habeas corpus*** is a court order used to check whether a person is being held lawfully. The person holding the detainee must bring the detainee before the court and explain the legal basis for the custody.

Article III, Section $15$ protects the privilege of this writ to guard **personal liberty against unlawful detention**. It does not govern property tax assessments, municipal land disputes, or civil employment access.

Test Section $15$ when police hold a delivery driver in a station cell for four days without any warrant or court charge:

1. Check the restraint: a person's physical liberty is restricted in custody.
2. Apply Section $15$: a *habeas corpus* petition lets a court review whether the detention is lawful.

:::pair #habeas-protects-liberty
Right: The privilege of the writ of *habeas corpus* protects personal liberty against unlawful detention.
Wrong: Treating *habeas corpus* as a remedy for property tax assessments or municipal land disputes.
Check whether the dispute challenges unlawful custody of a person rather than property or taxes.
:::

:::check
A shop owner complains that a city assessor overvalued her building for real estate taxes. Is *habeas corpus* the proper constitutional remedy?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. The writ of *habeas corpus* protects personal liberty against unlawful detention, not property tax assessments.
:::

### Section $15$: Conditions and Limits of Suspension

Article III, Section $15$ allows suspension of the privilege of the writ **only when both conditions hold**:

1. There is **invasion** (an outside force entering the country with force) **or rebellion** (an armed uprising against the government), and
2. **Public safety requires it**.

Invasion and rebellion need not occur together, yet a general emergency label never replaces armed invasion or rebellion. Likewise, administrative convenience never replaces public safety.

Limits stay intact during valid suspension under Article III, Section $13$ and Article VII, Section $18$. Suspension never makes every detention lawful or cancels bail or trial safeguards. Detainees must be judicially charged within $3$ days or released.

Test an arrest of a peaceful tenant for unpaid rent during a valid rebellion suspension:

1. Check grounds: rebellion plus public safety supports suspending the writ privilege.
2. Check limits: suspension never grants blank arrest power or cancels bail and trial rights.

:::why
Why keep strict limits during valid suspension? Suspending the writ's privilege pauses one custody challenge for covered cases without turning officers into unchecked jailers.
:::

:::pair #both-suspension-conditions
Right: Suspension requires invasion or rebellion together with a real public-safety need.
Wrong: Suspending the writ privilege on rebellion alone without showing that public safety requires it.
Both prongs in Section $15$ must hold, and a storm or calamity label never replaces invasion or rebellion.
:::

:::pair #suspension-never-blank-power
Right: Valid suspension leaves limits intact and never makes every detention lawful or cancels trial safeguards.
Wrong: Claiming valid suspension makes every detention lawful or erases all trial protections.
Even when validly suspended, detention stays bounded by law, the $3$-day charge rule, and the right to bail.
:::

:::check
After a major volcanic eruption, officials declare a state of emergency and seek to suspend *habeas corpus* for public order. Does Section $15$ allow that?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. A natural-disaster emergency involves neither invasion nor rebellion, so Section $15$ forbids suspension.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Suspension Without the Required Grounds

Officials seek to suspend the privilege of the writ of *habeas corpus* for convenience. There is neither invasion nor rebellion. Is the stated ground enough?

**Solution:**

1. Check the first condition: Article III, Section $15$ requires invasion or rebellion, and neither exists here.
2. Check the second condition: public safety must require suspension, not mere official convenience.

Answer: No, convenience meets neither requirement under Article III, Section $15$.

**Check:** Both invasion or rebellion and a public-safety demand are missing, so suspension is barred.

### #2: Transit Strike Without Armed Conflict

During a city-wide port and transit strike, officials declare an economic emergency. They propose suspending the privilege of the writ of *habeas corpus* to clear blocked terminals. Neither invasion nor rebellion exists. May the privilege be suspended?

**Solution:**

1. Check the conflict test: Section $15$ permits suspension only during invasion or rebellion.
2. Check the emergency claim: an economic emergency label cannot replace armed invasion or rebellion.

Answer: No, Section $15$ forbids suspension without invasion or rebellion.

**Check:** Order concerns during a labor strike cannot bypass Section $15$'s invasion-or-rebellion test.

### #3: Five Days Uncharged During Rebellion

During an actual rebellion, officials validly suspend the privilege of the writ of *habeas corpus* for public safety. A camp commander holds a local journalist for $5$ days with no court charge and refuses her bail application on a bailable offense. He claims the suspension removes all custody deadlines and bail rights. Is the commander right?

**Solution:**

1. Check the suspension context: rebellion and public safety support suspending the writ privilege for covered cases.
2. Check constitutional limits: Article III, Section $13$ keeps bail intact, and Article VII, Section $18$ requires judicial charging within $3$ days or release.

:::try
Finish the last step: rule on the $5$-day uncharged detention and the refusal of bail under the Constitution.
:::answer
The answer is **No, the commander violates both the $3$-day charging rule and the right to bail.** Under Article VII, Section $18$, a detainee must be judicially charged within $3$ days or released. Under Article III, Section $13$, suspension never impairs the right to bail.

**Check:** Valid suspension under Section $15$ leaves both the $3$-day court-charge deadline and bail rights fully in force.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Question asks what *habeas corpus* guards: match it to personal liberty against unlawful detention. {#habeas-protects-liberty}
- Officials cite rebellion alone or a non-armed emergency: require both invasion or rebellion and public-safety need. {#both-suspension-conditions}
- Warden claims suspension makes every arrest lawful: check bail, trial safeguards, and the $3$-day charge limit. {#suspension-never-blank-power}
:::
