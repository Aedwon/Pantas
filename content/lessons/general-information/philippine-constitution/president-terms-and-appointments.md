## Core Concept

### Presidential Succession and Term Limits

Under Article VII, Section $4$, the President serves $6$ years without reelection. The Vice-President also serves $6$ years, with at most $2$ successive terms. Regular $7$-year terms without reappointment belong to Constitutional Commission members and the Ombudsman.

Upon the President's death, permanent disability, removal, or resignation, the Vice-President becomes President for the unexpired term (Section $8$). Voluntary renunciation does not interrupt service for the Vice-President's full term.

A successor who serves as President for **more than $4$ years** cannot later be elected President (Section $4$).

Test succession when a Vice-President succeeds with $3$ years and $6$ months left in the term:

1. Under Section $8$, the Vice-President immediately becomes President for the remaining $3$ years and $6$ months.
2. Because $3$ years and $6$ months is not more than $4$ years, the successor may still run for President later.

:::why
Why set the cutoff at more than $4$ years? Serving over two-thirds of a term counts as a full turn, blocking another election as President.
:::

:::pair #vp-succeeds-permanently
Right: When the presidency becomes vacant, the Vice-President becomes President for the unexpired term.
Wrong: Treating the Vice-President as only an acting President until Congress chooses someone else.
Section $8$ makes an occupied Vice-Presidency the permanent successor for the rest of the term.
:::

:::pair #four-year-succession-bar
Right: A successor who serves more than $4$ years as President cannot later be elected President.
Wrong: Letting a successor who served over $4$ years run for President because the term was inherited.
Section $4$ bars later election once succession service exceeds $4$ years.
:::

:::check
The President resigns with $4$ years and $3$ months left, and the Vice-President serves the rest. May the successor later run for President?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Serving $4$ years and $3$ months exceeds $4$ years, so Section $4$ bars later election as President.
:::

### Executive Duties and Appointment Consent

The President controls executive departments, bureaus, and offices and ensures laws are carried out (Section $17$). The President submits the budget within $30$ days from the opening of each regular congressional session (Section $22$).

The **Commission on Appointments** is a body of Senators and Representatives that confirms listed presidential appointments under Article VII, Section $16$:

1. Department heads, ambassadors, other public ministers, and consuls.
2. Armed forces officers from colonel or naval captain upward, plus Constitutional Commission members.

Judges need no confirmation because they come from a Judicial and Bar Council list (Article VIII, Section $9$). Appointing the **Vice-President** to the Cabinet also needs **no confirmation** (Article VII, Section $3$).

Sort two appointments made on the same day:

1. An army colonel needs Commission on Appointments consent under Section $16$.
2. A Court of Appeals Justice comes from a Judicial and Bar Council list without confirmation.

:::pair #vp-cabinet-no-confirmation
Right: Appointing the Vice-President to the Cabinet requires no Commission on Appointments confirmation.
Wrong: Requiring Commission on Appointments consent for the Vice-President's Cabinet post.
Article VII, Section $3$ expressly exempts the Vice-President's Cabinet appointment from confirmation.
:::

:::check
The President names a lawyer as Secretary of Justice and appoints a trial judge from a Judicial and Bar Council list. Which needs confirmation?
:::answer
The answer is **The Secretary of Justice only**. Department heads need Commission consent under Section $16$, while judges need no confirmation.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Appointment Consent

Which needs Commission on Appointments consent: an ambassador or a Supreme Court Justice?

**Solution:**

1. An ambassador is listed in Article VII, Section $16$, so Commission consent is required.
2. A Supreme Court Justice is appointed from at least $3$ Judicial and Bar Council nominees without confirmation (Article VIII, Section $9$).

Answer: The ambassador needs Commission on Appointments consent; the Supreme Court Justice does not.

**Check:** Ambassadors require Commission consent, while judicial posts follow the Council list without confirmation.

### #2: Succession at the Four-Year Boundary

The President resigns with exactly $4$ years remaining. The Vice-President succeeds and serves those $4$ years. Who becomes President, and may that successor later run for President?

**Solution:**

1. Under Article VII, Section $8$, the Vice-President becomes President for the unexpired term.
2. Under Section $4$, the bar hits only service of more than $4$ years, and exactly $4$ years is not over $4$.

Answer: The Vice-President becomes President for the rest of the term and may still run for President later.

**Check:** Section $4$ bars reelection only after more than $4$ years of succession service, not exactly $4$.

### #3: Vice-President in the Cabinet

The President appoints the Vice-President as Secretary of Education and names a CPA as Chair of the Commission on Audit. Which appointment needs Commission on Appointments consent?

**Solution:**

1. Check the Commission on Audit Chair: test whether Constitutional Commission members fall under Article VII, Section $16$.
2. Check the Vice-President's Cabinet post: test which Article VII rule governs appointing the Vice-President to the Cabinet.

:::try
Finish the last step: state which rule applies to each nominee and whether either needs confirmation.
:::answer
The answer is **Only the Commission on Audit Chair needs Commission consent, while the Vice-President needs no confirmation.** Section $16$ covers Constitutional Commission members, whereas Article VII, Section $3$ expressly exempts the Vice-President when appointed to the Cabinet.

**Check:** Section $16$ covers Constitutional Commission chairs and ordinary Cabinet heads, whereas Section $3$ exempts the Vice-President.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Presidential vacancy and terms: Vice-President succeeds permanently and can serve $2$ successive terms, while the President gets no reelection. {#vp-succeeds-permanently}
- Inherited presidential term: serving over $4$ years as successor bars later presidential election. {#four-year-succession-bar}
- Appointment consent: ambassadors and Cabinet heads need Commission consent, while judges and the Vice-President in the Cabinet do not. {#vp-cabinet-no-confirmation}
:::
