## Core Concept

### Graft and Corruption

**Graft and corruption** is $1$ combined ground in Article XI, Section $2$. It means misusing public office for an improper private benefit.

Under Republic Act No. $3019$, Section $3(e)$, giving a private party unwarranted advantage through favoritism or bad faith is one example. Criminal laws explain treason, bribery, and graft, yet Congress still tries impeachment separately from criminal courts.

Test two official decisions against graft and corruption:

1. Disqualifying qualified bidders in bad faith so a friend wins a ferry contract gives an unwarranted advantage, raising graft and corruption.
2. Raising a bridge fee after open hearings without favoritism or private gain is not graft and corruption.

:::pair #graft-vs-treason-contract
Right: Match misusing public power to give an unlawful contract favor to graft and corruption.
Wrong: Labeling an unlawful domestic contract favor as treason through aid to a wartime enemy.
Domestic contract favoritism misuses public power for private gain, whereas treason involves aiding a wartime enemy.
:::

:::pair #no-overlooking-named-grounds
Right: Keep all $6$ Section $2$ grounds in view, including culpable constitutional violation, treason, bribery, graft, high crimes, and betrayal of public trust.
Wrong: Rejecting culpable violation of the Constitution or betrayal of public trust as outside Section $2$'s list.
Both phrases appear expressly in Section $2$, while mere unpopularity is left out.
:::

:::check
An impeachable officer waives safety inspections in bad faith so a relative's shipping firm avoids port fees. Which Section $2$ ground fits best?
:::answer
The answer is **Graft and corruption**. Misusing official power in bad faith to give a relative an unwarranted advantage points to graft and corruption.
:::

### Meaning and Limits of Other High Crimes

**Other high crimes** covers serious offenses bearing on fitness for high office (UP College of Law Impeachment Primer). The Constitution gives no closed offense list under this phrase.

Do not assume every illegal act qualifies just because it breaks a law. An alleged ordered killing is grave wrongdoing usable under other high crimes, yet still needs evidence and a Senate decision.

:::why
Why leave "other high crimes" without a fixed list? Framers could not predict every grave crime, so the phrase covers severe offenses bearing on fitness for office.
:::

Compare two unlawful acts by impeachable officials under other high crimes:

1. Returning an agency library book $3$ days late lacks gravity for high office.
2. Ordering an armed kidnapping of a witness is a grave offense bearing on high office, subject to proof at trial.

:::pair #high-crimes-not-every-offense
Right: Limit other high crimes to grave offenses affecting fitness for high office, rather than every minor unlawful act.
Wrong: Claiming any offense falls under other high crimes solely because every unlawful act qualifies.
Other high crimes requires serious wrongdoing bearing on high office, not minor infractions.
:::

:::check
A petitioner invokes "other high crimes" because an official parked once in a no-loading zone. Does every minor unlawful act qualify?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Other high crimes covers grave wrongdoing bearing on fitness for high office, not minor traffic tickets.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Checking What the Closed List Names

Review four claims about Section $2$. They say (a) culpable violation of the Constitution, (b) betrayal of public trust, (c) treason, and (d) any unpopular ruling are listed. Which claim fails?

**Solution:**

1. Claims (a), (b), and (c) name real grounds listed in Section $2$.
2. Claim (d) fails because ordinary unpopularity is not one of the $6$ named grounds.

Answer: Claim (d) misunderstands the list because unpopularity alone is not a named ground.

**Check:** Recall all $6$ named grounds so you neither drop one nor add unpopularity.

### #2: Favoritism in Public Procurement

An impeachable official alters bid specs overnight so only an ally's firm qualifies for a port contract. Name the ground and explain why impeachment differs from a criminal trial.

**Solution:**

1. Using public power in bad faith for an ally's contract advantage points to graft and corruption.
2. Anti-graft law explains the conduct, yet Congress holds impeachment for removal rather than criminal imprisonment.

Answer: Graft and corruption fits the accusation, while impeachment remains a constitutional trial in Congress.

**Check:** Match domestic contract favors to graft and corruption, and keep Congress separate from criminal courts.

### #3: Grave Offenses vs. Minor Lapses

Two claims hit an impeachable official: missing a vehicle inspection deadline and ordering an unlawful killing. Apply Section $2$'s "other high crimes" rule to both.

**Solution:**

1. Minor regulatory lapses like a missed vehicle inspection lack the gravity for other high crimes.
2. Ordering an unlawful killing is a grave offense bearing on fitness for office.

:::try
Finish the last step: state whether ordering a killing fits other high crimes and whether the claim alone removes the officer.
:::answer
The answer is **Ordering a killing may fit other high crimes, while the missed inspection cannot, and Senate trial proof is still needed.** Not every offense qualifies, and no accusation removes an officer before conviction.

**Check:** Test gravity first under other high crimes, then confirm proof and Senate conviction remain mandatory.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Graft vs. treason: match domestic contract favoritism to graft and corruption, not wartime treason. {#graft-vs-treason-contract}
- Named grounds: keep all $6$ listed grounds, and reject claims that ordinary unpopularity is one. {#no-overlooking-named-grounds}
- Other high crimes: require grave offenses bearing on high office, not every minor unlawful act. {#high-crimes-not-every-offense}
:::
