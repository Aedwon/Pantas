## Core Concept

### Judicial Power and Judicial Review

Article VIII, Section $1$ gives **judicial power** two core duties:

1. Settle actual controversies involving legally demandable and enforceable rights.
2. Determine whether any government branch or body committed **grave abuse of discretion** amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction.

**Judicial review** checks whether a law or government act follows the Constitution. Courts need a proper case, not an advisory request. Policy disagreement alone does not make a law unconstitutional.

Test Section $1$ when a board cancels a permit without legal power and calls it policy:

1. The holder brings a proper case over a legally enforceable right.
2. Acting beyond legal authority can be grave abuse, so the court may review it.

:::pair #grave-abuse-review
Right: In a proper case, courts settle legal disputes and review grave abuse of discretion by government bodies.
Wrong: Limiting courts to private disputes, or letting a policy label block review of unlawful abuse.
Section $1$ lets courts check government acts that lack or exceed legal authority.
:::

:::check
A city lawfully sets park closing hours at $9$ in the evening, and a resident sues preferring $11$. Should a court strike it down?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Judicial review checks constitutional or legal limits in a proper case, not policy preference.
:::

### Supreme Court Composition, Appointment, and Tenure

Under Article VIII, Section $4(1)$, the Supreme Court has a Chief Justice and $14$ Associate Justices ($15$ Members total). It sits **en banc** (full Court) or in divisions of $3$, $5$, or $7$ Members. Challenges to a law's constitutionality must be heard **en banc** (Section $4(2)$).

The **Judicial and Bar Council** sends at least $3$ nominees per vacancy. The President appoints judges from that list, with **no confirmation** (Sections $8$ and $9$). Judges serve during good behavior until age $70$ or incapacity (Section $11$).

Trace a Supreme Court appointment and a constitutional challenge:

1. The Council submits at least $3$ nominees, and the President appoints $1$ without confirmation.
2. All $15$ Members sit en banc to hear a constitutional challenge to an executive agreement.

:::why
Why exempt judges from confirmation and protect tenure until age $70$? Removing reconfirmation and short terms protects judicial independence.
:::

:::pair #supreme-court-15-en-banc
Right: The Supreme Court has $15$ Members (Chief Justice plus $14$ Associates) and hears constitutional challenges en banc.
Wrong: Counting $14$ Members total, or routing a constitutional challenge to a $3$-member division.
Divisions of $3$, $5$, or $7$ hear other cases, while constitutional challenges go en banc.
:::

:::pair #jbc-list-no-confirmation
Right: The President appoints judges from at least $3$ Judicial and Bar Council nominees without confirmation.
Wrong: Requiring Commission on Appointments consent for judges, or letting the Council appoint judges directly.
The Council nominates at least $3$ candidates; the President appoints without confirmation.
:::

:::check
A trial judge turns $68$ after $7$ years on the bench with good behavior. Does a $7$-year limit force retirement?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Under Article VIII, Section $11$, judges serve during good behavior until age $70$ or incapacity.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Court Composition and Review

How many Members form the Supreme Court, which division sizes are allowed, and which branch may declare a law unconstitutional in a proper case?

**Solution:**

1. Under Section $4(1)$, $1$ Chief Justice plus $14$ Associate Justices equals $15$ Members.
2. Divisions have $3$, $5$, or $7$ Members, yet constitutional challenges go en banc.
3. The judicial branch exercises judicial review in a proper case.

Answer: $15$ Members; divisions of $3$, $5$, or $7$; the judicial branch (constitutional challenges en banc).

**Check:** $1 + 14 = 15$ Members total, and Section $4(2)$ sends constitutional challenges en banc.

### #2: Grave Abuse vs. Policy Choice

A water agency fines a factory without legal power and claims courts cannot touch agency policy. May a court review the fine in a proper case?

**Solution:**

1. The factory brings a proper case challenging an act beyond legal authority.
2. Under Section $1$, judicial power covers grave abuse of discretion by any government body.

Answer: Yes, the court may examine grave abuse, though it never replaces valid policy choices.

**Check:** Calling an unauthorized act policy cannot block review under Section $1$.

### #3: Judicial Appointment and Hearing Format

The Judicial and Bar Council sends $4$ nominees for a Supreme Court seat, and the President appoints $1$. Next month, a proper petition challenges a statute as unconstitutional. Does the new Justice need confirmation, and can a $5$-member division decide it?

**Solution:**

1. Under Section $9$, appointments from the Council list need no Commission on Appointments consent.
2. Under Section $4(2)$, challenges to a law's constitutionality must be heard en banc.

:::try
Finish the last step: answer both questions and explain how divisions differ from en banc hearings.
:::answer
The answer is **No confirmation is needed, and a $5$-member division cannot decide the constitutional challenge.** Section $9$ waives confirmation for judges. Section $4(2)$ sends constitutional challenges to all $15$ Members en banc, leaving other cases to divisions of $3$, $5$, or $7$.

**Check:** Council shortlist appointments skip confirmation, and constitutional challenges require all $15$ Members en banc.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Government abuse claim: courts review grave abuse of discretion in a proper case, even when labeled policy. {#grave-abuse-review}
- Supreme Court size and hearings: count $15$ Members total, and hear constitutional challenges en banc. {#supreme-court-15-en-banc}
- Judicial appointments: President appoints from at least $3$ Judicial and Bar Council nominees with no confirmation. {#jbc-list-no-confirmation}
:::
