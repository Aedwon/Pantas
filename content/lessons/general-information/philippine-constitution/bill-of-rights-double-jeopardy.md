## Core Concept

### Section $21$: When Double Jeopardy Attaches

Article III, Section $21$ states that **no person shall be twice put in jeopardy of punishment for the same offense**. **Double jeopardy** means facing trial risk a second time for the same offense after a protected ending in the first case.

Under Rule $117$, Section $7$, these are the basic general conditions for double jeopardy to attach in the first case:

1. A **valid complaint or information** (formal charge),
2. Before a **court of competent jurisdiction** (a court with legal authority),
3. **Arraignment** (formal reading of the charge) and a **valid plea** (such as guilty or not guilty), and
4. A protected end: **conviction**, **acquittal** (not-guilty judgment), or **dismissal without the accused's express consent**.

Recognized exceptions, such as certain dismissals requested by the accused, are covered in Article III, Part $4$.

Test a theft complaint dropped before arraignment:

1. Check arraignment and plea: none occurred.
2. Since those required steps are missing, double jeopardy never attached, so refiling is allowed.

:::why
Why wait until arraignment, plea, and a protected end? Until an accused answers a valid charge before a proper court, trial risk has never begun.
:::

:::pair #complaint-not-jeopardy
Right: Double jeopardy requires a valid charge, competent court, arraignment, valid plea, and a protected end.
Wrong: Treating a filed complaint alone as triggering double jeopardy without arraignment, plea, or a protected end.
Check every step from charge and plea through the ending before barring a second case.
:::

:::check
An accused enters a valid plea in a proper court and is acquitted after trial. Can prosecutors retry the same offense?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. A valid charge, proper court, arraignment, plea, and acquittal trigger double jeopardy under Section $21$.
:::

### Included Offenses and Law vs. Ordinance

Section $21$ and Rule $117$, Section $7$ also cover two related situations:

- **Included offenses:** An offense whose parts all sit inside the first charge (or one that includes it), plus attempt or frustrated stages. Renaming the charge does not evade the bar.
- **National law and local ordinance:** One act may violate both a national law and a local ordinance. Conviction or acquittal under either bars another prosecution for the **same act** under the other.

Test an ordinance acquittal for illegal dumping when prosecutors later file a national-law charge for the exact same act:

1. Check the first case: valid charge, proper court, arraignment, plea, and acquittal under the ordinance.
2. Apply Section $21$: an acquittal under the ordinance bars prosecuting the same act under national law.

:::pair #law-and-ordinance
Right: When an act is punished by a law and an ordinance, either conviction or acquittal under one bars prosecuting the same act under the other.
Wrong: Allowing a second trial because the law and ordinance have different sources, or thinking only a conviction bars the second case.
Section $21$ names both conviction and acquittal as a bar between law and ordinance.
:::

:::check
A driver is acquitted under a city traffic ordinance after arraignment and plea in a proper court. Can prosecutors charge the same act under a national law?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Under Section $21$, an ordinance acquittal bars prosecuting the same act under national law.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Locating Two Protections

Which two sections of Article III protect against compelled self-incrimination and double jeopardy, respectively?

**Solution:**

1. Locate forced testimony against oneself: Section $17$.
2. Locate double jeopardy: Section $21$.

Answer: Section $17$ and Section $21$, in that order.

**Check:** Section $17$ sits among trial protections, while Section $21$ sits right before Section $22$'s prohibited laws.

### #2: A Dropped Complaint

Police file an estafa complaint against a bookkeeper in a trial court. Before the court reads the charge or takes a plea, the prosecutor withdraws the complaint. Two months later, the prosecutor refiles the same charge, and the bookkeeper claims double jeopardy. Does Section $21$ bar the refiled case?

**Solution:**

1. Check the basic conditions: a valid complaint was filed in a proper court, yet no arraignment and no valid plea occurred.
2. Check trial risk: without arraignment, plea, and a protected end, double jeopardy never attached.

Answer: No, filing a complaint before arraignment and plea does not trigger double jeopardy.

**Check:** All four steps from valid charge through protected ending must be met, so withdrawing a complaint early allows refiling.

### #3: National Law and Local Ordinance

Dumping waste into a river violates both a city sanitation ordinance and a national clean-water statute. A factory owner faces a valid ordinance charge in a proper court, enters a not-guilty plea at arraignment, and wins an acquittal. Later, prosecutors charge the owner under the national statute for the exact same river-dumping incident. Can the national case proceed?

**Solution:**

1. Verify the first case: valid charge, court with authority, arraignment, valid plea, and acquittal are all present.
2. Check the second charge: it prosecutes the exact same act under national law.

:::try
Finish the last step: state whether the national prosecution may proceed and explain why the different law source and prior acquittal do not allow it.
:::answer
The answer is **No, the national case is barred because Section $21$ forbids a second prosecution for the same act after an ordinance acquittal.** Section $21$ treats both conviction and acquittal under either a national law or a local ordinance as a bar under the other.

**Check:** Different lawmaking bodies (city council and Congress) do not permit trying the same act twice once the first case meets the required steps.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Only a complaint was filed: require a valid charge, competent court, arraignment, valid plea, and protected ending. {#complaint-not-jeopardy}
- Same act under law and ordinance: conviction or acquittal under either bars prosecuting the same act under the other. {#law-and-ordinance}
:::
