## Core Concept

### Who is a citizen?

**Citizenship** means legal membership in a country. Some rights and government offices depend on it.

Article IV, Section $1$, names four groups of Filipino citizens:

- Citizens when the Constitution was adopted.
- Those whose fathers or mothers are Filipino citizens.
- Those born before January $17$, $1973$, to Filipino mothers who elect Philippine citizenship upon reaching the age of majority.
- Those naturalized under law.

**Elect** here means formally choose citizenship under law, not vote. **Age of majority** means legal adulthood. RA $6809$ lowered the general age from $21$ to $18$; older cases require the law that applied then.

Check the parent's citizenship at the child's birth. Birth abroad does not prevent Philippine citizenship. Birth in the Philippines alone does not grant it.

### Natural-born and naturalized

Under Article IV, Section $2$, a **natural-born citizen** is Filipino from birth without an act to acquire or complete citizenship. Those who elect under Section $1(3)$ are also deemed natural-born.

**Naturalization** is the legal process by which an alien becomes Filipino. An **alien** is someone who is not a citizen of the country concerned. A naturalized citizen is not natural-born.

The President, Senators, Representatives, and Civil Service Commission members must be natural-born. Citizenship alone does not meet that requirement.

Commonwealth Act $473$, Section $2$, generally requires age $21$ at the hearing and $10$ continuous years of residence. Section $3$ reduces residence to $5$ years for listed cases. Other conditions and disqualifications apply.

RA $9139$ provides a separate administrative process for aliens born and residing here since birth. It requires age $18$ at filing and other qualifications. Do not apply one process's age rule to every naturalization case.

### Keeping and regaining citizenship

Article IV, Section $4$, keeps citizenship after marriage to an alien unless an act or failure to act amounts to renunciation under law. **Renunciation** means giving up citizenship.

Section $3$ leaves loss and reacquisition to law. Under RA $9225$, Section $3$, natural-born Filipinos who lost citizenship through foreign naturalization reacquire it by the required oath of allegiance. Those naturalized abroad after the law took effect retain Philippine citizenship upon taking that oath.

An **oath of allegiance** is a sworn promise of loyalty to the Philippines and obedience to its Constitution and laws. Under Section $5(2)$, an election candidate must also personally renounce all foreign citizenship under oath before an authorized officer when filing the certificate of candidacy. Other office qualifications still apply.

**Repatriation** means restoring former Philippine citizenship. RA $8171$ covers Filipino women who lost it through marriage to aliens and natural-born Filipinos who lost it through political or economic necessity, including their minor children. Its conditions include an oath and registration with the proper civil registry and Bureau of Immigration.

**Dual citizenship** means citizenship in two countries. It can result from different citizenship laws or retention under RA $9225$. **Dual allegiance** involves voluntarily owing loyalty to two states.

Article IV, Section $5$, calls dual allegiance harmful to the national interest and directs that law address it. Having two citizenships alone does not establish dual allegiance.

### Who may vote?

**Suffrage** means the right to vote. Article V, Section $1$, requires Philippine citizenship, no legal disqualification, and these minimums:

- Age $18$.
- Residence in the Philippines for $1$ year.
- Residence in the place of voting for $6$ months immediately before the election.

RA $8189$, Section $9$, measures age and residence on election day. A person may register earlier if these requirements will be met then. Section $10$ requires registration to vote.

Section $11$ lists legal disqualifications, including certain final criminal judgments. A charge alone is not a final judgment. The law also provides ways to restore voting rights.

No literacy, property, or other substantive requirement may be added. Literacy means the ability to read and write.

### Protecting the vote

Article V, Section $2$, requires secrecy and sanctity of the ballot. This protects the voter's private choice and keeps the vote free from interference.

Congress must provide absentee voting for qualified Filipinos abroad. RA $9189$, as amended by RA $10590$, sets overseas voting rules, including registration and qualifications.

Congress must also design voting without assistance for voters with disabilities and those who cannot read. Until then, voting follows existing laws and COMELEC rules that protect ballot secrecy.

## Examples

### #1: Filipino Mother

A child was born in $2005$. The mother was a Filipino citizen when the child was born. The father was an alien. Is the child Filipino from birth?

**Solution:**

Yes, under Article IV, Section $1(2)$, because the mother was Filipino at birth.

### #2: Birth Abroad

Both parents were Filipino when their child was born abroad. Does birthplace prevent Philippine citizenship?

**Solution:**

No. The child is Filipino through the parents.

### #3: Election of Citizenship

Lina was born in $1970$. Her mother was a Filipino citizen when Lina was born. Her father was an alien. She validly elected Philippine citizenship upon majority. Is she natural-born?

**Solution:**

Yes. Article IV, Section $2$, expressly includes those who elect under Section $1(3)$.

### #4: Senate Qualification

Ana is natural-born. Ben is naturalized. Both meet all other Senate qualifications. Who meets the citizenship requirement?

**Solution:**

Ana, because Senators must be natural-born.

### #5: Marriage to an Alien

A Filipino marries an alien without legally renouncing citizenship. Is citizenship lost?

**Solution:**

No. Marriage alone does not remove it under Article IV, Section $4$.

### #6: Reacquisition and Candidacy

A natural-born Filipino lost citizenship through foreign naturalization. What separate steps does RA $9225$ require to regain citizenship and seek elective office?

**Solution:**

1. Take the required oath of allegiance to reacquire citizenship.
2. Meet office qualifications and personally renounce all foreign citizenship under oath before an authorized officer when filing the certificate of candidacy.

### #7: Two Citizenships

A child is Filipino through a parent who was a Filipino citizen when the child was born. The child is also a foreign citizen through birthplace. Does this alone establish dual allegiance?

**Solution:**

No. Two citizenships resulting from different laws do not alone establish voluntary loyalty to two states.

### #8: Election-Day Age

A registered Filipino turns $18$ on election day. By then, residence is $2$ years in the country and $8$ months locally. No disqualification applies. May this person vote?

**Solution:**

Yes. All minimums are met on election day.

### #9: Local Residence

Two adult Filipinos have each lived in the country for $10$ years. One has lived in the voting place for $8$ months, the other for $3$ months. Who meets both residence minimums?

**Solution:**

Only the first. The second lacks the required $6$ months locally.

### #10: Reading and Property Tests

May a law require voters to pass a reading test or own land?

**Solution:**

No. Article V, Section $1$, bars literacy and property requirements.

### #11: Ballot Protection

What must Congress provide under Article V, Section $2$?

**Solution:**

1. Protect ballot secrecy and sanctity.
2. Provide absentee voting for qualified Filipinos abroad.
3. Design independent voting for voters with disabilities and those who cannot read. Until then, existing laws and COMELEC rules must protect secrecy.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Birthplace Alone

> Check the parent's citizenship at birth.

Birth here alone does not make a child Filipino.

### #2: Election for Everyone

> The special election rule covers the group in Section $1(3)$.

Do not require every child of a Filipino mother to elect citizenship. Check the birth date and legal basis.

### #3: Marriage

> Marriage to an alien alone does not remove citizenship.

Look for legal renunciation, not just a foreign spouse.

### #4: Naturalization

> Naturalized and natural-born are different.

An office requiring natural-born status needs more than citizenship alone.

### #5: Dual Status

> Dual citizenship alone is not dual allegiance.

RA $9225$ allows retention under conditions. Candidates covered by this law must make a separate sworn renunciation of foreign citizenship. For dual citizens by birth, filing the certificate of candidacy counts as renouncing foreign citizenship.

### #6: Voting Minimums

> Check age and both residence periods separately.

Long residence in the country cannot replace local residence. Reading ability and land ownership are not required.

### #7: Voting Abroad

> The Constitution requires a system; law sets its conditions.

Being abroad alone does not qualify someone. Overseas voting rules and registration still apply.
