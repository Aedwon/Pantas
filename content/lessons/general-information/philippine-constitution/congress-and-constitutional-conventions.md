## Core Concept

### Three Proposal Routes

Recall from The Three Branches and Their Powers that Congress has a Senate and a House of Representatives.

Article XVII gives $3$ proposal routes for constitutional changes:

1. **Congress** (Section $1(1)$): proposes amendments or revisions.
2. **Constitutional convention** (Section $1(2)$): a special drafting body formed to propose amendments or revisions.
3. **People's initiative** (Section $2$): voters signing a petition to propose **amendments only**.

Asking voters whether to call a convention is not a fourth proposal route. Calling a convention directly or by voter referral both form the same convention route.

Sort three actions: lawmakers draft a change, a special elected assembly writes a charter, and voters sign a petition:

1. Lawmakers use the **Congress** route under Section $1(1)$.
2. A special assembly uses the **constitutional convention** route under Section $1(2)$.
3. Voters signing a petition use **people's initiative** under Section $2$, for amendments only.

:::pair #three-proposal-routes
Right: Article XVII gives $3$ proposal routes: Congress, a constitutional convention, and people's initiative (amendments only).
Wrong: Counting $2$ routes without initiative, or counting $4$ routes by treating voter referral for a convention as an extra route.
Asking voters whether to call a convention is part of forming the convention route, not a fourth route.
:::

:::check
A resolution asks voters whether to call a convention while lawmakers debate proposing an amendment themselves. How many proposal routes exist under Article XVII?
:::answer
The answer is **$3$ proposal routes**. Counting Congress, a constitutional convention, and people's initiative gives $3$; voter referral only decides whether to form a convention.
:::

### Congressional Thresholds and All Members

Article XVII sets three congressional votes, each counted out of **all** Members:

1. Propose an amendment or revision (Section $1$): $\dfrac{3}{4}$ of **all** Members.
2. Call a convention directly (Section $3$): $\dfrac{2}{3}$ of **all** Members.
3. Ask voters whether to call a convention (Section $3$): a **majority** (more than half) of **all** Members.

Counting only Members present gives too low a threshold. Hypothetical calculations below do not decide whether the Houses vote jointly or separately.

Suppose a hypothetical body has $300$ total Members, with $240$ present. Find the votes to propose a change and to call a convention directly:

1. Propose a change under Section $1$: take $\dfrac{3}{4}$ of all $300$ Members, giving $\dfrac{3}{4} \times 300 = 225$ votes.
2. Call a convention directly under Section $3$: take $\dfrac{2}{3}$ of all $300$ Members, giving $\dfrac{2}{3} \times 300 = 200$ votes.

:::why
Why count all Members instead of Members present? High supermajorities stop a small sitting group from changing the Constitution when seats are absent.
:::

:::pair #match-congressional-fractions
Right: Use $\dfrac{3}{4}$ to propose changes, $\dfrac{2}{3}$ to call a convention directly, and a majority to ask voters whether to call one.
Wrong: Using $\dfrac{2}{3}$ to propose changes, or using a simple majority to call a convention directly.
Each congressional threshold in Sections $1$ and $3$ has a distinct job.
:::

:::pair #count-all-members
Right: Compute every Article XVII congressional threshold from **all** Members, and require strictly more than half for a majority.
Wrong: Taking $\dfrac{3}{4}$, $\dfrac{2}{3}$, or a majority from only the Members present.
Absent Members stay in the base, so using only Members present undercounts the threshold.
:::

:::check
In a hypothetical body of $180$ total Members where $140$ attend, how many votes are needed to ask voters whether to call a convention?
:::answer
The answer is **$91$ votes**. Half of all $180$ Members is $\dfrac{180}{2} = 90$, so a majority is $90 + 1 = 91$ votes.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Match the Route

Which route uses lawmakers, a special drafting body, or voters signing a petition?

**Solution:**

1. Lawmakers acting in the legislature form the **Congress** route (Section $1(1)$).
2. A special drafting body is a **constitutional convention** (Section $1(2)$).
3. Voters signing a petition use **people's initiative** for amendments only (Section $2$).

Answer: Congress uses lawmakers, a convention uses a special drafting body, and people's initiative uses voters signing a petition.

**Check:** Each of the $3$ routes matches one proposing body or voter petition under Sections $1$ and $2$.

### #2: Congress Proposes

For a hypothetical congressional voting body with $240$ Members, how many votes are required to propose a constitutional change?

**Solution:**

1. Under Article XVII, Section $1$, Congress needs $\dfrac{3}{4}$ of all Members to propose a change.
2. Multiply $\dfrac{3}{4}$ by all $240$ Members:

$$
\dfrac{3}{4}\times240=180
$$

Answer: At least $180$ votes are needed. This hypothetical calculation does not decide whether the Houses vote jointly or separately.

**Check:** Two-thirds ($160$) only calls a convention, whereas proposing a change needs $\dfrac{3}{4}$ ($180$).

### #3: Calling a Convention

Using a hypothetical membership of $240$, compare calling a convention directly with asking voters whether to call one.

**Solution:**

1. Under Section $3$, the direct convention call requires $\dfrac{2}{3}$ of all $240$ Members:

$$
\dfrac{2}{3}\times240=160
$$

2. Asking voters requires a majority of all $240$ Members, meaning more than half.

:::try
Finish the last step: calculate the minimum vote to ask voters whether to call a convention and compare it with $160$.
:::answer
The answer is **Asking voters needs at least $121$ votes, compared with $160$ votes for a direct call.** Half of $240$ is $\dfrac{240}{2} = 120$, so a strict majority is $120 + 1 = 121$ votes, $39$ fewer than calling the convention directly.

**Check:** Exactly $120$ is half and fails; $121$ clears a majority of all $240$ Members, while $160$ clears $\dfrac{2}{3}$.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Remember the descending ladder of **all** Members: $\dfrac{3}{4}$ proposes changes, $\dfrac{2}{3}$ calls a convention directly, and more than $\dfrac{1}{2}$ asks voters.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Counting proposal routes: count $3$ routes (Congress, convention, initiative), not the ways to call a convention. {#three-proposal-routes}
- Mixing congressional fractions: match $\dfrac{3}{4}$ to proposing, $\dfrac{2}{3}$ to calling a convention, and a majority to asking voters. {#match-congressional-fractions}
- Counting only Members present: always use **all** Members as the base in Article XVII votes. {#count-all-members}
:::
