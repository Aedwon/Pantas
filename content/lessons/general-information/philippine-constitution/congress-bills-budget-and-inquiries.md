## Core Concept

### Bills Starting in the House and the Budget Limit

A **bill** is a proposed law, and to **originate** means to start. Article VI, Section $24$ requires six bills to start in the House:

1. **Appropriation bills** authorize spending public money.
2. **Revenue bills** raise government income, and **tariff bills** set customs duties on goods.
3. Bills increasing public debt, bills of local application (a particular place), and private bills (particular persons or private interests).

The Senate may propose or agree to amendments on these bills. Under Section $25(1)$, Congress cannot increase the President's recommended appropriations for government operations in the budget.

Test these rules on a tariff bill and the annual budget bill:

1. A tariff bill sets customs duties, so it starts in the House under Section $24$, and the Senate may amend it.
2. Congress may cut the President's recommended operating budget, yet cannot increase it under Section $25(1)$.

:::why
Why start tax and spending bills in the House? District members sit closer to local taxpayers, while Senate amendments keep national review intact.
:::

:::pair #house-origin-senate-amends
Right: Listed bills must start in the House, and the Senate may propose or agree to amendments.
Wrong: Starting a national revenue bill in the Senate, or barring Senate amendments on a House bill.
House origin controls where a bill starts, while Section $24$ protects Senate amendments.
:::

:::pair #no-budget-increase
Right: Congress cannot increase the President's recommended appropriations for government operations as specified in the budget.
Wrong: Raising those recommended operating appropriations through a majority vote in both Houses.
Article VI, Section $25(1)$ bars that budget increase even when both Houses agree.
:::

:::check
A Senator files a bill renaming a public market in one town. Does Section $24$ allow that bill to start in the Senate?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. A bill for one town is a bill of local application, so it must start in the House.
:::

### Inquiries in Aid of Legislation

Under Article VI, Section $21$, either House or its committees may hold inquiries to help make laws. Two safeguards bind every legislative inquiry:

1. It must follow **duly published rules** of procedure.
2. It must **respect the rights** of people appearing in or affected by it.

A Senate tax inquiry does not let a revenue bill start in the Senate. Both Section $21$ and Section $24$ apply.

Test Section $21$ on a House committee studying food safety before drafting a labeling bill:

1. The lawmaking goal authorizes the inquiry.
2. The committee must publish its rules in advance and respect every witness's constitutional rights.

:::pair #inquiry-rules-and-rights
Right: Legislative inquiries require duly published rules and respect for witnesses' rights.
Wrong: Ignoring witnesses' rights or published rules because an inquiry supports lawmaking.
Lawmaking opens the inquiry, yet Section $21$ still enforces published rules and personal rights.
:::

:::check
A Senate committee publishes its rules, respects witness rights, and studies customs smuggling. May it originate a new tariff bill in the Senate?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Even a valid Section $21$ inquiry does not change Section $24$, which requires tariff bills to start in the House.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Starting a Bill

Must a revenue bill start in the House? Must every education bill start there?

**Solution:**

1. A revenue bill must start in the House under Article VI, Section $24$.
2. An education bill need not start there unless it fits a listed category, such as an appropriation bill.

Answer: Yes for a revenue bill; no for a general education bill unless it fits a listed category.

**Check:** Only the six listed categories in Section $24$ must originate in the House.

### #2: Inquiry Safeguards

A Senate committee investigates a problem to help draft a law. It uses unpublished inquiry rules and ignores witnesses' rights. Does its lawmaking purpose satisfy Article VI, Section $21$?

**Solution:**

1. The goal supports an inquiry in aid of legislation.
2. Section $21$ still requires duly published rules and respect for people's rights.

Answer: No, both published rules and respect for rights are required.

**Check:** Under Section $21$, published rules and witnesses' rights bind every legislative inquiry.

### #3: Budget Limit

Congress proposes to increase the President's recommended appropriations for government operations as specified in the budget by passing a majority vote in each House. Does Article VI, Section $25(1)$ allow this increase?

**Solution:**

1. Identify the target: the proposal raises the President's recommended appropriations for government operations.
2. Check Article VI, Section $25(1)$: Congress may not increase those recommended appropriations.

:::try
Finish the last step: rule on the proposed increase and explain why a majority vote in each House does not allow it.
:::answer
The answer is **No, Article VI, Section $25(1)$ bars Congress from increasing those recommended appropriations.** A majority vote cannot override an express constitutional ban.

**Check:** Section $25(1)$ lets Congress approve or cut recommended operating appropriations, never raise them.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Revenue or tariff bill: start it in the House, and allow Senate amendments. {#house-origin-senate-amends}
- Legislative inquiry: require duly published rules and respect for witnesses' rights. {#inquiry-rules-and-rights}
- Recommended operating budget: Congress cannot increase it, even by majority vote. {#no-budget-increase}
:::
