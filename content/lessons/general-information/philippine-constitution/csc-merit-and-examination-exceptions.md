## Core Concept

### Merit, Fitness, and Examination Exceptions

Appointments must be based on **merit and fitness**. Merit concerns a person's qualifications and demonstrated ability. Fitness concerns whether the person is suited to the duties of the position.

For example, relevant skills and a strong work record can support a hiring decision. Friendship with an official does not show the ability to do the work. Passing an exam alone does not guarantee an appointment.

As far as practicable, merit and fitness are determined by **competitive examination**. This is an exam used to assess applicants against a common standard. The Constitution names exceptions for these positions:

- **Policy-determining:** the main work involves setting government policies or directions.
- **Primarily confidential:** the work mainly requires close personal trust with the official served, beyond the trust expected in an ordinary job.
- **Highly technical:** the work requires a high level of special knowledge or skill.

These are exceptions to the competitive-examination rule. Merit and fitness still apply. A position does not become primarily confidential just because it handles some private records.

## Examples

### #1: Examination Exception

A position is correctly classified as highly technical. May the appointing authority ignore merit and fitness because the competitive-examination exception applies?

**Solution:**

1. Highly technical positions are excepted from the competitive-examination rule.
2. The exception does not remove merit and fitness.
3. The authority must still choose someone qualified and suited to the work.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Exam Exceptions Still Require Ability

> No competitive exam does not mean no qualifications.

Policy-determining, primarily confidential, and highly technical positions still require merit and fitness. A job's actual duties matter when applying these categories.
