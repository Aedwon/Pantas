## Core Concept

### Security of Tenure

Article IX-B, Section $2(3)$, protects civil service workers from arbitrary firing or punishment.

No civil service officer or employee may be **removed or suspended except for cause provided by law**.

1. **Security of tenure** covers both removal and suspension.
2. It bars firing or suspension based on personal dislike or politics.
3. It never shields a worker from lawful discipline when a legal cause is proved.

Article IX-B also protects government employees' right to self-organization (Section $2(5)$) and statutory protection for temporary workers (Section $2(6)$).

Compare two disciplinary actions against permanent clerks:

1. Clerk A is suspended for $30$ days after due process proves falsified timecards under law, resting on legal cause.
2. Clerk B is suspended because a chief dislikes Clerk B's accent, lacking any legal cause.

:::pair #tenure-covers-lawful-discipline
Right: Security of tenure guards against arbitrary penalties, yet allows removal or suspension for lawful cause.
Wrong: Treating permanent status as blocking any suspension even when a legal cause exists.
Tenure stops unfair penalties without legal cause, never lawful discipline under statute.
:::

:::pair #tenure-requires-legal-cause
Right: Removal and suspension require a cause provided by law, never a supervisor's personal dislike.
Wrong: Treating a supervisor's personal dislike as lawful cause to suspend a civil service worker.
Personal friction is not a statutory offense, so suspending over dislike breaks Section $2(3)$.
:::

:::check
A permanent engineer is dismissed after an administrative hearing finds verified neglect of duty under civil service law. Does security of tenure block that dismissal?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Article IX-B, Section $2(3)$, allows removal when it rests on a cause provided by law.
:::

### Partisan Campaigning, Voting, and Exceptions

Article IX-B, Section $2(4)$, bars civil service workers from partisan politics.

No civil service officer or employee may engage, **directly or indirectly**, in any **electioneering or partisan political campaign**. **Electioneering** means working to help a candidate or party win.

1. Direct campaigning (speeches) and indirect campaigning (handing out flyers) are both banned.
2. **Voting differs from campaigning:** casting a private ballot under Article V, Section $1$, remains protected.
3. **Political-office exception:** political officials, such as elected mayors, may campaign within legal limits.
4. Also note Section $6$: no losing election candidate may be appointed to any government, GOCC, or GOCC subsidiary office within $1$ year after that race.

:::why
Why keep civil servants out of partisan campaigns? Neutral staff serve every citizen fairly no matter which party wins.
:::

Check two election-season acts by a public school teacher:

1. Casting a private ballot on election day exercises suffrage, not campaigning.
2. Posting party slogans urging neighbors to elect a slate is barred partisan campaigning.

:::pair #voting-vs-campaigning
Right: Civil service workers may vote on election day, yet cannot campaign directly or indirectly for a candidate or party.
Wrong: Treating casting a vote and working to help a political party win as the same banned activity.
Article V protects voting, while Article IX-B, Section $2(4)$, bars partisan campaign work.
:::

:::check
A permanent agriculture clerk hands out campaign shirts for a local party slate during lunch break. Does doing it on break make it lawful?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Article IX-B, Section $2(4)$, bars direct and indirect partisan campaigning even outside paid duty hours.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Lawful Discipline vs. Arbitrary Suspension

Two permanent clerks face suspension. Clerk M is suspended after proved bribery under civil service statutes. Clerk N is suspended because a new director wants to hire friends. Which suspension breaks security of tenure?

**Solution:**

1. Under Article IX-B, Section $2(3)$, removal or suspension requires a cause provided by law.
2. Proved bribery under statute is a legal cause, so Clerk M's suspension stands.
3. A director's preference for friends is not a legal cause, so Clerk N's suspension fails.

Answer: Only Clerk N's suspension violates security of tenure.

**Check:** Test every removal or suspension by asking whether a law provides the cause.

### #2: Voting vs. Indirect Campaigning

A municipal clerk casts a secret ballot at $8$ a.m. That afternoon, a civil service record officer hands out flyers for a congressional candidate behind a booth. Which worker broke Section $2(4)$?

**Solution:**

1. Casting a secret ballot is voting, not partisan campaigning.
2. Handing out campaign flyers helps a candidate win, which is barred partisan campaigning.

Answer: The record officer broke Section $2(4)$, while the clerk lawfully voted.

**Check:** Separate private voting (allowed) from helping any candidate or party win (barred).

### #3: Political Officials and Career Staff

An elected city mayor speaks on stage endorsing a party slate within legal limits. Meanwhile, the city's permanent civil service engineer wears a party vest on stage and asks voters to back that slate. Apply Section $2(4)$ to both.

**Solution:**

1. The elected mayor holds a political office, an exception allowed to campaign within legal limits.
2. The permanent city engineer is a civil service employee bound by Section $2(4)$.

:::try
Finish the last step: rule on the city engineer's stage speech and explain the difference between both roles.
:::answer
The answer is **The elected mayor falls under the political-office exception, while the permanent city engineer violates Article IX-B, Section $2(4)$.** Political officers may campaign within legal limits, whereas civil servants are barred from direct or indirect partisan campaigning.

**Check:** Check whether the person holds an exempt political office or a covered civil service post.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Lawful discipline: security of tenure allows removal or suspension for a cause provided by law. {#tenure-covers-lawful-discipline}
- Arbitrary cause: never treat personal dislike as lawful cause for suspension or removal. {#tenure-requires-legal-cause}
- Voting vs. campaigning: voting is allowed, while direct or indirect partisan campaigning is barred. {#voting-vs-campaigning}
:::
