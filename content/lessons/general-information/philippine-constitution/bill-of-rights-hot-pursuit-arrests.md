## Core Concept

### Two Rules for a Hot-Pursuit Arrest

Under Article III, Section $2$ and Rule $113$, Section $5$, an officer may make a warrantless **hot-pursuit arrest** only when two strict conditions are met:

1. **Immediacy:** An offense has in fact just been committed.
2. **Personal knowledge:** The officer has probable cause based on **personal knowledge of facts or circumstances** showing that the person to be arrested committed it.

Test how both conditions work right after a store robbery. A clerk calls police, and the arriving officer sees broken glass, footprints next door, and a person holding the cash drawer:

1. Check immediacy: the robbery just occurred moments ago.
2. Check personal knowledge: the officer directly observed the broken glass, trail, and cash drawer, linking that person to the robbery.
3. Both conditions hold, so the hot-pursuit arrest is valid even though the officer did not see the robbery happen.

:::pair #hot-pursuit-immediacy
Right: Hot pursuit requires an offense that has just been committed, followed by immediate police action.
Wrong: Invoking hot pursuit for an old or unsolved crime from weeks or months earlier.
Old or stale crimes require applying to a judge for an arrest warrant.
:::

:::pair #need-not-witness-crime
Right: For hot pursuit, the officer need not witness the crime itself, as long as it just occurred and the officer personally knows supporting facts.
Wrong: Claiming hot pursuit fails solely because the officer did not personally witness the entire crime.
Witnessing the crime is the in-presence rule; hot pursuit covers an immediate follow-up backed by personally observed facts.
:::

:::why
Why does hot pursuit allow an arrest without witnessing the crime? Right after a crime happens, fresh physical clues let an officer link a fleeing suspect quickly. At the same time, the rule blocks stale arrests based on rumor.
:::

:::check
Minutes after a burglary, an officer sees a broken lock and tracks muddy shoe prints to a man carrying the stolen laptop. Does hot pursuit fail because the officer did not see the break-in?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. The crime just happened and the officer personally saw facts linking the suspect to it.
:::

### Personal Knowledge Is Not an Unverified Tip

**Personal knowledge** means facts the officer sees, hears, or gathers directly at the scene or during an immediate investigation. A bare guess, rumor, or unverified text message does not give probable cause.

Test secondhand rumor. Three days after a street assault, an officer hears neighborhood gossip naming a driver and arrests him without checking facts:

1. Check immediacy: an assault from three days ago did not just occur.
2. Check personal knowledge: secondhand gossip supplies no personally known facts or circumstances.
3. Since both conditions fail, hot pursuit does not justify the arrest.

:::pair #personal-knowledge-not-tip
Right: Probable cause in hot pursuit must rest on facts or circumstances the officer personally knows.
Wrong: Treating an unverified report or tip alone as supplying personal knowledge.
An unverified tip must be investigated first to gather personally known facts supporting probable cause.
:::

:::check
An officer acts within five minutes of receiving an anonymous text naming a neighbor for a theft, without inspecting the scene or gathering any facts. Does this meet hot pursuit?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Acting fast on an unverified message gives no personal knowledge of supporting facts.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Personal Knowledge After a Crime

An officer arrives moments after a stabbing. The officer sees the wounded victim, a fresh blood trail, and a suspect holding a bloodied knife. The officer immediately arrests that suspect without a warrant. Which warrantless-arrest exception applies?

**Solution:**

1. Check immediacy: the officer arrived moments after the stabbing, so the offense had just occurred.
2. Check personal knowledge: the officer directly saw the victim, fresh blood trail, and bloodied knife.
3. Even without witnessing the stabbing itself, those personally observed facts meet the rule.

Answer: The hot-pursuit exception under Rule $113$, Section $5$.

**Check:** Both immediacy (moments after the crime) and personal knowledge (directly observed physical clues) are present, distinguishing this from an in-presence arrest.

### #2: A Tip Without Personal Knowledge

An officer arrests a man without a warrant based solely on an unverified message saying he stole a bicycle the previous month. Does the hot-pursuit exception justify the arrest?

**Solution:**

1. Test immediacy: a bicycle theft from the previous month did not just occur.
2. Test personal knowledge: an unverified message gives the officer no personally known facts linking the man to the theft.

:::try
Finish the last step: state whether hot pursuit applies and name both missing conditions.
:::answer
The answer is **No, hot pursuit fails on both rules: a month-old theft is not recent, and a bare message gives no personal knowledge.** Without both conditions, officers must apply to a judge for an arrest warrant.

**Check:** Even acting quickly after reading a tip cannot replace the two mandatory conditions under Rule $113$, Section $5$.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Crime happened weeks or months ago: hot pursuit requires an offense that just occurred. {#hot-pursuit-immediacy}
- Officer arrives right after the crime: hot pursuit does not require witnessing the crime itself if personal facts link the suspect. {#need-not-witness-crime}
- Arrest rests only on an unverified message: require personally known facts or circumstances, not a bare tip. {#personal-knowledge-not-tip}
:::
