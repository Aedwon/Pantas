## Core Concept

### Section $9$: Private Property and Just Compensation

Under Article III, Section $9$, private property cannot be taken for public use without **just compensation**. Just compensation means the full and fair value of the property taken.

A public purpose allows the taking, yet it never makes the property free. The rule covers a taking for public use, not every government rule that affects property.

Test Section $9$ on a town widening a canal across a house lot while also setting a fence height limit:

1. Check the canal lot: taking private land for a public canal requires full and fair payment.
2. Check the fence limit: a general safety rule regulates use without taking property for public use.

:::why
Why require full and fair payment when the project helps everyone? One owner should not pay the whole cost of a public work alone.
:::

:::pair #public-use-still-pays
Right: Taking private property for public use requires paying its full and fair value.
Wrong: Treating public use or access to the finished project as excusing payment.
Public use explains why the taking is allowed, yet Section $9$ still requires full and fair payment.
:::

:::check
A city takes part of a farm for a public market and offers free market stall access instead of payment. Does that satisfy Section $9$?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Access to the finished project does not replace paying the property's full and fair value.
:::

### Section $10$: Non-Impairment of Contracts

Article III, Section $10$ bars laws that impair the obligation of contracts. **Impair** means weaken or reduce duties agreed to in a valid contract, such as paying or delivering service.

A later law cannot simply erase valid contract duties without constitutional limits. Still, contracts remain subject to valid government regulation for public welfare.

Check both the law's effect on agreed duties and its valid public-welfare basis. A law affecting a contract is not automatically unconstitutional.

Apply Section $10$ when a new city health rule requires pricier food covers for contracted school canteens:

1. Check the contract duty: the catering deal sets meal delivery and price duties.
2. Check public welfare: inspect both the law's effect on those duties and its valid health purpose.
3. Result: because the rule serves a genuine food-safety purpose, added cost alone does not violate Section $10$.

:::pair #contract-public-welfare-balance
Right: Examine both a later law's effect on contract duties and its valid public-welfare basis.
Wrong: Claiming any effect on an existing contract automatically makes a later law unconstitutional.
Valid public-welfare regulation can affect existing contracts without automatically violating Section $10$.
:::

:::pair #welfare-label-no-erasure
Right: Calling a measure public welfare still leaves constitutional limits on erasing contract duties.
Wrong: Treating any public-welfare label as power to erase every existing contract duty.
A valid public-welfare basis must be examined, not accepted on a bare label alone.
:::

:::check
A city passes a fire rule requiring sprinklers in leased warehouses. Does added compliance cost automatically make the rule unconstitutional under Section $10$?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. You must examine both the rule's effect on contract duties and its valid public-welfare basis.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Land for a Road

The government takes private land for a public road and refuses to pay for it. Which constitutional protection applies?

**Solution:**

1. Identify the action: private land is taken for public use.
2. Apply Article III, Section $9$: taking for public use requires **just compensation**.
3. Check the excuse: a public road does not make private land free.

Answer: Just compensation under Article III, Section $9$.

**Check:** Section $9$ requires paying full and fair value whenever private property is taken for public use.

### #2: A Later Law and a Contract

A new law releases one party from a duty already accepted in a valid contract. Which constitutional protection should be examined?

**Solution:**

1. Identify the issue: a later law weakens an existing contract duty.
2. Apply Article III, Section $10$: the Constitution protects the obligation of contracts.
3. Check the limits: examine both the law's effect on the duty and any valid public-welfare basis.

Answer: Non-impairment of contracts under Article III, Section $10$.

**Check:** Section $10$ guards agreed contract duties while still leaving room for valid public-welfare regulation.

### #3: Setback Rule vs. Clinic Lot

A town passes a safety rule requiring bakeries to keep ovens two meters from street walls. Next month, the town takes half of a baker's yard to build a public clinic. Does Section $9$ require just compensation for the setback rule, the clinic lot, or both?

**Solution:**

1. Check the setback rule: limiting where ovens sit is a safety regulation, not a taking for public use.
2. Check the clinic lot: taking half the yard transfers private land to public clinic use.

:::try
Finish the last step: state which action requires just compensation and explain why public clinic use does not excuse payment.
:::answer
The answer is **The clinic lot requires just compensation, while the setback rule does not**. Public clinic use triggers Section $9$ payment instead of excusing it, so full and fair value is owed. An ordinary safety rule regulates use without taking property.

**Check:** Section $9$ turns on a taking for public use, which triggers full and fair payment rather than excusing it.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Public project takes private property: always pay its full and fair value under Section $9$. {#public-use-still-pays}
- Later law affects an existing contract: check both its effect and its valid public-welfare basis. {#contract-public-welfare-balance}
- Law carries a public-welfare label: check constitutional limits rather than letting it erase contract duties. {#welfare-label-no-erasure}
:::
