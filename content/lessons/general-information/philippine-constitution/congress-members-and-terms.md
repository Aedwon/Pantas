## Core Concept

### The Senate: Composition and Term Limits

Congress has two Houses: the Senate and the House of Representatives. The Senate has $24$ Senators elected **at large**, meaning nationwide (Article VI, Section $2$).

Senators serve $6$-year terms, with at most $2$ consecutive terms (Article VI, Section $4$). Consecutive means in a row, not a lifetime total. Giving up office by choice does not interrupt service for the full elected term.

Test the consecutive-term rule on a Senator elected in $2016$ and reelected in $2022$:

1. Count the terms served in a row: $2016$ to $2022$ is term $1$, and $2022$ to $2028$ is term $2$.
2. Because $2$ consecutive terms are the limit, the Senator cannot run in $2028$, yet may run again later.

:::why
Why cap consecutive terms instead of lifetime terms? Sitting out one cycle breaks continuous incumbency while letting experienced leaders return in a later election.
:::

:::pair #senate-nationwide-and-terms
Right: All $24$ Senators are elected nationwide at large under Article VI, Section $2$.
Wrong: Treating Senators as elected by local legislative district like district Representatives.
District elections belong to district Representatives, while all $24$ Senators are elected nationwide.
:::

:::check
A Senator resigns in year $5$ of his second consecutive term. Does that early resignation let him run for a third term right away?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. Under Article VI, Section $4$, voluntary renunciation does not interrupt service for the full elected term, so his second term still counts.
:::

### The House: Size, Party-List, and Term Limits

The House of Representatives has district and party-list members. Section $5(1)$ sets not more than $250$ Members, unless law fixes another total.

Section $5(2)$ sets the party-list share at $20\%$ of total House membership, including party-list seats. Some party-list seats may stay unfilled. Representatives serve $3$ years, with at most $3$ consecutive terms, and early resignation does not break the count (Section $7$).

Calculate maximum party-list seats when total House membership is $250$, including party-list seats:

1. Multiply total House seats by $20\%$: $0.20 \times 250 = 50$ party-list seats.
2. Subtract from $250$ to see the base district count: $250 - 50 = 200$ district seats.

:::pair #house-terms-not-senate
Right: Representatives serve $3$-year terms, with at most $3$ consecutive terms.
Wrong: Applying the Senate's $6$-year term and $2$-term limit to Representatives, or vice versa.
Senate rules are $6$ years and $2$ consecutive terms; House rules are $3$ years and $3$ consecutive terms.
:::

:::pair #house-cap-and-party-list
Right: The $20\%$ party-list share is computed from total House membership, including party-list seats.
Wrong: Taking $20\%$ of district seats alone instead of total House membership.
Article VI, Section $5(2)$ includes party-list seats in the total before applying $20\%$.
:::

:::check
Suppose a law sets total House membership at $300$ seats, including party-list seats. What is the maximum number of party-list seats?
:::answer
The answer is **$60$ party-list seats**. Take $20\%$ of the total $300$ House seats: $0.20 \times 300 = 60$.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Congressional Terms

What term and consecutive-term limit apply to each House?

**Solution:**

1. Check Article VI, Section $4$ for the Senate: $6$-year term, at most $2$ consecutive terms.
2. Check Article VI, Section $7$ for the House: $3$-year term, at most $3$ consecutive terms.

Answer: Senators serve $6$ years with at most $2$ consecutive terms; Representatives serve $3$ years with at most $3$ consecutive terms.

**Check:** Notice both limits apply to consecutive terms in a row, and neither counts as a lifetime cap.

### #2: Resigning Early in a Third House Term

A district Representative is in year $2$ of her third consecutive term and resigns to join a foundation. May she run for the same House seat in the immediately following election?

**Solution:**

1. Check her consecutive count: she is serving her third consecutive House term.
2. Apply Article VI, Section $7$: voluntary renunciation of office for any length of time does not interrupt full-term service.

Answer: No, she cannot run right away because the resignation does not break her three-term count.

**Check:** Under Section $7$, giving up the seat early still counts as serving the third consecutive term.

### #3: House Size Beyond $250$

Congress passes a law increasing total House membership to $350$ seats, including party-list seats. Does the Constitution forbid exceeding $250$, and what is the maximum number of party-list seats?

**Solution:**

1. Check Article VI, Section $5(1)$: the House has not more than $250$ Members unless a law provides otherwise.
2. Check Article VI, Section $5(2)$: party-list seats equal $20\%$ of total House membership, including party-list seats.

:::try
Finish the last step: state whether $350$ seats is allowed and compute the maximum number of party-list seats.
:::answer
The answer is **Yes, a law may set $350$ seats, giving at most $70$ party-list seats.** Take $20\%$ of total House membership: $0.20 \times 350 = 70$.

**Check:** Section $5(1)$ expressly permits a different total by law, and Section $5(2)$ applies $20\%$ to total House seats ($70$ of $350$ leaves $280$).
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Senate election: all $24$ Senators are elected nationwide at large, never by district. {#senate-nationwide-and-terms}
- House vs. Senate terms: keep $3$ years and $3$ terms for Representatives separate from $6$ years and $2$ terms for Senators. {#house-terms-not-senate}
- Party-list share: compute $20\%$ from total House membership, never district seats alone. {#house-cap-and-party-list}
:::
