## Core Concept

Article III of the $1987$ Constitution is the **Bill of Rights**. Its $22$ sections protect people against unlawful government action. A section number tells where to look, but the rule and its limits tell how to answer a case.

### Section $1$: Due Process and Equal Protection

**Due process** requires fair and lawful government action before taking life, liberty, or property. For a question about the steps taken, ask whether the person had notice and a real chance to answer. Also check whether the action has a lawful basis.

These are basic checks. The exact steps required depend on the kind of case. Do not assume that every government action requires the same kind of hearing.

**Equal protection** requires fair treatment under the law. People in similar situations should be treated alike. Different treatment requires a valid reason connected to the law's purpose.

For a law that groups people, check whether the groups have real differences. The grouping must fit the law's purpose, apply beyond present conditions, and treat everyone in the same group alike. A difference unrelated to the purpose does not justify the grouping.

These are basic tests for lawful grouping. Some kinds of unequal treatment require stricter court review. A case about an unfair grouping raises a different issue from a case about lack of notice.

### Sections $2$ and $3$: Searches and Privacy

Section $2$ protects people, homes, papers, and belongings against unreasonable searches and seizures. A **search** looks for evidence. A **seizure** takes control of a person or thing.

A **warrant** is a court order authorizing a search or arrest. Search warrants and arrest warrants require **probable cause**, reasonable grounds supported by facts. A judge must personally decide whether it exists.

For a search warrant, the judge personally examines the complainant and any witnesses under oath or affirmation. An **oath or affirmation** is a formal promise to tell the truth. The judge asks questions to check the facts supporting the requested search.

For an arrest warrant, the judge may personally evaluate the prosecutor's report and supporting evidence. Personal questioning of the complainant and witnesses is not always needed when those records are enough. The judge must decide independently, not simply accept the prosecutor's conclusion.

A search warrant must specifically describe the place to be searched and the things to be seized. An arrest warrant must identify the person to be arrested. A general command to search anywhere or take anything fails this requirement.

Some searches and arrests are lawful without a warrant. An officer may arrest someone who commits an offense in the officer's presence. Following a lawful arrest, the officer may search the person for weapons or evidence under the rule for a search incident to arrest.

A **hot-pursuit arrest** may follow an offense that has just been committed. The officer must have probable cause based on personal knowledge of facts or circumstances pointing to the person arrested. A bare guess or an unverified tip does not meet that rule.

The crime must have just occurred, and the officer must act with the required immediacy. Information about a crime long ago does not by itself meet this exception. The officer need not have seen the crime itself, but must personally know facts supporting the arrest.

Another search exception is freely given, valid **consent**, permission to search. It must be clear, specific, informed, and free from threats or pressure. Mere silence or submission to armed officers does not by itself show free consent.

The circumstances must show that the permission was valid. A lawful arrest does not allow an unlimited search of any place. Each warrant exception has its own conditions.

Section $3$ protects private messages and correspondence, such as letters. It allows intrusion upon lawful court order. It also allows an exception when public safety or order requires it, as provided by law.

The **exclusionary rule** keeps evidence obtained in violation of Section $2$ or $3$ from being used for any purpose in any proceeding. First decide whether a violation occurred. Then apply the rule to evidence obtained through it.

### Section $4$: Expression and Public Action

**Speech and expression** cover sharing ideas through words and other forms, such as signs or art. Look for government action that blocks or punishes the sharing of ideas, including criticism of officials.

**Press freedom** protects publishing and reporting news and views. A ban on a report simply because it criticizes an official raises a press-freedom issue. A report need not praise the government to receive protection.

**Peaceful assembly** means gathering peacefully with others to express a shared view. Check that the gathering is peaceful and that the restriction concerns the gathering. This protection does not give permission for violence.

A **petition for redress** asks the government to fix a wrong or respond to a complaint. Residents submitting a request for action on unsafe roads are petitioning the government. The right to ask does not promise that the request will be granted.

These freedoms have lawful limits. Identify the activity and check the reason and legal basis for the restriction. Calling an act expression does not settle whether a restriction is valid.

### Section $5$: Religion

The government may not establish a religion or ban its free exercise. **Non-establishment** means the government cannot make a religion the State's religion. **Free exercise** protects religious belief and worship without unfair treatment or preference.

No **religious test** may be required to exercise civil or political rights. A person cannot be required to belong to a certain faith to vote. Religious belief does not give a general right to harm others or ignore every law.

### Section $6$: Abode and Travel

**Liberty of abode** means choosing where to live and changing that place. Within limits set by law, it may be restricted only upon a lawful court order.

The **right to travel** has a different rule. It may be restricted for national security, public safety, or public health, as provided by law. Identify whether the case concerns residence or travel before choosing the rule.

### Sections $7$ and $8$: Information and Association

Section $7$ recognizes the right to information on matters of public concern. Citizens may access official records and papers about official acts, deals, and decisions. This also covers government research data used to make policy.

Access is subject to limits provided by law. Check whether the request concerns public affairs and protected records. Then check for a lawful limit, such as legally confidential information.

Section $8$ protects forming **associations**, groups organized for a shared purpose. Public and private employees may form unions, and people may form other groups. The purpose must not be contrary to law.

### Sections $9$ to $22$: Other Protections

Section $9$ requires **just compensation** when private property is taken for public use. This means fair payment for the property taken.

Section $10$ bars laws that impair the obligation of contracts. It protects duties under agreements from unlawful changes by legislation.

Section $11$ says poverty cannot be the reason for denying access to courts or adequate legal help. It also covers **quasi-judicial bodies**, government bodies outside the courts that hear facts and decide certain disputes.

Section $12$ protects a person under investigation for an offense. It includes being told of the right to silence and to a capable, independent lawyer, preferably of the person's choice. A lawyer must be provided if the person cannot afford one.

Giving up those rights requires a written waiver in the lawyer's presence. Torture, force, threats, and similar methods are banned. A confession or admission obtained in violation of Section $12$ or $17$ cannot be used against the person.

Section $13$ protects **bail**, security for temporary release while ensuring that the person returns to court. Before conviction, its stated exception concerns a charge punishable by **reclusion perpetua**, a severe prison penalty, when evidence of guilt is strong. Both conditions matter.

Excessive bail is forbidden. The right to bail remains protected even when the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus is suspended.

Section $14$ protects an accused person's fair criminal trial. It includes innocence until guilt is proved, a chance to answer with a lawyer, knowledge of the charge, and a speedy, impartial, public trial. It also protects meeting opposing witnesses and obtaining court orders for defense witnesses and evidence.

Section $15$ concerns **habeas corpus**, a court remedy for checking whether detention is lawful. Its privilege may be suspended only during invasion or rebellion when public safety requires it.

Section $16$ requires cases to be handled and resolved without unreasonable delay. It covers courts, quasi-judicial bodies, and administrative bodies. **Administrative bodies** are government offices handling matters under the law.

Section $17$ protects against being forced to give testimony against oneself. **Testimony** is a statement revealing what a person knows, such as an admission of guilt.

Section $18$ bars detention solely for political beliefs. It also bans **involuntary servitude**, forced work against one's will, except as punishment after due conviction for a crime.

Section $19$ bans excessive fines and cruel, degrading, or inhuman punishment. Such punishment attacks basic human dignity. The companion lesson, Article III, Part $4$, explains the constitutional death-penalty wording and the statutory ban under Republic Act No. $9346$.

Section $20$ bars imprisonment for debt or nonpayment of a **poll tax**, a tax charged per person. This does not excuse a separate crime merely because money is involved.

Section $21$ protects against **double jeopardy**, being put at risk of punishment again for the same offense after a protected end to the first case. The first case must have a valid charge in a court with authority to hear it. The accused must have been arraigned and entered a valid plea.

**Arraignment** is the formal reading of the charge, when the accused answers with a plea. The case must end in conviction, acquittal, or dismissal or termination without the accused's express consent. An **acquittal** is a judgment of not guilty.

The rule also covers certain included offenses, whose required parts are contained in another charge. A new charge's name alone does not settle the issue. Article III, Part $4$ explains these cases and exceptions in more detail.

If an act is punished by both a national law and a local ordinance, conviction or acquittal under either bars another prosecution under the other for the same act. An **ordinance** is a local law.

Section $22$ bans **ex post facto laws** and **bills of attainder**. An ex post facto law makes a harmful criminal-law change apply backward, such as punishing an earlier lawful act or increasing punishment for a past crime. A bill of attainder imposes punishment on named or identifiable people without a court trial.

## Examples

### #1: Counting the Sections

How many sections make up Article III of the $1987$ Constitution?

**Solution:**

Article III contains $22$ sections, from Section $1$ through Section $22$.

### #2: Notice and a Hearing

A government office takes a person's property without a lawful basis, notice, or a chance to answer. Which Section $1$ protection is most directly involved?

**Solution:**

1. The case concerns the government's basis and steps for taking property.
2. The person had no notice or chance to be heard.
3. These facts point to due process.

### #3: Unequal Treatment

A law gives different treatment to two groups. Their difference has no connection to the law's purpose. Which protection should be checked?

**Solution:**

1. Compare the grouping with the law's purpose.
2. The stated difference does not support that purpose.
3. The case raises equal protection under Section $1$.

### #4: A General Search Warrant

A search warrant names a house but permits officers to take any object they wish. Which warrant requirement is missing?

**Solution:**

1. Section $2$ requires a specific description of the things to be seized.
2. Permission to take anything does not supply that description.
3. The warrant lacks the required specific description.

### #5: An Arrest Warrant Based on Records

A judge personally reviews a prosecutor's report and supporting evidence and finds probable cause for an arrest warrant. Must the judge also personally question the complainant and witnesses merely because the warrant concerns an arrest?

**Solution:**

1. The judge must personally determine probable cause.
2. For an arrest warrant, personal review of sufficient supporting evidence can meet that duty.
3. Personal questioning of the complainant and witnesses is not always required.

### #6: An Offense in an Officer's Presence

An officer sees a person commit a theft and immediately arrests that person without a warrant. Does the absence of a warrant alone make this arrest unlawful?

**Solution:**

1. An offense committed in the officer's presence is a recognized ground for warrantless arrest.
2. The officer saw the theft being committed.
3. The lack of a warrant alone does not make this arrest unlawful.

### #7: A Search After Lawful Arrest

After lawfully arresting a person for an offense committed in the officer's presence, an officer searches that person for weapons and evidence. Is a separate search warrant always needed for that search?

**Solution:**

1. A search of the person following a lawful arrest is a recognized exception.
2. The stated arrest is lawful, and the search concerns weapons and evidence on that person.
3. A separate warrant is not required under these facts. This does not allow a search of any place the officer wishes.

### #8: Personal Knowledge After a Crime

An officer arrives moments after a stabbing. The officer sees the wounded victim, a fresh blood trail, and a person at the trail's end holding a bloodied knife. The officer immediately arrests that person based on those observations. Which warrantless-arrest exception should be checked?

**Solution:**

1. The facts point to an offense that has just been committed.
2. The officer personally observes facts pointing to the person arrested and acts immediately.
3. The hot-pursuit exception is the relevant rule. It requires probable cause from those facts, even though the officer did not see the stabbing.

### #9: A Tip Without Personal Knowledge

An officer arrests someone without a warrant based only on an unverified message saying the person committed a theft the previous month. Does the hot-pursuit exception justify the arrest on these facts?

**Solution:**

1. The alleged crime did not just occur.
2. The officer has only an unverified message, with no personal knowledge of supporting facts.
3. The stated facts do not meet the hot-pursuit exception.

### #10: Free Consent and Mere Submission

In one case, a person understands a request to inspect a bag and clearly permits that specific search without pressure. In another, armed officers surround a person, order the bag opened, and treat silence as permission. Does silence establish valid consent in the second case?

**Solution:**

1. The first case describes informed, specific permission freely given.
2. The second describes silence under pressure from armed officers.
3. Mere submission does not establish free consent. The consent exception does not justify the second search on those facts alone.

### #11: Unlawfully Obtained Messages

Officials obtain private letters in violation of Section $3$. May those letters be admitted as evidence in an administrative proceeding?

**Solution:**

1. The facts state that Section $3$ was violated.
2. Its exclusionary rule applies for any purpose in any proceeding.
3. The letters cannot be admitted in that proceeding.

### #12: Speech and the Press

An official bans a newspaper's peaceful criticism of public policy solely because it embarrasses the official. Which Section $4$ freedoms are most directly involved?

**Solution:**

1. The criticism expresses a view on public policy.
2. Publishing it in a newspaper also involves the press.
3. The ban raises freedom of speech and expression and freedom of the press.

### #13: Gathering and Petitioning

Residents peacefully gather and submit a request that officials repair a dangerous bridge. Officials ban both acts solely because they dislike the complaint. Which rights are involved?

**Solution:**

1. The peaceful gathering involves the right of peaceful assembly.
2. Asking officials to fix the problem involves petition for redress.
3. Section $4$ protects both activities. It does not guarantee approval of the request.

### #14: A Religious Voting Test

A rule requires voters to belong to a particular religion. Which constitutional prohibition does it violate?

**Solution:**

1. Voting is a political right.
2. Section $5$ forbids a religious test for exercising civil or political rights.
3. The required religious membership is such a test.

### #15: Establishment and Worship

One law declares a particular faith the State's religion. Another bans peaceful worship solely because officials dislike that faith. Which Section $5$ protections are involved?

**Solution:**

1. The first law raises the ban on establishing a religion.
2. The second interferes with free exercise of religion.
3. Section $5$ protects both government non-establishment and religious worship.

### #16: Residence and Travel

An official orders a person to change residence without a lawful court order. In a separate case, a law restricts travel because of a serious public health risk. Which Section $6$ rules should be checked?

**Solution:**

1. Forced change of residence concerns liberty of abode and requires checking for a lawful court order.
2. Travel has separate grounds: national security, public safety, and public health, as provided by law.
3. Public health is a listed travel ground. It does not replace the abode rule in the first case.

### #17: Records and Groups

A citizen requests a record of a government contract. Public employees also seek to form an association with a lawful purpose. Which sections cover these requests?

**Solution:**

1. An official contract record concerns access to public information under Section $7$, subject to lawful limits.
2. Forming an association for a lawful purpose falls under Section $8$.
3. Public employment does not remove the association right.

### #18: The Limits of Record Access

A citizen requests government policy research data. The office claims that part of the file is confidential under a law. Does naming Section $7$ alone decide whether the whole file must be released?

**Solution:**

1. Policy research data falls within the kinds of records Section $7$ covers.
2. Access remains subject to limits provided by law.
3. Check whether the claimed law actually protects that material. Neither the right's name nor the office's claim settles that question alone.

### #19: Locating Two Protections

Which sections protect against compelled self-incrimination and double jeopardy, respectively?

**Solution:**

1. Section $17$ protects against forced testimony against oneself.
2. Section $21$ protects against double jeopardy.
3. The answer is Section $17$ and Section $21$, in that order.

### #20: The Bail Exception

Before conviction, a person is charged with an offense punishable by reclusion perpetua, and the evidence of guilt is strong. Does the stated constitutional exception to bail apply?

**Solution:**

1. Section $13$ names offenses punishable by reclusion perpetua in its exception.
2. It also requires strong evidence of guilt.
3. Both conditions are given, so the stated exception applies.

### #21: A Completed First Case

An accused faces a valid charge in a proper court, is arraigned, and enters a valid plea. The court acquits the accused. May the State start a second prosecution for exactly the same offense on the same facts?

**Solution:**

1. The first case had the required charge, court authority, arraignment, and plea.
2. It ended in acquittal.
3. Section $21$ bars the second prosecution under these facts.

### #22: Punishing Without a Trial

A new law directly orders punishment of named people without a court trial. What prohibited kind of law is this?

**Solution:**

It is a bill of attainder because the law itself imposes punishment without a court trial.

### #23: A New Penalty for a Past Act

A law makes a previously lawful act a crime and punishes people who did it before the law took effect. Which Section $22$ ban is involved?

**Solution:**

1. The act was lawful when done.
2. The new criminal law reaches back to punish it.
3. This is an ex post facto law.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Due Process and Equal Protection Differ

> A hearing problem and an unfair grouping are different issues.

Check notice, a chance to answer, and lawful government action for due process. Compare groups and the reason for different treatment for equal protection.

### #2: Warrant Procedures Differ

> Personal determination does not always require personal questioning.

A judge personally examines the complainant and witnesses for a search warrant. For an arrest warrant, the judge may decide probable cause through personal review of sufficient supporting evidence.

### #3: A Warrant Exception Has Conditions

> A missing warrant does not settle every search or arrest question.

Check the facts against a recognized exception. An offense in an officer's presence can justify arrest. Hot pursuit requires a just-committed offense and personal knowledge supporting probable cause.

### #4: Silence Is Not Free Consent

> Permission to search must be freely and clearly given.

A person may submit because armed officers create pressure. Silence or lack of resistance does not by itself prove valid consent.

### #5: Privacy Exceptions Need a Legal Basis

> An official's wish to read a message is not a privacy exception.

Look for a lawful court order or a public-safety or public-order need provided for by law. Calling something urgent does not remove privacy.

### #6: Abode Is Not Travel

> Do not transfer the travel grounds to a residence question.

The Constitution gives these rights different restriction rules. Identify the action before applying an exception.

### #7: Rights Are More Than Labels

> Naming a section does not decide whether its conditions are met.

For bail, check both the charged penalty and the strength of evidence. For association, check that the group's purpose is lawful.

### #8: Debt Is Not a Separate Crime

> Failure to pay a debt alone is not a ground for imprisonment.

Section $20$ protects against imprisonment for debt or unpaid poll tax. It does not prevent punishment for a separate crime proved through a fair trial.

### #9: A Complaint Alone Does Not Establish Double Jeopardy

> Check the charge, court, plea, and end of the first case.

The rule requires more than a complaint. A valid charge in a proper court, arraignment and a valid plea, and a protected end to the case must be checked.
