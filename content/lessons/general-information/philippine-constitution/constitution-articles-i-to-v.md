## Core Concept

### Articles I and II: Territory and State Principles

The first two Articles define Philippine space and basic State rules:

1. **Article I: National Territory** defines national land, waters, and airspace. Waters around, between, and connecting islands form internal waters.
2. **Article II: Declaration of Principles and State Policies** sets State principles and goals. The Philippines is democratic and republican, so the people hold the highest political authority. **Sovereignty** belongs to the people, civilian authority leads the military, and government must serve and protect people.

An Article II State policy sets a broad goal, such as health or ecology. It never supplies full operating steps, so specific statutes and rules still apply.

Sort two questions between Articles I and II:

1. Sea passages connecting Visayan islands fall under **Article I** (National Territory).
2. A health policy's clinic budget rules need statutes beyond **Article II**.

:::pair #article-i-vs-ii
Right: Match territory and internal waters to Article I (National Territory), and State principles to Article II.
Wrong: Mixing Article I (National Territory) with Article II (Declaration of Principles and State Policies) or Article XI (Accountability).
Article I defines geographic territory, whereas Article II states governing principles and policies.
:::

:::pair #policy-not-full-procedure
Right: Treat an Article II State policy as a guiding goal that still relies on specific laws for operating steps.
Wrong: Treating a broad State policy as a complete operating procedure that removes the need to check specific laws.
Broad aims set direction, while statutes supply detailed procedures.
:::

:::check
An official cites Article II's youth policy to run a school-meal program without checking procurement statutes or nutrition rules. Is that valid?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. An Article II policy sets a goal, not a complete procedure, so statutes still govern.
:::

### Articles III, IV, and V: Rights, Citizenship, and Suffrage

Articles III, IV, and V cover individual rights, citizenship, and voting:

1. **Article III: Bill of Rights** guards liberties across $22$ sections. It covers due process, equal protection, speech, religion, privacy, and accused persons' rights.
2. **Article IV: Citizenship** sets citizenship by parentage or naturalization. **Natural-born citizens** are citizens from birth needing no acquisition act (plus a pre-$1973$ election exception).
3. **Article V: Suffrage** sets voting rules for citizens not disqualified by law. It requires age $18$ or older, $1$ year national and $6$ months local residence, and bars literacy or property tests.

:::why
Why keep Articles III, IV, and V in order? Each answers one question: what rights protect you (III), who are citizens (IV), and who may vote (V).
:::

Route three legal questions to Articles III, IV, or V:

1. Warrantless bag searches go to **Article III** (Bill of Rights).
2. Birth citizenship through a Filipino mother goes to **Article IV** (Citizenship).
3. Property-test bans for voters go to **Article V** (Suffrage).

:::pair #articles-iii-iv-v-order
Right: Keep Article III (Bill of Rights), Article IV (Citizenship), and Article V (Suffrage) separate in order.
Wrong: Placing the Bill of Rights in Article IV, Citizenship in Article II, or Suffrage under Citizenship.
Rights sit in III, citizenship in IV, and voting rules in V.
:::

:::check
A reviewer needs rules on keeping citizenship after marrying a foreigner, followed by secret ballot rules. Which two Articles should be opened?
:::answer
The answer is **Article IV, then Article V**. Citizenship retention sits in Article IV, while ballot secrecy sits in Article V.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Separating Territory From Civilian Supremacy

Rule (a) says waters connecting islands form internal waters. Rule (b) says civilian authority leads the military. Match each to Article I or Article II.

**Solution:**

1. Rule (a) defines national waters in Article I (National Territory).
2. Rule (b) states a governing principle in Article II (Declaration of Principles and State Policies).

Answer: Rule (a) sits in Article I, and rule (b) sits in Article II.

**Check:** Match land and waters to Article I and governing principles to Article II.

### #2: State Ecology Goal vs. Implementing Law

Article II directs the State to protect a balanced ecology. Explain why a factory permit still requires checking environmental statutes beyond Article II.

**Solution:**

1. Article II State policies state broad goals rather than detailed permit steps.
2. Environmental statutes supply filing steps, fees, and emission limits.

Answer: Article II sets the goal, while statutes and rules supply operating procedures.

**Check:** Never treat an Article II policy goal as replacing detailed statutes.

### #3: Fixing Misplaced Entries in Articles I to V

An index puts "Right Against Self-Incrimination" in IV, "Natural-Born Citizenship" in V, and "Age $18$ Voting Rule" in III. Correct all three.

**Solution:**

1. "Right Against Self-Incrimination" protects individuals from forced testimony, so it moves to Article III (Bill of Rights).
2. "Natural-Born Citizenship" defines citizen status, so it moves to Article IV (Citizenship).

:::try
Finish the last step: assign "Age $18$ Voting Rule" to its true Article and summarize III, IV, V.
:::answer
The answer is **Move the $18$-year rule to Article V (Suffrage), giving III (Bill of Rights), IV (Citizenship), and V (Suffrage).** Each Article covers a distinct subject without overlapping.

**Check:** Verify III = rights, IV = citizenship status, and V = voting rules.
:::

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Articles I and II: match geographic territory to Article I and principles or policies to Article II. {#article-i-vs-ii}
- Policy vs. procedure: treat Article II State policies as broad goals, never complete operating rules. {#policy-not-full-procedure}
- Articles III to V: keep Bill of Rights (III), Citizenship (IV), and Suffrage (V) distinct in order. {#articles-iii-iv-v-order}
:::
