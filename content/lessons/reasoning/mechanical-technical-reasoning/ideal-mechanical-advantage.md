## Core Concept

**Pulleys** and **levers** are simple machines. Many of them let you move a load with less force, by moving your hand through a longer distance. Some do not: a fixed pulley can just guide the rope, and a third-class lever trades force for distance.

The **effort** is the force you apply. The **load** is the force you work against, such as a weight. The **mechanical advantage** (MA) tells how many times the machine multiplies your force:

$$\text{MA}=\dfrac{F_\text{load}}{F_\text{effort}}$$

A machine is **ideal** when it has no friction and its ropes, pulleys and bar have no weight. Then the work you put in equals the work you get out. Work is force times distance, so a smaller effort must act through a longer distance:

$$F_\text{effort}\,d_\text{effort}=F_\text{load}\,d_\text{load}$$

So MA is also the effort distance divided by the load distance, each measured in the direction of its force. When the effort and load act at right angles to the bar, the points where they act move along curved paths, and these distances are in the same ratio as the lever arms. Real machines usually need more effort than the ideal value because of friction.

Weight is a force in newtons (N): $W=mg$, with mass $m$ in kilograms and $g=9.8\text{ m/s}^2$.

A **fixed pulley** is fastened in place. A single ideal fixed pulley that guides the rope has MA $1$.

## Examples

### #1: Fixed Pulley

An ideal fixed pulley lifts a $490\text{ N}$ load at steady speed. What pull is needed?

**Solution:**

Here the ideal mechanical advantage is $1$, so the rope tension must equal the load's weight:

$$F=490\text{ N}.$$

It changes the direction of the pull, not the size of the force.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Counting a Fixed Pulley as Extra Mechanical Advantage

> A fixed pulley that only redirects the rope adds no mechanical advantage.

The segment from a fixed pulley to your hand does not pull on the load. Only its direction changes.

### #2: Ignoring Friction

> Ideal MA is a model. Friction usually makes the real effort larger, and the weight of the parts can change it.

In a pulley system, friction and the weight of the moving pulley raise the effort.

A lever bar's own weight acts at its **center of gravity**, the point where all its weight seems to act. For a bar with the same thickness and material all along, that point is the middle. The weight can raise or lower the effort depending on which side of the fulcrum that point lies, and if a question gives no friction or bar weight to use, answer with the ideal value.
