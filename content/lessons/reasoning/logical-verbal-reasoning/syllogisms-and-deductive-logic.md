## Core Concept

**Deductive reasoning** asks whether a conclusion must be true if the starting statements are true. Those starting statements are called **premises**.

A deductive argument is **valid** when true premises would force the conclusion to be true. It is **sound** when it is valid and its premises are also true.

Deduction does not have to go from a broad statement to a specific one. It can use broad or specific statements. What matters is whether the conclusion must follow from the premises.

A **categorical syllogism** is a deductive argument about groups. It uses statements such as "all A are B," "no A are B," or "some A are B."

For group statements, circles can show the relationship:

- If **all A are B**, draw the A circle inside the B circle.
- If **no A are B**, draw the two circles apart.
- If **some A are B**, draw overlapping circles and place a mark in the overlap.

Use the circles to test the conclusion. A conclusion is valid only if the picture forced by the premises also forces the conclusion.

### Common Conditional Forms

The letters $P$, $Q$, and $R$ stand for whole statements. For example, $P$ could mean "the permit is approved."

"If $P$, then $Q$" means that whenever $P$ is true, $Q$ must also be true.

**Modus ponens** is valid:

1. If $P$, then $Q$.
2. $P$.
3. Therefore, $Q$.

**Modus tollens** is also valid:

1. If $P$, then $Q$.
2. Not $Q$.
3. Therefore, not $P$.

A **conditional chain** links statements in the same direction. If $P$ leads to $Q$, and $Q$ leads to $R$, then $P$ leads to $R$.

Two common forms are invalid:

- **Affirming the consequent:** If $P$, then $Q$; $Q$; therefore $P$.
- **Denying the antecedent:** If $P$, then $Q$; not $P$; therefore not $Q$.

These fail because $Q$ may happen for another reason. Knowing that $P$ did not happen does not prove that $Q$ did not happen.

Another valid form is **disjunctive syllogism**. If the premises say "either $P$ or $Q$" and also say "not $P$," then $Q$ must be true. In some questions, "or" may allow both choices to be true, but ruling out one still leaves the other.

## Examples

### #1: Valid Categorical Argument

All mammals are warm-blooded. A whale is a mammal. Therefore, a whale is warm-blooded. Is the argument valid? If it is valid, is it sound?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: nested circles with Mammals inside Warm-blooded, and a point labeled whale inside Mammals -->

**Solution:**

1. The whale is inside the mammal group, and the mammal group is inside the warm-blooded group. The conclusion must follow.
2. The premises are true, so the argument is sound as well.

### #2: Modus Ponens

If a permit is approved, the office sends a notice. The permit was approved. Therefore, the office sent a notice. Is the argument valid?

**Solution:**

This matches modus ponens: if $P$ then $Q$, and $P$ is true, so $Q$ follows. The argument is valid.

### #3: Affirming the Consequent

If a student studies hard, they will pass the exam. Maria passed the exam. Therefore, Maria studied hard. Is the argument valid?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: a circle labeled Students who study hard inside a larger circle labeled Students who pass, with Maria shown in the larger circle but outside the smaller circle -->

**Solution:**

Passing does not prove that studying hard was the only cause. Maria could have passed for another reason, so the argument is invalid.

### #4: Modus Tollens

If the alarm is working properly under these conditions, it will ring during a fire. It did not ring during the fire. Therefore, it was not working properly under those stated conditions. Is the argument valid?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: a circle labeled Alarms working properly under these conditions inside a larger circle labeled Alarms that ring during a fire, with the tested alarm shown outside the larger circle -->

**Solution:**

This matches modus tollens: if $P$ then $Q$, and $Q$ is false, so $P$ must be false. The argument is valid.

### #5: Denying the Antecedent

If Leo takes the bus, he will arrive by noon. Leo did not take the bus. Therefore, Leo will not arrive by noon. Is the argument valid?

**Solution:**

The conclusion does not have to follow. Leo may arrive by noon in another way, so this is the invalid form called denying the antecedent.

### #6: Valid but Unsound

All birds can fly. A penguin is a bird. Therefore, a penguin can fly. Is the argument valid and sound?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: a circle labeled Birds inside a larger circle labeled Can fly as the first premise assumes, with a point labeled penguin inside Birds -->

**Solution:**

1. The form is valid because the conclusion follows from the two premises.
2. The premise "All birds can fly" is false, so the argument is not sound.

### #7: Disjunctive Syllogism

Either the document is in Cabinet A or it is in Cabinet B. It is not in Cabinet A. Therefore, it is in Cabinet B. Is the argument valid?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: two overlapping circles labeled Cabinet A and Cabinet B, with the Cabinet A region crossed out and a document icon placed in Cabinet B -->

**Solution:**

The premise says the document must be in at least one of the two cabinets. Cabinet A is ruled out, so Cabinet B remains. The argument is valid.

### #8: Conditional Chain

If the records are complete, the application will be reviewed. If the application is reviewed, a decision will be sent. The records are complete. What conclusion must follow?

**Solution:**

1. Complete records lead to a reviewed application.
2. A reviewed application leads to a sent decision. Therefore, a decision must be sent.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

**Test validity before truth.** First pretend the premises are true and ask whether the conclusion must follow. Check whether the premises are actually true only after that.

**Follow conditional arrows in the same direction.** For a chain, move from $P$ to $Q$ to $R$. Do not reverse an arrow unless the logic gives you a valid reason.

**Use circles for group statements.** Words such as *all*, *no*, and *some* tell you how the circles should sit. If the conclusion is not forced by that picture, do not accept it.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: General to Specific

> Deduction is not defined by moving from a broad idea to a narrow one.

A question may use broad or specific premises. The test is whether the conclusion must follow.

### #2: True Conclusion Means Valid

> A true conclusion does not prove that the reasoning is valid.

An invalid argument can reach a true conclusion by accident. Check the path from the premises.

### #3: Reversing a Conditional

> If $P$ leads to $Q$, do not assume that $Q$ leads to $P$.

That mistake is affirming the consequent. The question may offer the reversed claim as a tempting answer.

### #4: Denying the First Part

> Not $P$ does not prove not $Q$.

$Q$ may still happen in another way. This is the denying-the-antecedent trap.

### #5: Valid Means Sound

> A valid argument can still have a false premise.

Soundness needs both valid reasoning and true premises. Check both when the question asks about soundness.
