## Core Concept

### Separating Premises from the Conclusion

An argument uses reasons to back up one central claim. A **premise** is a given fact or reason. The **conclusion** is the claim or action the argument tries to prove.

Read this library argument:

"The library should keep its Sunday hours. Some workers cannot visit on weekdays, and Saturday reading rooms are often full. Closing on Sunday would remove an available study option."

1. Ask what action the writer defends: keeping Sunday hours. The conclusion is **"The library should keep its Sunday hours."**
2. Ask why: workers miss weekdays, Saturday rooms are full, and closing removes an option. Those are premises.

:::pair #premise-as-conclusion
Right: the conclusion is **The library should keep its Sunday hours.**
Wrong: Some workers cannot visit on weekdays.
Worker schedules are a premise backing the recommendation, not the claim being proved. Ask what final action the reasons support.
:::

:::check
"Morning waits at the clinic are long, and afternoon slots stay empty. The clinic should offer afternoon bookings, since it already has staff on duty then." What is the conclusion?
:::answer
The answer is **The clinic should offer afternoon bookings.** The long waits, the empty slots, and the staff on duty are premises supporting that action.
:::

### Testing Necessary Assumptions by Negation

An **assumption** is an unstated claim the argument relies on. A necessary assumption does not prove the conclusion alone, yet the argument's stated reason breaks without it.

Use the **negation test**:

1. **Negate** the statement (make it false).
2. Test that negation against the argument's *stated reason*.
3. If the stated reason collapses, the assumption is necessary.

Test "Replacement lamps will provide usable light" against: "The library should replace its failed reading-room lamps so visitors can read there again."

- Negate it: replacement lamps will *not* provide usable light.
- Check the stated reason: visitors still cannot read there, so it collapses. The assumption is **necessary**.

:::why
Why test the stated reason instead of thinking of other benefits? A plan might look good for another reason, like saving electricity. Necessary-assumption questions test whether the writer's *given* reason still holds when the assumption fails.
:::

:::pair #helpful-not-needed
Right: the lamp argument needs **Replacement lamps will provide usable light.**
Wrong: Replacement lamps will be more energy-efficient.
Lower energy bills are helpful, yet negating that claim still lets visitors read under usable light. Reject helpful extras that leave the stated reason working.
:::

Watch extreme words like *every*, *all*, or *only* when partial help is enough. In "expanding peak-hour transit routes will ease traffic by shifting car trips to buses," shifting *some* car trips already helps.

:::pair #extreme-universal-claim
Right: the transit argument needs **New routes can replace some peak-hour car trips.**
Wrong: Every car user will stop driving at peak hours.
Negating "every car user stops" gives "not every car user stops," which still lets *some* car trips shift onto buses. Reject extreme rules when *some* is enough.
:::

:::check
"Rooftop solar will lower school bills by supplying power it would otherwise buy from the grid." Does this reason require solar panels to meet *every* school power need?
:::answer
The answer is **no**. Negating "meet every need" still lets *some* solar power replace paid grid power and lower bills.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Finding a Needed Assumption

Argument: "The office should replace its printed notices with website notices. This will lower printing costs while still giving every employee access to the notices." Which assumption is necessary?

(A) Every employee prefers reading on a screen.
(B) Every employee can access the website notices.
(C) Every nearby office uses website notices.

**Solution:**

1. Stated reason: lower costs while keeping access for *every* employee.
2. Negate (A): some workers dislike screens. They can still read notices, so the reason holds.
3. Negate (B): at least one employee cannot access the website notices. That breaks access for every employee, so **(B)** is necessary.
4. Negate (C): nearby offices do not use websites. This does not affect this office.

**Check:** Notice the word *every* in the stated reason ("every employee access"), which matches the *every* in (B).

### #2: Helpful but Not Necessary

Statement: "All employees who finish the training program will receive a promotion." Does this assume the company uses an electronic system to track completion?

**Solution:**

1. Negate the assumption: the company does not use an electronic system.
2. Check whether promotions can still work: yes, staff can track completion on paper forms.
3. The answer is **no**.

**Check:** An electronic tracker is convenient, yet helpful tools are unnecessary when another method works.

### #3: Checking a Causal Assumption

After Municipality Y began a waste-sorting program, landfill volume fell. The report concludes the program caused at least some of the fall. Which unstated assumption is necessary?

(A) The program was the biggest change that year.

(B) Landfill volume fell after the program began.

(C) Other changes are not the whole cause.

**Solution:**

1. Conclusion: the program caused *at least some* of the drop.
2. Drop (B): it repeats a stated premise rather than adding a missing assumption.

:::try
Finish the last step: negate (A) and (C) to choose the needed assumption.
:::answer
The answer is **(C) Other changes are not the whole cause.** Negating (C) means other changes caused the *entire* drop, leaving zero causal role for the program. Negating (A) only means a bigger change happened, which still lets the program cause part of the drop.

**Check:** The conclusion claims *at least some* role, not the *only* cause, so ruling out a full outside cause is enough.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

**Negate and ask: "Does the reason collapse?"** If the stated reason still works after you negate a choice, eliminate it.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- A supporting fact in the passage: treat it as a premise, not the conclusion. {#premise-as-conclusion}
- A helpful bonus: negate it to test whether the stated reason still stands. {#helpful-not-needed}
- Extreme words like *every* or *only*: check whether *some* already supports the reason. {#extreme-universal-claim}
:::
