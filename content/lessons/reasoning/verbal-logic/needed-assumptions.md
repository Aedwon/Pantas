## Core Concept

### Separating Premises from the Conclusion

An argument uses reasons to back up one central claim. A **premise** is a given fact or reason. The **conclusion** is the claim or action the argument tries to prove.

Find the conclusion of this argument:

"The post office should keep its Saturday counter open. Some workers cannot visit on weekdays, and the online service cannot take parcels. Closing Saturday would leave them no way to send one."

1. Ask what action the writer defends: keeping the Saturday counter open. The conclusion is **"The post office should keep its Saturday counter open."**
2. Ask why: some workers cannot visit on weekdays, online cannot take parcels, and closing leaves no way to send one. Those are premises.

:::pair #premise-as-conclusion
Right: the conclusion is **The post office should keep its Saturday counter open.**
Wrong: Some workers cannot visit on weekdays.
A worker's schedule is a premise backing the recommendation, not the claim being proved. Ask what final action the reasons support.
:::

### Testing Necessary Assumptions by Negation

An **assumption** is an unstated claim the argument relies on. The argument's stated reason breaks without a necessary assumption, though the conclusion may need more.

Use the **negation test**:

1. **Negate** the statement (make it false).
2. Test that negation against the argument's *stated reason*.
3. If the stated reason collapses, the assumption is necessary.

Decide whether "The new lamps will give enough light to paint by" is necessary to: "The school should replace the failed lamps in its art room so students can paint there again."

- Negate it: the new lamps will *not* give enough light to paint by.
- Check the stated reason: students still cannot paint there, so it collapses. The assumption is **necessary**.

:::why
Why test the stated reason instead of thinking of other benefits? A plan might look good for another reason, like saving electricity. Necessary-assumption questions test whether the writer's *given* reason still holds when the assumption fails.
:::

:::pair #helpful-not-needed
Right: the art-room argument needs **The new lamps will give enough light to paint by.**
Wrong: Replacement lamps will be more energy-efficient.
Lower energy bills are helpful, yet negating that claim still lets students paint in good light. Reject helpful extras that leave the stated reason working.
:::

Watch extreme words like *every*, *all*, or *only* when partial help is enough. In "The city should add a bike lane so fewer people drive at peak hours. Drivers can bike instead," some drivers switching already helps.

:::pair #extreme-universal-claim
Right: the bike-lane argument needs **Some peak-hour drivers will switch to bikes.**
Wrong: Every car user will stop driving at peak hours.
Negating "every car user stops" gives "not every car user stops," which still lets *some* drivers switch. Reject extreme rules when *some* is enough.
:::

:::check
"The school should add a ramp at its side door so wheelchair users can enter." Which assumption is necessary?

(A) Every wheelchair user will use the ramp.

(B) The side door is wide enough for a wheelchair.

(C) The ramp will be painted in the school colors.
:::answer
The answer is **(B)**. If the door is too narrow, the ramp cannot get wheelchair users in, so the stated reason fails. Negating (A) still lets some wheelchair users use the ramp, and negating (C) leaves entry unchanged.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Finding a Needed Assumption

Find the necessary assumption in this argument: "The office should replace its printed notices with website notices. This will lower printing costs while still giving every employee access to the notices."

(A) Every employee prefers reading on a screen.

(B) Every employee can access the website notices.

(C) Every nearby office uses website notices.

**Solution:**

1. Conclusion: switch to website notices. Stated reason: lower costs while keeping access for *every* employee.
2. Negate (A): at least one employee does not prefer screens. That employee can still read notices, so the reason holds.
3. Negate (C): at least one nearby office does not use website notices. This office is unaffected.
4. Negate (B): at least one employee cannot access the website notices. That breaks access for every employee, so **(B)** is necessary.

### #2: A Helpful Tracking System

Does "The company should promote staff who finish the training program, because it makes them better at their jobs" need the assumption that an electronic system tracks completion?

**Solution:**

1. Conclusion: promote staff who finish training. Stated reason: training improves their work.
2. Negate the assumption: no electronic system tracks completion.
3. Check the stated reason: paper forms can track completion, and training still improves work.
4. The reason holds, so the answer is **no**.

### #3: Checking a Causal Assumption

Find the necessary assumption. After a school started a free breakfast program, attendance rose. The principal concludes the program caused at least some of the rise.

(A) The program was the largest change that term.

(B) Attendance rose after the program started.

(C) Other changes did not cause all of the rise.

**Solution:**

1. Conclusion: the program caused *at least some* of the rise.
2. Drop (B): it repeats a stated premise rather than adding a missing assumption.

:::try
Finish the last step: negate (A) and (C) to choose the needed assumption.
:::answer
The answer is **(C) Other changes did not cause all of the rise.** Negating (C) means other changes caused the *entire* rise, leaving no part for the program. Negating (A) only means the program was not the largest change, which still lets it cause part of the rise.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

**Negate and ask: "Does the reason collapse?"** If the stated reason still works after you negate a choice, eliminate it.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- A supporting fact in the passage: treat it as a premise, not the conclusion. {#premise-as-conclusion}
- A helpful bonus: negate it to test whether the stated reason still stands. {#helpful-not-needed}
- Extreme words like *every* or *only*: check whether *some* already supports the reason. {#extreme-universal-claim}
:::
