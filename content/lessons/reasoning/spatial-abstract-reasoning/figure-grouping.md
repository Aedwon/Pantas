## Core Concept

**Figure grouping** questions present several figures and ask you to identify the one that does not belong with the others. The figures that do belong together share some common rule or feature; your job is to discover that shared rule, then find which figure breaks it.

Since the rule is not stated, approach this systematically by checking figures against a checklist of common shared-feature categories:

- **Number of sides or components**: do most figures share the same number of sides, dots, or lines, while one has a different count?
- **Symmetry**: do most figures have a line of symmetry (can be folded in half so both sides match exactly), while one does not?
- **Number of enclosed regions**: do most figures split their interior into the same number of separate sections, while one differs?
- **Curved vs. straight lines**: are most figures made entirely of straight lines (or entirely of curves), while one mixes both or uses the opposite type?
- **Shading pattern**: do most figures share a similar shading rule (like "always exactly one shaded part"), while one breaks it?

The correct approach is to find the rule that makes four (or however many) figures belong to one consistent group, leaving exactly one figure that clearly does not fit that same rule.

## Examples

### #1: Grouping by Number of Sides

Four figures are shown: a square, a rectangle, a rhombus, and a triangle. Which one does not belong, and why?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: four labeled figures side by side, a square, a rectangle, a rhombus (all four-sided), and a triangle (three-sided); the triangle is the odd one out -->


**Solution:**
1. Check the number of sides: the square, rectangle, and rhombus all have $4$ sides (they are all quadrilaterals).
2. The triangle has only $3$ sides.
3. The triangle is the odd-one-out, since it does not share the "$4$-sided" feature with the other three.

### #2: Grouping by Symmetry

Four figures are shown: a perfect circle, a square, an equilateral triangle, and an irregular, lopsided blob shape with no matching sides. Which one does not belong?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: four figures, a circle, a square, an equilateral triangle (all symmetric), and an irregular lopsided blob (no symmetry); the blob is the odd one out -->


**Solution:**
1. Check symmetry: the circle, square, and equilateral triangle can all be folded along at least one line so both halves match exactly (they are all symmetric).
2. The irregular blob shape has no line along which it folds evenly.
3. The irregular blob is the odd-one-out, since it lacks the symmetry the other three share.

### #3: Grouping by Number of Enclosed Regions

Four figures are shown, each a circle with straight lines drawn through it: one circle with $1$ line through the center (creating $2$ regions), one with $2$ lines through the center (creating $4$ regions), one with $3$ lines through the center (creating $6$ regions), and one with $2$ lines not through the center, creating $3$ uneven regions. Which one does not belong?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: four circles cut by straight lines, one with 1 line through the center (2 regions), one with 2 lines through the center (4 regions), one with 3 lines through the center (6 regions), and one with 2 off-center lines (3 uneven regions); the last is the odd one out (odd region count / lines not through the center) -->


**Solution:**
1. Check the pattern: the first three figures follow a consistent rule. Lines drawn through the center split a circle into an even number of regions ($2$, $4$, $6$).
2. The fourth figure's lines do not pass through the center, creating an uneven split (an odd number of regions, $3$), breaking the "lines through center create even regions" pattern.
3. The fourth figure (off-center lines, $3$ regions) is the odd-one-out.

### #4: A Subtler Grouping Rule

Four figures are shown, each a different shape, but each one contains exactly one small shaded (black) dot somewhere inside it. A fifth figure contains two small shaded dots inside it. Which one does not belong?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: five different outer shapes, four containing exactly one small black dot inside and one containing two black dots; the figure with two dots is the odd one out -->


**Solution:**
1. Check the shading pattern: four figures share the specific feature of containing exactly one shaded dot, regardless of their outer shape.
2. The fifth figure contains two shaded dots, breaking that specific count.
3. The fifth figure is the odd-one-out, since the shared rule here is about the dot count, not the outer shape at all.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

**Run through the checklist categories systematically** (side count, symmetry, enclosed regions, curve vs. straight, shading) rather than staring at the figures hoping a rule jumps out: a checklist approach finds subtle rules faster than random guessing.

**If the outer shape seems to have no shared pattern, check inside the figures** (like dot counts, internal line counts, or shading), since some grouping questions test smaller internal details rather than the overall outline.

**Confirm your rule against all the "belonging" figures before finalizing your answer**, not just two or three of them: a rule that fits three figures but fails on the supposed fourth "correct" one is not the actual rule.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Fixating on the Most Obvious Visual Difference

> The most visually striking figure is not always the correct odd-one-out: check the actual shared rule carefully rather than picking whichever figure "looks weirdest" at a glance.

A figure that looks unusual in size or orientation might still share the true underlying rule (like side count or symmetry) with the rest of the group, while a more "normal-looking" figure actually breaks the rule.

### #2: Assuming the Rule Is About Outer Shape When It's About an Internal Detail

> Some grouping rules are about details inside the figures (dot counts, internal lines, shading), not the outer shape at all: check both levels before deciding there's no pattern.

Missing an internal-detail rule (like "each figure has exactly one shaded dot") because you were only comparing outer shapes leads to picking the wrong figure as the odd-one-out.

### #3: Choosing a Rule That Only Fits Some of the "Belonging" Figures

> A valid shared rule must apply to all the figures that belong together, not just most of them: if your proposed rule fails on one of the supposedly matching figures, keep searching for a better rule.

Settling for a rule that fits three out of four "matching" figures (while ignoring that it does not actually fit the fourth) means you have not yet found the true shared pattern.
