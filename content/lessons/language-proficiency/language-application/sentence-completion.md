## Core Concept

The current public CSE verbal scope includes **sentence completion**. Whatever the exact item format, a good completion must fit both the sentence's **meaning** and its **grammar**.

Useful evidence includes:

**Context and discourse relations.** Words such as *although, because, therefore, however,* and *for example* can signal contrast, cause, result, or illustration. Treat them as clues to the relationship between ideas, not as automatic "positive/negative" switches.

**Grammar.** Check what part of speech or verb form the blank requires. Agreement, tense/aspect, complementation, articles, prepositions, and parallel structure can eliminate choices.

**Collocation and idiom.** Some words naturally occur together: *strong evidence*, *make a decision*, *interested in*. A choice can be grammatically possible but still be an unnatural or meaning-changing combination.

**Whole-sentence meaning.** After choosing a completion, reread the entire sentence. The best answer should produce a coherent meaning, not merely fit the words immediately next to the blank.

For multiple blanks, solving the more constrained blank first can be efficient, but there is no requirement to solve them in a fixed order.

## Examples

### #1: Contrast

"Although the weather forecast predicted heavy rain, the outdoor event was surprisingly ______."

(A) postponed (B) successful (C) canceled (D) rescheduled

**Solution:**

*Although* sets up a contrast between the bad forecast and the outcome. **Successful** provides that contrast and fits grammatically.

### #2: Cause

"Because the bridge was severely damaged, engineers deemed it ______ for public use."

(A) suitable (B) essential (C) unsafe (D) convenient

**Solution:**

Severe damage supports **unsafe** as the logical complement of *deemed it*.

### #3: Two Blanks

"Despite her ______ preparation, she felt ______ before the presentation."

(A) careless, confident (B) thorough, nervous (C) thorough, confident (D) minimal, nervous

**Solution:**

*Despite* signals an unexpected contrast. **Thorough, nervous** creates a coherent contrast: thorough preparation would ordinarily support confidence, yet she remained nervous.

### #4: Grammar Fit

"The committee ______ the proposal after months of careful review."

(A) approve (B) approving (C) approved (D) approval

**Solution:**

In this standalone sentence, **approved** produces the natural reading of one completed review-and-decision event and supplies a finite verb. The other choices would require a different grammatical structure. Do not infer a general rule that the phrase *after months of...* always forces past tense; context can support other time frames.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

**Predict before looking at the choices.** Decide what grammatical category and approximate meaning the blank needs.

**Use signal words as relations, not formulas.** *Although* signals concession/contrast; *because* introduces a reason; *therefore* marks a result or inference.

**Check collocation.** If two options mean similar things, the more idiomatic combination may decide the answer.

**Reread the completed sentence.** Make sure grammar, meaning, and tone all work together.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Choosing by Meaning Alone

> A semantically plausible word can still have the wrong grammatical form or collocation.

### #2: Treating Transition Words as Simple Opposite/Same-Direction Switches

> Discourse relations can be more nuanced than positive versus negative tone.

### #3: Assuming a Time Phrase Mechanically Determines Tense

> Tense/aspect depends on the sentence's timeline and context, not on one nearby phrase alone.

### #4: Solving One Blank in Isolation

> In multi-blank items, the choices usually need to work as a coherent pair or set across the whole sentence.
