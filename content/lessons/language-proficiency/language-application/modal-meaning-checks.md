## Core Concept

**Modal verbs** (such as *can, may, must, might, could, should*) add meanings such as ability, permission, requirement, or possibility. Check two things in every modal item: the meaning and the verb form right after it.

### Choosing the Modal for the Meaning

Common matches between modal and meaning:

- **Ability:** *can* ("Rosa **can** swim across the pool.")
- **Permission:** *may* or *can* ("You **may** leave the room now.")
- **Requirement or firm conclusion:** *must* ("By law, drivers **must** wear seat belts.")
- **Possibility:** *might* or *may* ("It **might** rain tomorrow.")

:::why
Why do policy and forecast items test *must* against *might*? Both take the exact same base verb after them, so grammar alone cannot pick the winner. Only the clue in the sentence (*a policy that requires the action* versus *only a possibility*) tells them apart.
:::

:::pair #modal-meaning
Right: "Under this policy, staff must register visitors at reception."
Wrong: "Under this policy, staff might register visitors at reception."
Here *might* shows only possibility. A policy that requires the action calls for *must*.
:::

:::check
Choose the modal that fits: After months of practice, Ben *(can / must)* now play the whole song easily.
:::answer
*Can*: "After months of practice, Ben can now play the whole song easily." Practice builds ability. *Must* would state a requirement or a firm conclusion.

**Check:** Swap in *is able to*: "Ben is able to play the whole song." The meaning stays the same.
:::

### After a Modal

A **base verb** is the plain verb with no *to* and no endings like *-s*, *-ed*, or *-ing* (*register, arrive, leave*). Core modals such as *can, may, must, might, could, should* are followed by the bare base verb, even when the subject is singular (*She must register*, not *must registers*).

:::pair #modal-plus-to
Right: "The delayed shipment might arrive tomorrow."
Wrong: "The delayed shipment might to arrive tomorrow."
Modals like *might* and *must* connect straight to the base verb. Do not copy *to* from phrases like *want to* or *have to*.
:::

:::pair #modal-verb-ending
Right: "Under this policy, staff must register visitors at reception."
Wrong: "Under this policy, staff must registers visitors at reception."
After a core modal, do not add *-s*, *-ed*, or *-ing* to the verb. Keep the plain base verb *register*.
:::

:::check
Choose the correct form: New staff *(should bring / should to bring / should brings)* a photo ID.
:::answer
*Should bring*: "New staff should bring a photo ID." *Should* takes the bare base verb *bring*, with no *to* and no ending.

**Check:** Swap the subject to *I*. "I should bring a photo ID" keeps the same verb *bring*, so no ending belongs there.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Matching Ability and Permission

Choose the modal that fits each sentence: (a) Ability: "Marco *(can / must)* lift that crate." (b) Permission: "You *(may / might)* sit in the front row."

**Solution:**

1. Sentence (a) asks for ability: "Marco **can** lift that crate."
2. Sentence (b) gives permission: "You **may** sit in the front row."

**Check:** Read each meaning aloud: "Marco is able to lift it" matches *can*, and "You are allowed to sit there" matches *may*.

### #2: Policy Requirement and Verb Form

Choose the correct form: "Under the new safety rule, technicians *(must wear / might wear / must to wear / must wears)* goggles inside the lab."

**Solution:**

1. The phrase *under the new safety rule* asks for a requirement, which fits **must**.
2. A modal takes the bare base verb directly, with no *to* and no *-s* ending.
3. Combine them: "Under the new safety rule, technicians **must wear** goggles inside the lab."

**Check:** Swap in *are required to*: "technicians are required to wear goggles." The meaning stays the same.

### #3: Meaning and Form Together

Choose the correct form: "Residents *(may park / may to park / may parking / must park)* here after six if they wish."

**Solution:**

1. Check the meaning first, then the word right after the modal.

:::try
Finish the last step: which form is correct, and what is the finished sentence?
:::answer
*May park*: "Residents may park here after six if they wish." *May* gives permission, while *must* states a requirement. *Park* is the plain base verb with no *to* or *-ing*.

**Check:** Swap in *are allowed to*: "Residents are allowed to park here after six." The meaning stays the same.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Split every modal check into two fast questions: does the modal match the meaning (rule vs chance), and is the next word the bare base verb with no *to* and no endings?

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- A policy or rule vs a mere chance: usually pick *must* for a requirement and *might* for a possibility. {#modal-meaning}
- *To* placed after *must* or *might*: drop *to* and connect the modal straight to the base verb. {#modal-plus-to}
- An *-s* or *-ing* ending on the verb after a modal: strip the ending back to the base verb. {#modal-verb-ending}
:::
