## Core Concept

### Joining Two Independent Clauses

An **independent clause** can stand alone as a complete sentence. Three common ways to join two independent clauses:

1. A period plus a capital letter: "Mika was the cashier on duty. She counted the receipts."
2. A semicolon: "Mika was the cashier on duty; she counted the receipts." (Use this when the ideas are closely linked and you do not want to add a cause or result word like *because* or *so*.)
3. A comma plus a coordinating joining word (*for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*): "Mika was the cashier on duty, and she counted the receipts."

A comma alone between two independent clauses creates a **comma splice**. Leaving out punctuation and joining words creates a **run-on**. Lowercasing the first letter after a period is also an error.

:::pair #comma-splice
Right: "Mika was the cashier on duty; she counted the receipts."
Wrong: "Mika was the cashier on duty, she counted the receipts."
Both halves can stand alone, so a comma by itself cannot join them.
:::

:::pair #run-on-or-case
Right: "Mika was the cashier on duty. She counted the receipts."
Wrong: "Mika was the cashier on duty she counted the receipts."
Never run two complete sentences together with no mark, and start each new sentence with a capital letter.
:::

:::check
Which punctuation fixes "The bus arrived late, the meeting started on time" without adding a joining word?
:::answer
The answer is **A semicolon or a period** ("The bus arrived late; the meeting started on time." or "The bus arrived late. The meeting started on time."). Both halves are independent clauses.
:::

### Transitions Like However and Dependent Clauses

Words like *however*, *therefore*, *meanwhile*, and *instead* are transition adverbs, not coordinating conjunctions like *and* or *but*. A comma before *however* cannot join two independent clauses.

Put a **semicolon** (or period) before *however* and a comma after it: "The company expanded into three regions; **however,** initial profits stayed low."

:::why
Why does a comma before *however* fail when a comma before *but* works? Only the seven coordinating words (*for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*) can join two independent clauses after a comma. Transition words like *however* modify the second clause, so the first clause still needs a semicolon or period before it.
:::

:::pair #however-splice
Right: "The company expanded into three regions; however, profits stayed low."
Wrong: Accept "The company expanded into three regions, however, profits stayed low" as No error.
Both sides of *however* are complete clauses. Put a semicolon before *however*, not a comma.
:::

A **dependent clause** (*because the office closed*) joins an independent clause differently. Add a comma when the dependent clause comes first ("Because the office closed, we left"), and usually skip the comma when it comes second ("We left because the office closed").

:::check
Identify the punctuation fix needed: "The file was complete, therefore, the clerk approved it."
:::answer
The answer is **Change the comma before *therefore* to a semicolon** (or a period with capital *Therefore*). Both sides are complete clauses, and *therefore* is a transition word.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Fixing a Comma Splice and Lowercase Start

Identify the error in each sentence:
(a) The office closed early, the storm became stronger.
(b) The office closed early. the storm became stronger.

**Solution:**

1. In (a), both halves can stand alone, so the comma alone creates a comma splice. Fix: "The office closed early; the storm became stronger."
2. In (b), the period ends the first sentence, so the next one needs a capital: "The office closed early. **The** storm became stronger."

**Check:** Read each half alone: "The office closed early." "The storm became stronger." Both are complete sentences, confirming they need a semicolon, period, or comma plus *and/but/so*.

### #2: Checking Clauses Around However

Identify the faulty segment: "The company expanded its operations into three new regions, however, initial profits remained below expectations."

**Solution:**

1. Check the left side: *The company expanded its operations into three new regions* can stand alone.
2. Check the right side: *initial profits remained below expectations* can also stand alone.
3. Because *however* cannot join two independent clauses with only a comma, the error sits in **into three new regions,** (change the comma to a semicolon).

**Check:** Read the fix: "The company expanded its operations into three new regions; however, initial profits remained below expectations." Both halves are complete clauses.

### #3: Joining Without Adding Cause or Result

Join these two facts into one sentence without adding a cause or result: "The server crashed at noon." "The technician restarted it."

**Solution:**

1. Check both thoughts: each has its own subject and verb and stands alone.
2. Words like *because* or *so* would add a cause or result, so avoid them.

:::try
Finish the last step: write one correct one-sentence version.
:::answer
The answer is **"The server crashed at noon; the technician restarted it."** A semicolon joins two independent clauses without adding a cause or result. A comma plus *and* also works.

**Check:** Test each side of the semicolon as its own sentence: "The server crashed at noon." "The technician restarted it." Both are complete, and no cause or result was added.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Cover the transition word (*however, therefore*) and read both sides. If both stand alone, join them with a semicolon or period.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Two complete clauses separated by only a comma: replace the comma with a semicolon, period, or comma + *and/but/so*. {#comma-splice}
- Two complete clauses with no punctuation, or a lowercase letter after a period: add the boundary mark and capitalize the new sentence. {#run-on-or-case}
- *However* or *therefore* between two complete clauses: put a semicolon before the transition word, not a comma. {#however-splice}
:::
