## Core Concept

*Excited* and *nervous* are **adjectives**, or words that describe. Here they tell how someone feels. Use *about* to name what brings on the feeling.

“Rosa is excited about seeing the chicks hatch.” Seeing them hatch is what she looks forward to. “Rosa is nervous about holding a chick.” Holding it is what worries her.

*Excited for* can show that you share someone's joy. “I'm excited for you” can mean you feel happy because something good is happening to that person. You may also hear *excited for* with an upcoming event in casual speech.

*Nervous of* can mean afraid of something, as in “nervous of large dogs.” *Nervous about* is a usual choice when someone worries about a task or what may happen.

## Examples

### #1: Looking Forward to an Event

Choose the best word. “Paolo is excited ______ his first trip to the planetarium next week.”

(A) to

(B) about

(C) on

**Solution:**

1. The trip is next week. Looking forward to it makes Paolo feel excited.
2. Use *about* to link his feeling to what causes it. Choose (B), *excited about*.

### #2: The Welcome Speech

Choose the best word. “Mia is nervous ______ whether she will remember her welcome speech.”

(A) about

(B) of

(C) on

**Solution:**

1. Mia worries that she might forget her speech. Use *about* before *whether* to show what she is unsure of.
2. *Nervous of* can name something a person fears, such as large dogs. Use *nervous about whether* for Mia's worry. Choose (A).

### #3: Two Feelings

Choose the best pair. “Inez was excited ______ the play, but she was nervous ______ forgetting her lines.”

(A) for, with

(B) on, about

(C) about, about

**Solution:**

1. The play is what Inez looks forward to. *Excited about the play* fits.
2. Forgetting her lines is what worries her. *Nervous about forgetting* fits. Choose (C).

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Ask “Excited about what?” or “Nervous about what?” Match each feeling to its own cause before choosing a pair.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Picking a Pair Too Early

> Check both blanks when the choices come in pairs.

In the play example, *excited for the play* can sound natural in casual speech. The same choice gives *nervous with forgetting*, which does not fit.
