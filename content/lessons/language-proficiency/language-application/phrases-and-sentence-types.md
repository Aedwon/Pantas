## Core Concept

**Classify** means name the sentence or word-group type by counting its clauses.

### Word Groups Inside a Sentence

A **phrase** is a word group without its own subject-and-verb pair, such as *after the lengthy staff meeting* or *during the annual staff review*. Words like *after* and *during* can open a phrase when only a noun follows.

A **clause** has both a subject and a finite verb:

- **Independent clause:** can stand alone as a complete thought (*the clerk resigned yesterday*).
- **Dependent clause:** has a subject and verb, yet begins with a joining word like *because, although, when,* or *if*, so it cannot stand alone (*because the clerk resigned*).

:::pair #phrase-vs-dependent
Right: *Because the clerk resigned* is a dependent clause (subject: *the clerk*; verb: *resigned*).
Wrong: Count *after the lengthy staff meeting* as a dependent clause.
*After the lengthy staff meeting* has no subject doing a verb, so it is a phrase, not a clause.
:::

:::pair #standalone-clause
Right: *The clerk resigned yesterday* is an independent clause.
Wrong: Count *the clerk resigned yesterday* as a dependent clause.
*The clerk resigned yesterday* has a subject, a verb, and no subordinating word, so it stands on its own.
:::

:::check
Is *before the morning shift* a phrase, a dependent clause, or an independent clause?
:::answer
*A phrase.* There is no verb inside *before the morning shift*.

**Check:** A clause needs a subject and a verb. *The morning shift* is a noun group, and no verb follows it.
:::

### Counting Clauses

Count how many independent and dependent clauses appear in the whole sentence:

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: the sentence "Although the counter was closed, the guard accepted the envelope, and the clerk recorded its arrival." split into three labeled bracketed boxes: [Although the counter was closed] = "dependent (1)", [the guard accepted the envelope] = "independent (1)", [the clerk recorded its arrival] = "independent (2)" -->

- **Simple:** $1$ independent clause, $0$ dependent clauses (*The guard opened the gate.*). Watch out: one subject with two verbs (*The guard opened the gate and greeted us*) is still $1$ clause.
- **Compound:** $2$ or more independent clauses, $0$ dependent clauses (*The guard opened the gate, and the visitors entered.*).
- **Complex:** $1$ independent clause and $1$ or more dependent clauses (*When the guard opened the gate, the visitors entered.*).
- **Compound-complex:** $2$ or more independent clauses and $1$ or more dependent clauses.

:::why
Why does "The guard opened the gate and greeted us" stay a simple sentence? A new clause needs its own subject-verb pair. One subject (*the guard*) sharing two verbs (*opened* and *greeted*) stays a single independent clause.
:::

:::pair #count-all-clauses
Right: "Although the counter was closed, the guard accepted the envelope, and the clerk recorded its arrival" has two independent and one dependent clause.
Wrong: Count it as "Two independent clauses with no dependent clause."
*Although the counter was closed* has its own subject (*counter*) and verb (*was*), so it counts as a dependent clause.
:::

:::check
Classify this sentence: "The clerk waited because the printer jammed."
:::answer
*Complex*. It has one independent clause (*The clerk waited*) and one dependent clause (*because the printer jammed*).

**Check:** Read each part alone: "The clerk waited." is complete. "Because the printer jammed." is not.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Phrase, Independent Clause, or Dependent Clause

Classify each word group as a phrase, an independent clause, or a dependent clause:
(a) during the annual review
(b) while the clerk checked the files
(c) the clerk checked the files

**Solution:**

1. Group (a) has no verb, so it is a **phrase**.
2. Group (b) has subject *clerk* and verb *checked*, and starts with *while*, so it cannot stand alone: **dependent clause**.
3. Group (c) has subject *clerk* and verb *checked* and stands alone: **independent clause**.

**Check:** Test each one with a period at the end: only (c) makes a complete sentence by itself.

### #2: Simple, Compound, or Complex

Classify each sentence:
(a) The manager reviewed the forms and signed the log.
(b) The manager reviewed the forms, and the clerk filed them.

**Solution:**

1. In (a), *the manager* is the only subject, sharing *reviewed* and *signed*. That is $1$ independent clause: **simple**.
2. In (b), *the manager reviewed* and *the clerk filed* are two separate subject-verb pairs joined by *and*: **compound**.

**Check:** Split each at *and*: in (a), *signed the log* lacks its own subject; in (b), both halves stand as complete sentences.

### #3: Counting Clauses in a Long Sentence

Count the independent and dependent clauses in this sentence and classify it: "The drivers stayed on route although the rain grew heavier, and the supervisor tracked each van."

**Solution:**

1. Check *although the rain grew heavier*: subject *rain*, verb *grew*, opens with *although*, so it is $1$ dependent clause.
2. Check *The drivers stayed on route*: complete thought, so it is $1$ independent clause.

:::try
Finish the last step: count *the supervisor tracked each van*, state the total clause count, and classify the sentence.
:::answer
Two independent and one dependent, so the sentence is compound-complex. *The supervisor tracked each van* is a second independent clause, making $2$ independent clauses plus $1$ dependent clause.

**Check:** Read each independent clause alone: "The drivers stayed on route." "The supervisor tracked each van." Read the third part: "Although the rain grew heavier" still needs a main clause.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Underline every subject-verb pair first. Count how many pairs can stand alone (independent) and how many start with a word like *because* or *although* (dependent).

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- A time word like *after* or *during* followed only by nouns: treat it as a phrase, not a clause. {#phrase-vs-dependent}
- A subject-verb group with no joining word: usually an independent clause, not a dependent one. {#standalone-clause}
- A long sentence with *although* and *and*: count every subject-verb pair so you do not miss the dependent clause. {#count-all-clauses}
:::
