## Core Concept

### Labeling an Infinitive Group by Grammar System

In this lesson, an **infinitive** is *to* plus the base form of a verb (*to obtain, to revise, to finish*). It carries no tense and does not change for singular or plural subjects.

Grammar books label the group *to obtain a certified copy* in *"The applicant hopes to obtain a certified copy"* in two ways:

1. **Basic school grammar:** calls it an **infinitive phrase** (the infinitive plus the words that go with it).
2. **Modern clause grammar:** may call it a **nonfinite clause** because it has a verb with no tense and a subject that is stated or understood (*the applicant* will obtain the copy).

Follow the system named in the question. Under either system, an infinitive group has no finite verb, so it cannot be an independent clause, a finite dependent clause, or a complete formal sentence.

:::why
Why do two books give different labels? School grammar requires a finite verb carrying tense to call a group a clause. Modern syntax counts a verb group with no tense as a nonfinite clause when its subject is stated or understood. Both agree it cannot stand alone as a sentence.
:::

:::pair #infinitive-not-finite-clause
Right: Under basic school grammar, in "The applicant hopes **to obtain a certified copy** before the office closes," the bold group is an **Infinitive phrase**.
Wrong: Classify *to obtain a certified copy* as an **Independent clause**, a **Finite dependent clause**, or a **Complete formal sentence**.
Although *the applicant* is understood to act, *to obtain* carries no tense. Without a finite verb, the group cannot be a finite clause or stand as a sentence.
:::

:::check
Using modern clause grammar, how is *to submit the report* classified in "The team plans to submit the report by Friday"?
:::answer
The answer is **Nonfinite clause**. *To submit* carries no tense, and the doer, *the team*, is understood. Basic school grammar would call it an infinitive phrase.
:::

### Fragments and Sentences

Adding extra time words, a passive participle inside the group (*revised* in *to have the policy revised*), or a second infinitive (*to revise ... and to notify ...*) never makes an infinitive group a complete formal sentence.

Compare a fragment with a sentence:

- Fragment: *"To revise the policy and to notify all employees."*
- Sentence: *"The unit plans to revise the policy."*

:::pair #long-infinitive-still-fragment
Right: *"The unit plans to revise the policy."* is a complete formal sentence.
Wrong: Pick *"To revise the policy before the next inspection."*, *"To have the policy revised before the inspection."*, or *"To revise the policy and to notify all employees."* as a complete formal sentence.
Look for a stated subject paired with a tense-carrying finite verb like *plans*; if no finite verb appears, the group is a fragment.
:::

:::check
Which is a complete sentence, and which word carries its tense: *"To verify every receipt before noon"* or *"The clerk plans to verify every receipt before noon"*?
:::answer
**The clerk plans to verify every receipt before noon.** *Plans* carries present tense. *To verify* has no tense, so the first group has no main clause.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Labeling Under Two Grammar Systems

In *"Ana hopes to finish the project,"* can *"to finish the project"* stand alone as a complete formal sentence? Give its label in basic school grammar and in a system using nonfinite clauses.

**Solution:**

1. *Hopes* supplies present tense and matches *Ana*.
2. *To finish* is an infinitive with no tense, so *"to finish the project"* cannot stand alone as a formal sentence.
3. Basic school grammar calls it an **infinitive phrase**; a system using nonfinite clauses calls it a **nonfinite clause** with *Ana* as the understood doer.

**Check:** *"To finish the project."* has no finite verb, so it cannot stand alone.

### #2: Spotting an Infinitive Fragment

Which entry is a complete formal sentence: *"To mail all the forms before the deadline."* or *"The office plans to mail the forms."*?

**Solution:**

1. In *"To mail all the forms before the deadline,"* *to mail* is an infinitive and no finite verb carries tense. It is a fragment.
2. In *"The office plans to mail the forms,"* *The office* is the subject and *plans* is the finite verb.
3. *"The office plans to mail the forms."* is the complete formal sentence.

**Check:** *Plans* becomes *planned* in past time, so it is finite.

### #3: Checking Tense and Label Together

Using basic school grammar, classify the bold words in "The manager wants **to review the budget** before Monday," and find which verb supplies the main tense.

**Solution:**

1. Find the two action words: *wants* and *to review the budget*.

:::try
Finish the last step: which one carries the main tense, and what is the bold group called?
:::answer
**Wants** carries the main tense and matches *The manager*. The bold group is an **infinitive phrase**: *to review* has no tense form.

**Check:** Shift to past time: *"The manager wanted to review the budget before Monday."* *Wants* shifts to *wanted*, while *to review* stays unchanged.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Check the verbs first. An action starting with *to* never supplies the finite main verb, and always match your label (*infinitive phrase* vs. *nonfinite clause*) to the system named in the question.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Infinitive group with an understood doer: label it by the system in the question, never as a finite clause or complete sentence. {#infinitive-not-finite-clause}
- Long or paired infinitive groups like *"To revise ... and to notify ..."*: look for a subject and tense-carrying verb before calling it a sentence. {#long-infinitive-still-fragment}
:::
