## Core Concept

### Five Core Word Classes

A **part of speech**, or **word class**, names a word's job in a sentence. Traditional school grammar uses eight classes. This lesson covers five, and the next part covers three more.

- **Noun**: names a person, place, thing, or idea (*Maria, city, book, freedom*). In "Maria bought a **book**," *book* names a thing.
- **Pronoun**: replaces a noun or name (*he, she, they, it, who*). In "Carlos finished **his** part, and **he** left," *he* stands for *Carlos*.
- **Verb**: states an action, event, or state (*laughed, happen, is, felt*). In "The children **laughed**," *laughed* tells what they did.
- **Adjective**: describes a noun or pronoun (*diligent, light, silly*). In "The **diligent** student smiled," *diligent* describes *student*.
- **Adverb**: modifies a verb, adjective, another adverb, or a whole statement (*loudly, very, remarkably*). In "She finished **quickly**," *quickly* tells how she finished. In "**Remarkably**, the library reopened," *remarkably* comments on the statement.

:::pair #adjective-vs-adverb
Right: In "The diligent student finished quickly," *diligent* is an adjective.
Wrong: Label *diligent* an adverb because diligence affects how she finished.
Ask which word it points to. *Diligent* describes the noun *student*, while *quickly* describes the verb *finished*.
:::

:::check
What part of speech is **laughed** in "The children **laughed** loudly at the jokes"?
:::answer
The answer is **Verb**. *Laughed* states the action the children did, while *loudly* tells how they laughed.
:::

### Same Word, Different Jobs

Many English words do more than one job. Read the sentence to check what the word does there.

Compare *light* in three uses:

1. "Turn on the **light**." *Light* follows *the* and names a thing, so it is a **noun**.
2. "Please **light** the candle." *Light* gives an action command, so it is a **verb**.
3. "The bag felt **light**." *Light* follows *felt* and describes *the bag*, so it is an **adjective**.

:::pair #class-by-habit
Right: In "She went for a run every morning," *run* follows *a* as an activity name, so it is a noun.
Wrong: Label *run* a verb because running is usually an action.
Look at *a run* after *for*. *Went* states the action, and *run* names the exercise session.
:::

:::check
What part of speech is **light** in "Turn on the **light** so we can see better"?
:::answer
The answer is **Noun**. *Light* follows *the* and names the lamp or its glow, while *turn on* is the verb.
:::

### Checking the Job, Not the Ending

Many adverbs end in *-ly*, like *quickly* and *loudly*. Still, *-ly* does not always mark an adverb. Words like *friendly*, *lively*, and *lonely* describe nouns, while adverbs like *often*, *very*, and *fast* (in *ran fast*) have no *-ly*.

:::why
Why can an *-ly* word be an adjective? English adds *-ly* to nouns like *friend* to form adjectives (*friendly*), and adds *-ly* to adjectives like *quick* to form adverbs (*quickly*). Checking which word is described separates them.
:::

:::pair #ly-ending-trap
Right: In "The friendly neighbor waved at us," *friendly* is an adjective.
Wrong: Label *friendly* an adverb because it ends in *-ly*.
*Friendly* describes the noun *neighbor*, not the action *waved*. Any word that describes a noun is an adjective.
:::

:::check
What part of speech is **very** in "The **very** tall tree blocked the sign"?
:::answer
The answer is **Adverb**. *Very* has no *-ly*, yet it modifies the adjective *tall* by telling how tall the tree is.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Pronoun, Adjective, and Adverb

Identify the part of speech of each word in bold: "Although Carlos was late, **he** finished the **difficult** task **quickly**."

**Solution:**

1. *He* stands for *Carlos*, so it is a **pronoun**.
2. *Difficult* describes *task*, so it is an **adjective**.
3. *Quickly* tells how he finished, so it is an **adverb**.

**Check:** Swap *Carlos* for *he* ("Carlos finished"), pair *difficult task* (adjective + noun), and pair *finished quickly* (verb + adverb).

### #2: Same Word, Three Jobs

Identify the part of speech of **light** in each sentence: "Turn on the **light**." "Please **light** the candle." "The bag felt **light**."

**Solution:**

1. First, *light* follows *the* and names a thing, so it is a **noun**.
2. Second, *light* directs the action, so it is a **verb**.
3. Third, *light* describes the weight of *the bag*, so it is an **adjective**.

**Check:** Swap words with one job: "Turn on the *lamp*" (noun), "Please *ignite* the candle" (verb), and "The bag felt *heavy*" (adjective).

### #3: Sentence Adverb and -ly Adjective

Identify the parts of speech of **remarkably** and **friendly**: "**Remarkably**, the library reopened." "The **friendly** neighbor waved at us."

**Solution:**

1. *Remarkably* comments on the whole reopening statement, rather than one noun.
2. A word modifying a whole statement in school grammar is an **adverb**.

:::try
Finish the last step: what part of speech is **friendly**, and which word does it describe?
:::answer
The answer is **Adjective**. *Friendly* describes the noun *neighbor*, telling what kind of person waved.

**Check:** Replace *remarkably* with *surprisingly* and *friendly* with *kind* ("The kind neighbor"); each keeps the same job.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Check what the word points to in that sentence. Nouns and pronouns name or replace things, verbs state actions or states, adjectives describe nouns, and adverbs modify verbs, adjectives, adverbs, or whole statements.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Familiar action word after *a* or *the*: check its job in that sentence before calling it a verb. {#class-by-habit}
- Modifier near an action: check whether it describes a noun (adjective) or a verb (adverb). {#adjective-vs-adverb}
- Word ending in *-ly*: test whether it describes a noun before calling it an adverb. {#ly-ending-trap}
:::
