## Core Concept

### Five Core Word Classes

A **part of speech**, or **word class**, names a word's job in a sentence. Traditional school grammar uses eight classes. This lesson covers five, and the next part covers three more.

- **Noun**: names a person, place, thing, or idea (*Maria, city, book, freedom*). In "Maria bought a **book**," *book* names a thing.
- **Pronoun**: often stands in for a noun phrase (*he, she, they, it, who*). In "Carlos finished his part, and **he** left," *he* stands for *Carlos*. In "**Who** called?" *who* asks which person is meant.
- **Verb**: states an action, event, or state (*laughed, happen, is, felt*). In "The children **laughed**," *laughed* tells what they did.
- **Adjective**: describes a noun or pronoun (*diligent, light, silly*). In "The **diligent** student smiled," *diligent* describes *student*.
- **Adverb**: modifies a verb, adjective, another adverb, or a whole statement (*loudly, very, remarkably*). In "She finished **quickly**," *quickly* tells how she finished. In "**Remarkably**, the library reopened," *remarkably* comments on the statement.

:::pair #adjective-vs-adverb
Right: In "The diligent student finished quickly," *diligent* is an adjective.
Wrong: Label *diligent* an adverb because diligence affects how she finished.
Ask which word it points to. *Diligent* describes the noun *student*, while *quickly* describes the verb *finished*.
:::

:::check
What part of speech is **sang** in "The choir **sang** softly at the ceremony"?
:::answer
The answer is **Verb**. *Sang* states the action the choir did, while *softly* tells how they sang.
:::

### Same Word, Different Jobs

Many English words do more than one job. Read the sentence to check what the word does there.

Compare *light* in three uses:

1. Noun, after *the*: "Turn on the **light**."
2. Verb, a command: "Please **light** the candle."
3. Adjective, describing *the bag*: "The bag felt **light**."

:::pair #class-by-habit
Right: In "She went for a run every morning," *run* follows *a* as an activity name, so it is a noun.
Wrong: Label *run* a verb because running is usually an action.
Look at *a run* after *for*. *Went* states the action, and *run* names the exercise session.
:::

:::check
What part of speech is **water** in "Please **water** the plants after school"?
:::answer
The answer is **Verb**. *Water* gives an action command, and *the plants* receive it.
:::

### Words Ending in -ly

Many adverbs end in *-ly*, like *quickly* and *loudly*. Still, *-ly* does not always mark an adverb. Words like *friendly*, *lively*, and *lonely* describe nouns, while adverbs like *often*, *very*, and *fast* (in *ran fast*) have no *-ly*.

:::why
Why can an *-ly* word be an adjective? English adds *-ly* to nouns like *friend* to form adjectives (*friendly*), and adds *-ly* to adjectives like *quick* to form adverbs (*quickly*). Checking which word is described separates them.
:::

:::pair #ly-ending-trap
Right: In "The friendly neighbor waved at us," *friendly* is an adjective.
Wrong: Label *friendly* an adverb because it ends in *-ly*.
*Friendly* describes the noun *neighbor*, not the action *waved*. A word that describes a noun is usually an adjective.
:::

:::check
What part of speech is **costly** in "The **costly** repair surprised us"?
:::answer
The answer is **Adjective**. *Costly* ends in *-ly*, yet it describes the noun *repair*.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Pronoun, Adjective, and Adverb

Identify the part of speech of each word in bold: "Although Rosa was tired, **she** finished the **long** report **calmly**."

**Solution:**

1. *She* stands for *Rosa*, so it is a **pronoun**.
2. *Long* describes *report*, so it is an **adjective**.
3. *Calmly* tells how she finished, so it is an **adverb**.

Marked: "Although Rosa was tired, **she** (pronoun) finished the **long** (adjective) report **calmly** (adverb)."

**Check:** Replace *calmly* with *slowly*: "finished the long report slowly" keeps the same job.

### #2: Same Word, Three Jobs

Identify the part of speech of **cool** in each sentence: "The room felt **cool**." "Please **cool** the soup." "Enjoy the **cool** of the evening."

**Solution:**

1. *Cool* describes *the room*, so it is an **adjective**: "The room felt **cool**."
2. *Cool* is what you do to the soup, so it is a **verb**: "Please **cool** the soup."
3. *Cool* names a thing after *the*, so it is a **noun**: "Enjoy the **cool** of the evening."

**Check:** Swap words with one job: "The room felt *chilly*" (adjective), "Please *refrigerate* the soup" (verb), and "Enjoy the *peace* of the evening" (noun).

### #3: Two Words to Classify

Identify the parts of speech of **luckily** and **lovely**: "**Luckily**, the bus arrived." "The **lovely** garden attracted visitors."

**Solution:**

1. *Luckily* comments on the whole statement *the bus arrived*, so it is an **adverb**: "**Luckily**, the bus arrived."

:::try
Finish the last step: what part of speech is **lovely**, and which word does it describe?
:::answer
The answer is **Adjective**: "The **lovely** garden attracted visitors." *Lovely* describes the noun *garden*, telling what kind of garden it was.

**Check:** Replace *luckily* with *fortunately* and *lovely* with *pretty* ("The pretty garden"); each keeps the same job.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Check what the word points to in that sentence. Nouns and pronouns name or replace things, verbs state actions or states, adjectives describe nouns, and adverbs modify verbs, adjectives, adverbs, or whole statements.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Familiar action word after *a* or *the*: check its job in that sentence before calling it a verb. {#class-by-habit}
- Modifier near an action: in the *diligent* / *quickly* pair, find whether the word describes the student (adjective) or the action (adverb). {#adjective-vs-adverb}
- Word ending in *-ly*: test whether it describes a noun before calling it an adverb. {#ly-ending-trap}
:::
