## Core Concept

### Collective Nouns

A **collective noun** names a group of people or things, such as *team*, *family*, *committee*, or *crowd*. Verb agreement depends on both the meaning and the variety of English.

1. In standard American English, when the group acts as one unit, use a singular verb: "The committee **has** reached a decision."
2. When a passage stresses members acting separately, especially in British English, a plural verb can fit: "The team **are** arguing among themselves."

Always follow any variety or one-unit instruction stated in the question.

:::pair #collective-unit-vs-members
Right: Treating the committee as one unit in American English, "The committee has reached a decision."
Wrong: Write "The committee have reached a decision" under a one-unit reading.
Even though a committee has many members, the American one-unit convention treats the whole group as singular.
:::

:::check
In standard American English, treating the family as one unit, choose the correct verb: The family *(is / are)* planning a trip this summer.
:::answer
The answer is **Is**. Treating the family as one unit making a shared plan takes singular *is*.
:::

### Indefinite Pronouns

An **indefinite pronoun** refers to people or things without naming them. Words such as *everybody*, *everyone*, *someone*, *somebody*, *anyone*, *nothing*, and *each* take singular verbs.

- "Everybody in the auditorium **is** welcome."
- "Nothing about the missing files **seems** to make sense."

Set aside any added phrase between the pronoun and the verb, such as *in the auditorium* or *about the missing files*. Modern English often uses singular *they* or *their* to refer back to *everybody*, yet the verb paired with *everybody* itself stays singular: "Everybody **has** their own opinion."

:::why
Why does *everybody* take a singular verb when it refers to many people? In English grammar, words built with *-body*, *-one*, or *-thing* point to members one at a time, so their verb stays singular even when singular *they* refers back later.
:::

:::pair #indefinite-singular-verb
Right: "Nothing about the missing files seems to make sense."
Wrong: "Nothing about the missing files seem to make sense."
Do not match the plural word *files* in the *about*-phrase. The subject is singular *nothing*, so the verb needs *-s*.
:::

:::check
Choose the correct verb: Everybody in both offices *(needs / need)* a new badge.
:::answer
The answer is **Needs**. Set aside *in both offices*; *everybody* is grammatically singular and takes *needs*.
:::

### Words Like All, Some, Most, and None

With *all*, *some*, *most*, *half*, and *a lot* followed by *of*, look at the noun inside the *of*-phrase to pick the verb.

1. Uncountable (mass) noun: "Most of the water **has** evaporated." (*Water* is one amount -> singular *has*.)
2. Plural countable noun: "Some of the cookies **are** gone." (*Cookies* are separate items -> plural *are*.)

With *none of*, uncountable nouns always take a singular verb ("None of the water **is** left"). With plural countable nouns, *none of* takes a singular verb in strict formal style ("not one") or a plural verb in general use ("not any").

:::pair #some-most-noun-after-of
Right: "Some of the cookies on the tray are already gone," and "Most of the water in the tank has evaporated."
Wrong: "Some of the cookies on the tray is already gone," or "Most of the water in the tank have evaporated."
Do not match *tray* or *tank*, or treat *some* as always singular or *most* as always plural. *Cookies* takes *are*, while *water* takes *has*.
:::

:::check
Choose the correct verb: Most of the milk in the pitchers *(has / have)* spoiled.
:::answer
The answer is **Has**. Set aside *in the pitchers*; *most* points to the mass noun *milk*, so choose singular *has*.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Collective Nouns by Context

Choose the natural form for each context: (a) Under standard American English treating the committee as one unit: The committee *(has / have)* reached a decision. (b) In British English stressing separate members: The team *(is / are)* arguing among themselves.

**Solution:**

1. In (a), the committee acts as one unit under American English, so choose singular **has**.
2. In (b), *among themselves* shows members acting separately, and British English permits plural agreement for that meaning, so choose **are**.

**Check:** Read each sentence with a matching substitute: "The committee *it* has reached" (one unit) versus "The team members *they* are arguing" (separate members).

### #2: Indefinite Pronoun with Singular Their

Fix the error in "Everybody have their own opinion" while keeping modern gender-neutral pronoun use.

**Solution:**

1. Find the subject of the main verb: *everybody*.
2. *Everybody* is grammatically singular, so change *have* to **has**.
3. Keep *their*, since modern formal English accepts singular *their* for an unspecified person.

Correct: "Everybody **has** their own opinion."

**Check:** Test the subject and verb alone: "Everybody has" fits singular agreement, while "Everybody have" does not.

### #3: Amount Versus Countable Items

Choose the correct verbs: Most of the water in the tank *(has / have)* evaporated, and some of the cookies on the tray *(is / are)* already gone.

**Solution:**

1. In the first clause, set aside *in the tank*. *Most* refers to *water*, which is a mass amount, so choose singular **has**.
2. In the second clause, set aside *on the tray*.

:::try
Finish the last step: what noun does *some* refer to in the second clause, and which verb matches it?
:::answer
The answer is **Are**. *Some* refers to the plural countable noun *cookies*, so the second clause needs plural *are*: "some of the cookies on the tray are already gone."

**Check:** Test each noun directly with its verb: "The water has evaporated" and "The cookies are already gone." Both agree.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Check collective nouns for one-unit versus separate-member cues, keep *-body/-one/-thing* pronouns singular, and look past *of* for *all*, *some*, *most*, and *half*.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Collective noun in American English acting as one unit: use a singular verb like *has* or *is*. {#collective-unit-vs-members}
- *Everybody*, *everyone*, or *nothing* before a plural phrase: ignore the phrase and keep the verb singular. {#indefinite-singular-verb}
- *All*, *some*, or *most* with *of*: match the noun after *of*, not a fixed rule for *some* or *most*. {#some-most-noun-after-of}
:::
