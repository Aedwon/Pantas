## Core Concept

### Collective Nouns

A **collective noun** names a group of people or things, such as *team*, *family*, *committee*, or *crowd*. Verb agreement depends on both the meaning and the variety of English.

1. In standard American English, when the group acts as one unit, use a singular verb: "The committee **has** reached a decision."
2. When a passage stresses members acting separately, especially in British English, a plural verb can fit: "The team **are** arguing among themselves."

Always follow any variety or one-unit instruction stated in the question.

:::pair #collective-unit-vs-members
Right: Treating the committee as one unit in American English, "The committee has reached a decision."
Wrong: Write "The committee have reached a decision" under a one-unit reading.
Even though a committee has many members, the American one-unit convention treats the whole group as singular.
:::

:::check
In standard American English, treating the class as one unit, choose the correct verb: The class *(is / are)* taking its final exam on Friday.
:::answer
The answer is **is**: "The class is taking its final exam on Friday." One unit taking one exam takes singular *is*.
:::

### Indefinite Pronouns

An **indefinite pronoun** refers to people or things without naming them. Singular pronouns such as *everybody*, *everyone*, *someone*, *somebody*, *anyone*, *nothing*, and *each* take singular verbs:

- "Everybody in the auditorium **is** welcome."
- "Nothing about the missing files **seems** to make sense."

Set aside any added phrase between the pronoun and the verb. Modern English often uses singular *they* or *their* to refer back to *everybody*, yet the verb paired with *everybody* itself stays singular: "Everybody **has** their own opinion."

:::why
Why does *everybody* take a singular verb when it refers to many people? English grammar treats words built with *-body*, *-one*, or *-thing* as singular, so their verb stays singular even when singular *they* refers back later.
:::

:::pair #indefinite-singular-verb
Right: "Everybody in the room is seated," and "Nothing about the missing files seems to make sense."
Wrong: "Everybody in the room are seated," or "Nothing about the missing files seem to make sense."
Do not use *are* because *everybody* means many people, or match the plural *files*. Both subjects stay singular.
:::

:::check
Choose the correct verb: Anyone with a late pass *(is / are)* allowed inside.
:::answer
The answer is **is**: "Anyone with a late pass is allowed inside." Set aside *with a late pass*; *anyone* is grammatically singular and takes *is*.
:::

### Words Like All, Some, Most, and None

With words such as *all*, *some*, *most*, *half*, and *a lot* followed by *of*, look at the noun inside the *of*-phrase to pick the verb. Compare one amount with separate items:

1. Uncountable (mass) noun, one amount: "Most of the water **has** evaporated."
2. Plural countable noun, separate items: "Some of the cookies **are** gone."

With *none of*, an uncountable noun takes a singular verb ("None of the water **is** left"). With a plural countable noun, either verb can occur, and formal style may prefer singular.

:::pair #some-most-noun-after-of
Right: "Some of the cookies on the tray are already gone," and "Most of the water in the tank has evaporated."
Wrong: "Some of the cookies on the tray is already gone," or "Most of the water in the tank have evaporated."
Do not match *tray* or *tank*, or treat *some* as always singular or *most* as always plural. *Cookies* takes *are*, while *water* takes *has*.
:::

:::check
Choose the correct verb: Most of the milk in the pitchers *(has / have)* spoiled.
:::answer
The answer is **has**: "Most of the milk in the pitchers has spoiled." Set aside *in the pitchers*; *most* points to the mass noun *milk*, so choose singular *has*.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Collective Nouns by Context

Choose the natural form for each context: (a) Under standard American English treating the jury as one unit: The jury *(has / have)* reached a verdict. (b) In British English stressing separate members: The staff *(is / are)* arguing about their own schedules.

**Solution:**

1. In (a), the jury acts as one unit under American English, so choose singular **has**.
2. In (b), *their own schedules* shows members acting separately, so British English can use plural **are**.

Correct: (a) "The jury has reached a verdict." (b) "The staff are arguing about their own schedules."

**Check:** "It has reached a verdict" (one unit); "They are arguing" (separate members).

### #2: Indefinite Pronoun with Singular Their

Fix the error in "Everyone have their own locker" while keeping modern gender-neutral pronoun use.

**Solution:**

1. Find the main subject: *everyone*.
2. *Everyone* is grammatically singular, so change *have* to **has**.
3. Keep *their*, since modern formal English accepts singular *their* for an unspecified person.

Correct: "Everyone **has** their own locker."

**Check:** Test the subject and verb alone: "Everyone has" fits singular agreement, while "Everyone have" does not.

### #3: Choosing Two Verbs

Choose the correct verbs: Most of the rice in the sacks *(has / have)* been packed, and some of the apples in the crate *(is / are)* bruised.

**Solution:**

1. In the first clause, set aside *in the sacks*. *Most* refers to mass noun *rice*, so choose singular **has**.

:::try
Finish the last step: what noun does *some* refer to in the second clause, and which verb matches it?
:::answer
The answer is **Are**. *Some* refers to the plural countable noun *apples*, so the second clause needs plural *are*: "Most of the rice in the sacks has been packed, and some of the apples in the crate are bruised."

**Check:** Test each noun directly with its verb: "The rice has been packed" and "The apples are bruised." Both agree.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Check collective nouns for one-unit or separate-member cues, keep *-body/-one/-thing* pronouns singular, and match the noun after *of* for *all*, *some*, *most*, and *half*.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Collective noun as one unit in American English: use a singular verb (*has*, *is*). {#collective-unit-vs-members}
- *Everybody*, *everyone*, or *nothing* before a plural phrase: ignore the phrase; keep the verb singular. {#indefinite-singular-verb}
- *All*, *some*, or *most* with *of*: match the noun after *of*. {#some-most-noun-after-of}
:::
