## Core Concept

### How Position Sets the Scope of Only

The **scope** of a modifier is the word or group it limits. Small words like **only**, **just**, **almost**, and **nearly** shift meaning when they move.

Watch what *only* limits in four positions:

1. *"Only she gave him the answer."* -> limits **she** (nobody else gave it).
2. *"She gave only him the answer."* -> limits **him** (nobody else got it).
3. *"She gave him only the answer."* -> limits **the answer** (nothing else).
4. *"The ticket admits a guest on Saturday only."* -> limits **on Saturday** (no other day).

In standard English, **position and context** both help show what *only* limits. Speakers often put *only* before a verb (*"She only gave him the answer"*) and rely on tone. In formal prose, place *only* right beside the word or phrase you limit so no reader guesses.

:::why
Why is *"position and context help show what only limits"* better than a rigid next-word rule? *Only* can limit nouns, verbs, amounts, or phrases, and mid-sentence placement before a verb appears in ordinary English when context is clear. Formal prose places *only* beside its target to remove doubt.
:::

:::pair #only-before-wrong-part
Right: *"The ticket admits a guest on Saturday only."*
Wrong: Write *"Only the ticket admits a guest on Saturday, according to the notice."* or *"The ticket admits only a guest on Saturday, according to the notice."*
*Only the ticket* limits what grants entry; *only a guest* limits who may enter.
:::

:::pair #rigid-only-rules
Right: Position and context help show what *only* limits in standard English.
Wrong: Claim *"It always limits just the next word, regardless of the surrounding context."* or *"It limits nouns alone, even when it appears directly before a verb."*
*Only* limits actions, amounts, or phrases as well as nouns, and context works with placement.
:::

:::check
What does *only* limit in each sentence: *"Only Ben ate the soup"* and *"Ben ate only the soup"*?
:::answer
The answer is **The eater; the food**. *Only Ben* limits who ate, and *only the soup* limits what Ben ate.
:::

### Almost and Nearly Before Verbs vs. Quantities

Putting **almost** or **nearly** before a **verb** usually means the action came close, yet did **not** happen. It can also mean the action was mostly done, so a position beside the amount is clearer:

- *"Mara almost missed the train."* -> she nearly missed it, so she caught it.
- *"The curator almost displayed all the vases."* -> unclear: she came close to showing them (none shown), or showed most of them.

Putting **almost** or **nearly** before a **quantity or frequency** (*every day, all the vases*) means just short of that whole amount or schedule:

- *"The cyclists rode to school almost every day."* -> they rode on nearly every day.
- *"The curator displayed almost all the vases."* -> nearly all of the vases were shown.

:::pair #almost-before-verb-vs-quantity
Right: *"The cyclists rode to school almost every day."* and *"The curator displayed almost all the vases."*
Wrong: Write *"The cyclists almost rode to school every day during the term."* or *"The curator almost displayed all the vases during the evening exhibition."*
Putting *almost* before the verb (*almost rode*, *almost displayed*) can say the action did not happen.
:::

:::check
Every tray nearly slipped from Tess's hands, and none fell. Which wording matches that scene: *"Tess almost dropped every tray"* or *"Tess dropped almost every tray"*?
:::answer
The answer is **"Tess almost dropped every tray."** *Almost* sits before the verb, so each drop came close and did not happen. In the other sentence, *almost* limits *every tray*, so she dropped nearly all of them.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Restricting a Day With Only

A coupon works at the café in March and in no other month. Which states that clearly: *"The coupon works only at the café in March"* or *"The coupon works at the café in March only"*?

**Solution:**

1. The restricted detail is the month (*March*).
2. *"Only at the café"* can limit the café or the whole place-and-time group, so the month stays unclear.
3. **"The coupon works at the café in March only"** clearly limits the month.

**Check:** Ask "What about April?" That sentence rules out other months.

### #2: Almost and How Often

Which says the club met on nearly every Friday, not every Friday: *"Almost all the members met every Friday"* or *"The club met almost every Friday"*?

**Solution:**

1. *"Almost all the members"* limits how many members met.
2. *"Almost every Friday"* limits how often they met.
3. **"The club met almost every Friday"** matches "nearly every Friday."

**Check:** Ask whether one Friday can pass without a meeting. Yes, so *almost every Friday* is right.

### #3: A Movie Night

A movie runs 120 minutes, and Mia watched all but the last five minutes. Which sentence says that clearly?

(a) *"Mia nearly watched the whole movie."*

(b) *"Mia watched nearly the whole movie."*

(c) *"Mia watched the whole movie nearly every night."*

**Solution:**

1. Mark what *nearly* sits beside in each option: (a) [watched], (b) [the whole movie], (c) [every night].

:::try
Finish the last step: choose the sentence, and say what *nearly* limits in each option.
:::answer
**(b) "Mia watched nearly the whole movie."** *Nearly* sits directly before *the whole movie*, so it limits how much she watched. In (a) it sits before the verb and can say the watching nearly happened, and in (c) it limits how often.

**Check:** Swapping in *"almost all"* keeps the meaning.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Box the small word and the word beside it, then check whether it limits the doer, action, quantity, or time.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Placing *only* before a noun when restricting a day or time: move *only* beside the time phrase. {#only-before-wrong-part}
- Treating *only* as locked to one word or only nouns: remember position and context both show its target. {#rigid-only-rules}
- *Almost* before a verb when you mean an amount: put it right before the quantity or frequency word. {#almost-before-verb-vs-quantity}
:::
