## Core Concept

### How Position Sets the Scope of Only

The **scope** of a modifier is the word or group it limits. Small words like **only**, **just**, **almost**, and **nearly** shift meaning when they move.

Watch what *only* limits in four positions:

1. *"Only she gave him the answer."* -> limits **she** (nobody else gave it).
2. *"She gave only him the answer."* -> limits **him** (nobody else got it).
3. *"She gave him only the answer."* -> limits **the answer** (nothing else).
4. *"The ticket admits a guest on Saturday only."* -> limits **on Saturday** (no other day).

In standard English, **position and context** both help show what *only* limits. Speakers often put *only* before a verb (*"She only gave him the answer"*) and rely on tone. In formal prose, place *only* right beside the word or phrase you limit so no reader guesses.

:::why
Why is *"position and context help show what only limits"* better than a rigid next-word rule? *Only* can limit nouns, verbs, amounts, or phrases, and mid-sentence placement before a verb appears in ordinary English when context is clear. Formal prose places *only* beside its target to remove doubt.
:::

:::pair #only-before-wrong-part
Right: *"The ticket admits a guest on Saturday only."*
Wrong: Write *"Only the ticket admits a guest on Saturday, according to the notice."* or *"The ticket admits only a guest on Saturday, according to the notice."*
*Only the ticket* limits what grants entry; *only a guest* limits who may enter. Keep *only* with *on Saturday* to restrict the day.
:::

:::pair #rigid-only-rules
Right: Position and context help show what *only* limits in standard English.
Wrong: Claim *"It always limits just the next word, regardless of the surrounding context."* or *"It limits nouns alone, even when it appears directly before a verb."*
Neither rigid claim holds. *Only* limits actions, amounts, or phrases as well as nouns, and context works with placement.
:::

:::check
What does *only* limit in *"Only she gave him the answer"* and *"She gave only him the answer,"* respectively?
:::answer
The answer is **The giver; the recipient**. *Only she* limits who gave it, and *only him* limits who received it.
:::

### Almost, Just, and Nearly Before Verbs vs. Quantities

Putting **almost** or **nearly** before a **verb** means the action came close, yet did **not** happen:

- *"Mara almost missed the train."* -> she nearly missed it, so she caught it.
- *"The curator almost displayed all the vases."* -> the curator nearly displayed them, yet did not.

Putting **almost** or **nearly** before a **quantity or frequency** (*every day, all the vases*) means most of that group or schedule:

- *"The cyclists rode to school almost every day."* -> they rode on most days.
- *"The curator displayed almost all the vases."* -> nearly all of the vases were shown.

:::pair #almost-before-verb-vs-quantity
Right: *"The cyclists rode to school almost every day."* and *"The curator displayed almost all the vases."*
Wrong: Write *"The cyclists almost rode to school every day during the term."* or *"The curator almost displayed all the vases during the evening exhibition."*
Putting *almost* before the verb (*almost rode*, *almost displayed*) says the action never happened. Put *almost* right before *every day* or *all the vases* when the action happened to most items or days.
:::

:::check
Which says Mara nearly missed the train yet caught it: *"Mara missed almost every train"* or *"Mara almost missed the train"*?
:::answer
The answer is **"Mara almost missed the train."** *Almost* before *missed* means the miss did not happen.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Restricting a Day With Only

A ticket admits a guest on Saturday and no other day. Which states that clearly: *"The ticket admits only a guest on Saturday"* or *"The ticket admits a guest on Saturday only"*?

**Solution:**

1. The restricted detail is the day (*Saturday*).
2. *"Only a guest"* limits who enters; *"on Saturday only"* sits beside the day.
3. The second sentence states the restriction clearly.

**Check:** Ask "What happens on Sunday?" Only *"on Saturday only"* rules out other days.

### #2: Near Action vs. Partial Frequency

Which says the cyclists rode on most days, not every day: *"Almost all the cyclists rode to school every day"* or *"The cyclists rode to school almost every day"*?

**Solution:**

1. *"Almost all the cyclists"* limits how many cyclists rode.
2. *"Almost every day"* puts *almost* right before *every day*, limiting how often they rode.
3. The second sentence matches "most days."

**Check:** Read *"almost every day"* alone: it means most days with some left out.

### #3: Preserving Almost All of a Group

The curator displayed nearly all the vases. Why do (a) *"Almost every curator displayed all the vases"* and (b) *"The curator displayed all the vases for almost the whole evening"* fail? Write the sentence that keeps the meaning.

**Solution:**

1. Option (a) puts *almost* on *every curator*, limiting how many curators participated.
2. Option (b) puts *almost* on *the whole evening*, limiting duration.

:::try
Finish the last step: write the sentence that keeps *almost* on the vases, and explain why it works.
:::answer
**"The curator displayed almost all the vases."** Putting *almost* directly before *all the vases* limits the number of vases shown, matching "nearly all the vases."

**Check:** Swap *"almost all"* back to *"nearly all"*: *"The curator displayed nearly all the vases."* Both give the exact same meaning.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Box *only, almost, just,* or *nearly* with the word or phrase right beside it, then check whether it limits the doer, action, quantity, or time.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Placing *only* before a noun when restricting a day or time: move *only* beside the time phrase. {#only-before-wrong-part}
- Treating *only* as locked to one word or only nouns: remember position and context both show its target. {#rigid-only-rules}
- Placing *almost* before a verb instead of *every* or *all*: put *almost* right before the quantity or frequency word when the action happened. {#almost-before-verb-vs-quantity}
:::
