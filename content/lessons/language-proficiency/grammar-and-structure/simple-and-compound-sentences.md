## Core Concept

### Counting Clauses

Here a **clause** has a finite verb and its subject (in a command, *you* is understood). An **independent clause** gives a complete thought and stands alone (*"The supervisor signed the request."*). A **dependent clause** cannot stand alone as a sentence's main clause. It often starts with a subordinating word such as *although, because,* or *when* (*"Although the supervisor signed the request"*), but the connector can be left out (*"I know we won"*).

A **complex sentence** has one independent clause and at least one dependent clause. A **compound-complex sentence** has two or more independent clauses and at least one dependent clause.

A **simple sentence** has **one independent clause** and **zero dependent clauses**. A simple sentence can still use *and*:

- **Compound subject:** two subjects sharing one verb (*"The dog and cat played together in the yard."*).
- **Compound verb:** one subject doing two verbs (*"The teacher prepared and rehearsed the play."*).
- **Prepositional phrases:** place or time groups with no subject-verb pair (*"Near the harbor, the old warehouse remained empty for several months."*).

:::pair #compound-parts-still-simple
Right: Classify *"The teacher and her students prepared and rehearsed the play."* as **Simple**.
Wrong: Classify *"The teacher and her students prepared and rehearsed the play."* as **Compound** or **Compound-complex**.
Two joined subjects and two joined verbs still share one clause. A compound sentence needs two clauses.
:::

:::check
How many independent clauses are in *"During the storm, the little boat near the dock rocked and creaked"*?
:::answer
The answer is **One**, so the sentence is **Simple**. *The little boat* is the single subject with the compound verb *rocked and creaked*; the other groups are prepositional phrases with no verbs.
:::

### Joining Main Clauses

A **compound sentence** has **two or more independent clauses** and **zero dependent clauses**.

Ways to join them:

1. A comma plus a **coordinating conjunction** (*for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*): *"She wanted to travel abroad, but she could not afford the airfare."* *"The rain stopped, so we went outside to play."*
2. A **semicolon** (;) alone between two linked independent clauses: *"The clinic reopened; appointments resumed."*

Do not count infinitives such as *to travel abroad* or *to play*, because they are not finite clauses.

:::why
Why does a semicolon make a compound sentence without *and* or *but*? A semicolon joins two independent clauses of equal rank. Both *"The clinic reopened"* and *"appointments resumed"* stand alone with a period, giving two independent clauses and zero dependent clauses.
:::

:::pair #but-so-semicolon-are-compound
Right: *"She wanted to travel abroad, but she could not afford the airfare."* and *"The clinic reopened; appointments resumed."* are **Compound**.
Wrong: Classify *"She wanted to travel abroad, but she could not afford the airfare."* as **Complex** or **Compound-complex**, or classify *"The clinic reopened; appointments resumed."* as **Simple** or **Complex**.
*But* is a coordinating connector, not subordinating, and *to travel abroad* is not a finite clause. A semicolon joins two full clauses into a compound sentence. It is not one simple clause or a subordinator.
:::

:::pair #compound-subject-not-compound
Right: *"The dog played, and the cat slept."* is a compound sentence.
Wrong: Pick *"The dog and cat played together in the yard."* or *"The dog played while the cat slept in the yard."* as a compound sentence.
*"The dog and cat played"* has only one clause with a compound subject. Using *while* makes *"while the cat slept in the yard"* a dependent clause, turning the sentence complex.
:::

:::check
How many independent clauses are in *"The shop opened early, but few customers came"*, and what joins them?
:::answer
The answer is **Two, joined by a comma and "but"**, so the sentence is **Compound**. *"The shop opened early"* and *"few customers came"* are independent clauses.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Checking Joined Subjects and Verbs

Classify *"The manager and her assistants reviewed and approved the budget."*

**Solution:**

1. *The manager and her assistants* is one compound subject.
2. *Reviewed and approved* is one compound verb sharing that subject.
3. With one independent clause and zero dependent clauses, the sentence is **Simple**: "[The manager and her assistants reviewed and approved the budget.]"

**Check:** Split at the second *and*: *approved the budget* has no subject.

### #2: Two Parts With a Semicolon

Classify *"The bell rang; the students left."*

**Solution:**

1. *"The bell rang"* has subject *the bell* and verb *rang*, so it stands alone.
2. *"The students left"* has subject *the students* and verb *left*, so it also stands alone.
3. Two independent clauses and zero dependent clauses make the sentence **Compound**: "[The bell rang]; [the students left]."

**Check:** Replace the semicolon with a period: "The bell rang. The students left." Both read as full sentences.

### #3: Classifying After a Deletion

Delete only *"Although the road was flooded"* from *"Although the road was flooded, the truck arrived, and the driver unloaded the supplies."* What structure remains?

**Solution:**

1. Remove the opening words: *"The truck arrived, and the driver unloaded the supplies."*
2. *"The truck arrived"* is an independent clause.

:::try
Finish the last step: name the remaining structure.
:::answer
The remaining structure is **Compound**: "The truck arrived, and the driver unloaded the supplies." *"The driver unloaded the supplies"* is a second independent clause with subject *the driver* and verb *unloaded*. Zero dependent clauses remain.

**Check:** Both halves stand alone as sentences, joined by *and*.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Split at a joining word (*and, but, so*) or a semicolon and test each side alone. If both sides stand as full sentences and neither holds a dependent clause, the sentence is compound. A subordinating word (*although, when, while, because*) is only a clue, so check each side for an embedded clause.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Using *and* to join two nouns or two verbs: verify each side has its own subject and finite verb before calling it compound. {#compound-parts-still-simple}
- Sentences joined by *but*, *so*, or a semicolon: test that both sides stand alone and neither holds a dependent clause, so you do not pick simple or complex. {#but-so-semicolon-are-compound}
- Swapping *and* for *while* or *when*: remember subordinating words create a dependent clause, not a compound sentence. {#compound-subject-not-compound}
:::
