## Core Concept

### Where Dependent Clauses Sit

In this lesson, count only clauses that have a subject and a finite verb. A **simple** sentence has one independent clause and no dependent clause. A **compound** sentence has two or more independent clauses and no dependent clause.

A **complex sentence** has **one independent clause** and **one or more dependent clauses**.

A dependent clause can sit at the front (*"Although the storm was severe, the town suffered minimal damage."*), at the end, or inside the main clause (*"The nurse, while she checked the records, called the patient."*).

Even **two dependent clauses** still make a **complex** sentence when there is only **one** independent clause. In *"[If the rain stops [when the guests arrive]], we will open the hall,"* the *when* clause sits inside the *if* clause. Both are dependent, and *we will open the hall* is the one independent clause.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: two-column clause inventory diagram: left column "Complex = 1 independent + 1 or more dependent" with example "[If the rain stops [when the guests arrive]] (dep, with a second dep clause inside) + [we will open the hall] (1 indep)"; right column "Compound-complex = 2+ independent + 1+ dependent" with example "[When the bell rang] (dep) + [the students grabbed their bags] (indep 1), and [they rushed out] (indep 2)" -->

:::pair #multiple-dependents-still-complex
Right: *"If the rain stops when the guests arrive, we will open the hall."* and *"Although the storm was severe, the town suffered minimal damage."* are **Complex**.
Wrong: Label the first **Compound-complex**, or label the second **Compound**.
Two dependent clauses with only one main clause make a complex sentence, and an *although*-clause is dependent, never a second main clause.
:::

:::check
Count the independent and dependent clauses in *"The driver, because the road was closed, took another route."*
:::answer
One independent clause (*The driver took another route*) and one dependent clause (*because the road was closed*) tucked inside it. That makes the sentence **complex**.
:::

### Joining Main Clauses

A **compound-complex sentence** has **two or more independent clauses** and **at least one dependent clause**.

Mark the clauses in *"When the bell rang, the students grabbed their bags, and they rushed out the door"*:

"[When the bell rang] (dependent), [the students grabbed their bags] (independent 1), and [they rushed out the door] (independent 2)."

:::pair #missed-second-main-or-dep
Right: *"When the bell rang, the students grabbed their bags, and they rushed out the door."* is **Compound-complex**.
Wrong: Label it **Compound** or **Complex**.
*Compound* skips the opening *when*-clause. *Complex* merges *"they rushed out the door"* into the first main clause, missing the subject *they*.
:::

:::check
Count the independent and dependent clauses in *"The tide rose, and the boats stayed at the dock because the wind was strong."*
:::answer
Two independent clauses (*The tide rose* and *the boats stayed at the dock*) and one dependent clause (*because the wind was strong*). That makes the sentence **compound-complex**.
:::

### Clue Words That Can Start Phrases or Clauses

*After, before,* and *since* start phrases or clauses. Ask what *after* introduces in each sentence:

- *"[After rehearsal], the musicians left."* -> phrase, **Simple**.
- *"[After rehearsal ended], the musicians left the hall."* -> dependent clause, **Complex**.

:::pair #after-phrase-vs-clause
Right: *"After rehearsal, the musicians left."* is simple; *"After rehearsal ended, the musicians left the hall."* is complex.
Wrong: Pick *"After rehearsal ended, the musicians left the hall."* as a simple sentence starting with *After*.
*After* can introduce a phrase or a clause. Spot the verb *ended* after *rehearsal*; in these examples, once a subject and verb appear, the group is a dependent clause.
:::

:::check
Which sentence is complex: *"The hosts set the table before noon."* or *"The hosts set the table before the guests arrived."*?
:::answer
The answer is the second: *"The hosts set the table [before the guests arrived]."* *Before* introduces *the guests* + *arrived*, a dependent clause. *Before noon* has no verb, so the first sentence is simple.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Explaining Why a Sentence Is Complex

Which clause inventory explains why *"Because the printer jammed, the staff left early"* is complex?

**Solution:**

1. *"Because the printer jammed"* has subject *the printer* and verb *jammed*, made dependent by *Because*.
2. *"The staff left early"* has no subordinating word and stands alone.
3. So it has **one independent clause and one dependent clause**: "[Because the printer jammed] (dependent), [the staff left early] (independent)."

**Check:** Drop *Because* and *"The printer jammed"* stands alone.

### #2: Simple vs. Complex Beginning With Since

Which sentence is simple: *"Since Monday, the clinic has opened early."* or *"Since the clerk resigned, the clinic has opened late."*?

**Solution:**

1. *"Since Monday"* has no verb, so *"Since Monday, the clinic has opened early."* has one clause: **Simple**.
2. *"Since the clerk resigned"* has subject *the clerk* and verb *resigned*, so it is dependent: *"Since the clerk resigned, the clinic has opened late."* is **Complex**.

**Check:** Sentence 1 has only one subject-verb pair.

### #3: Counting Clauses in a Long Sentence

Classify: *"When the alarm sounded, the guards locked the gate, and the visitors waited outside, but the manager stayed calm."*

**Solution:**

1. *When the alarm sounded* is **one dependent clause**.
2. *The guards locked the gate* is independent clause 1.
3. *The visitors waited outside* is independent clause 2.

:::try
Finish the last step: find the last clause, then combine the counts to name the sentence type.
:::answer
The last clause is *the manager stayed calm*, independent clause 3. So the sentence has three independent clauses and one dependent clause: "[When the alarm sounded] (dependent), [the guards locked the gate] (independent 1), and [the visitors waited outside] (independent 2), but [the manager stayed calm] (independent 3)." It is **Compound-complex**.

**Check:** Remove *"When the alarm sounded,"* and three full statements remain.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Keep two counts, independent clauses and dependent clauses, counting only clauses with a subject and a finite verb.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Two dependent clauses with one main clause: count main clauses separately so you pick complex, not compound-complex. {#multiple-dependents-still-complex}
- Subordinating word plus *and* or *but*: check for a second subject so you do not pick complex or compound. {#missed-second-main-or-dep}
- Opening word like *after*, *before*, or *since*: in this lesson, count a dependent clause only when a subject and finite verb follow. {#after-phrase-vs-clause}
:::
