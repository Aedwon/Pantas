## Core Concept

### Requirement, Advice, Ban, and Optional Action

Keep four closely related modal meanings separate in rules and notices. Typical forms:

1. **Requirement:** *must* and *have to* state a rule: "Applicants **have to** bring a photo pass." (Use *has to* for singular third person and *had to* for the past.)
2. **Advice:** *should* recommends an action: "Applicants **should** arrive ten minutes early."
3. **Ban (forbidden):** *must not* forbids an action: "Applicants **must not** bring phones inside."
4. **Optional (not required):** *do not have to* (or *does not have to*) leaves the choice open: "Applicants **do not have to** bring a sheet."

:::why
Why do *must* and *have to* split apart with *not*? *Must not* bans an action completely, while *do not have to* removes the rule so you may skip it or do it.
:::

:::pair #optional-vs-banned
Right: When bringing a printed reference sheet is optional, write "Applicants do not have to bring a sheet."
Wrong: Write "Applicants must not bring a printed sheet," "Applicants have to bring a printed reference sheet," or "Applicants should bring a printed reference sheet."
*Must not* turns optional into a ban, *have to* makes it a rule, and *should* makes it advice. Here only *do not have to* means optional.
:::

:::check
The host hopes guests wear a jacket but lets anyone in. Choose: "Guests *(must / should)* wear a jacket."
:::answer
The answer is **Should**. *Should* gives advice, whereas *must* makes it required.
:::

### Possibility with May, Might, and Could

Use **may**, **might**, or **could** when an event is possible but not certain:

- "The delivery **might** arrive after the office closes."
- "Traffic **may** delay the bus."

Context shows when these mean possibility rather than permission or past ability.

:::pair #might-shows-possibility
Right: In "The delivery might arrive after the office closes," *might* expresses possibility.
Wrong: Read *might* in this sentence as a strict requirement or as permission to enter.
Here *might* shows the late arrival could happen, not that anyone is required to act or allowed to enter.
:::

:::check
What does *may* express in "It may rain later": permission or possibility?
:::answer
The answer is **Possibility**. The speaker is predicting what could happen with the weather.
:::

### A Second Meaning of Must

Besides giving a rule, **must** can express a **strong conclusion** based on clues:

- "The reservation sheet is signed and the lights are on, so someone **must** be using the room."

The speaker is not ordering anyone to use the room. Instead, the clues show what is very likely true right now.

:::pair #must-conclusion-vs-rule
Right: In "Could I use the meeting room?" *could* asks permission. In "The sheet is signed and the lights are on, so someone must be using the room," *must* states a strong conclusion.
Wrong: Read *must* as an obligation to use the room, or read *could* as general ability in the past.
The signed sheet and lit room are evidence for what is happening now, not a rule. And *Could I use...* asks polite permission.
:::

:::check
In "Ana's bag is on her chair, so she must be here," is *must* a rule or a conclusion from evidence?
:::answer
The answer is **A conclusion from evidence**. Her bag on the chair is the clue supporting that conclusion.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Matching an Optional Notice

A notice states that bringing a printed reference sheet is optional. Choose the sentence that preserves that meaning: "Applicants must not bring a printed sheet," "Applicants have to bring a printed reference sheet," "Applicants should bring a printed reference sheet," or "Applicants do not have to bring a sheet."

**Solution:**

1. Define *optional*: allowed to bring it, and allowed to skip it.
2. Rule out *must not* (ban), *have to* (rule), and *should* (advice).
3. Choose **"Applicants do not have to bring a sheet."**

**Check:** Bringing a sheet is allowed (not *must not*), not required (not *have to*), and *should* adds advice the notice does not state.

### #2: Identifying Possibility in Context

In "The delivery might arrive after the office closes," determine what *might* expresses.

**Solution:**

1. Check the subject *the delivery*: an event arriving, not a person asking to enter or showing past skill.
2. Match *might* before *arrive*: it presents late arrival as uncertain, expressing **possibility**.

**Check:** Replace *might* with *may* ("The delivery may arrive after the office closes"): the uncertain prediction stays intact, confirming possibility.

### #3: Two Modals in One Notice

Read: "The lights are off and the door is locked, so the shop must be closed. The owner might open it at noon." What do *must* and *might* express?

**Solution:**

1. Split the notice into its two sentences.

:::try
Finish the job: what do *must* and *might* express?
:::answer
*Must* is **a strong conclusion from evidence**: the dark lights and locked door show what is very likely true now. *Might* is **possibility**: opening at noon is uncertain.

**Check:** Replace *must be closed* with *is probably closed*: the meaning fits the clues, whereas *has to be closed* would state a rule.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Separate *must not* (ban) from *do not have to* (optional), and ask whether *must* gives a rule or draws a conclusion from clues.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Optional action vs. ban or rule: use *do not have to* for optional, since *must not*, *have to*, and *should* change the meaning. {#optional-vs-banned}
- *May*, *might*, or *could* in a prediction: read it as possibility when no permission or past skill fits. {#might-shows-possibility}
- *Must* with evidence clues (*lights are on*, *so*): read it as a strong conclusion, not an obligation. {#must-conclusion-vs-rule}
:::
