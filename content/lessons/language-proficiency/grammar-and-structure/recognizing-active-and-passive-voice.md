## Core Concept

### Active Versus Passive Voice

First find the **subject** and the **verb**. In **active voice**, the subject does the action: "The dog **chased** the ball."

In **passive voice**, the subject receives the action: "The ball **was chased** by the dog." The **agent** (the doer, *the dog*) may follow *by* or be left out.

Judge voice by the grammatical subject, not by the object or the doer after *by*:

- "The dog chased the ball." (Subject = *the dog*, which acts -> active.)
- "The ball was chased by the dog." (Subject = *the ball*, which receives -> passive.)

:::pair #check-subject-not-by-phrase
Right: "The dog chased the ball" is active voice, and "The ball was chased by the dog" is passive voice.
Wrong: Call "The dog chased the ball" passive voice because the ball receives the action, or call "The ball was chased by the dog" active voice because the dog is the actor.
Look at the grammatical subject before the verb, not the object or the doer after *by*.
:::

:::check
Is "The chef cooked the meal" active or passive voice?
:::answer
The answer is **Active voice**. The subject *the chef* performs the cooking action.
:::

### The Be + Past Participle Pattern

Passive voice usually uses **be + past participle** (*is checked*, *was chased*, *had been submitted*, *have been changed*). The helper keeps the tense clear:

- "The report **had already been submitted** by the intern." (Past perfect + passive voice.)

Watch out for **linking constructions**. A form of *be* plus an adjective describes a state, not an action: "She **is** happy" links *she* to the adjective *happy*.

:::pair #be-plus-adjective
Right: "She is happy" is not passive because *is* links the subject to an adjective.
Wrong: Think *happy* must be a past participle whenever it follows a form of *be*.
Check whether the word after *be* names an action done to the subject (*was invited*) or describes a state (*is happy*).
:::

:::check
Which sentence is passive: "The package was heavy" or "The package was delivered"?
:::answer
The answer is **"The package was delivered."** *Delivered* is a past participle naming an action done to the package, while *heavy* is an adjective.
:::

### When Passive Voice Fits Best

Use passive voice when the agent is unknown, obvious, or less important than the result: "The access codes **have been changed**." If no one knows who changed them, passive voice states the fact without guessing a doer.

:::why
Why do formal notices often use passive voice? When readers need to know what changed rather than who changed it, naming an unverified actor would add false detail.
:::

:::pair #keep-unknown-agent-passive
Right: When the doer is unknown and the result matters now, write "The access codes have been changed."
Wrong: Invent a doer ("The administrator has changed the access codes") or use simple past ("The access codes were changed").
Never invent an actor when the doer is unknown. Keep *have been changed* (present perfect passive) to link the change to now, rather than simple past *were changed*.
:::

:::check
No one knows who misplaced the files. Which fits better: "Someone lost the files" or "The files were lost"?
:::answer
The answer is **"The files were lost."** Passive voice reports what happened without inventing an unknown actor.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Active, Passive, and Linking Clauses

Label each sentence: (a) "The dog chased the ball." (b) "The ball was chased by the dog." (c) "She is happy."

**Solution:**

1. In (a), the subject *the dog* does the chasing -> **active voice**.
2. In (b), the subject *the ball* receives the chasing (*was chased*) -> **passive voice**.
3. In (c), *is* links *she* to the adjective *happy* -> **linking construction**, not passive.

**Check:** Test each verb group: *chased* has no *be* helper (active), *was chased* pairs *be* with a past participle (passive), and *is happy* pairs *be* with an adjective (*very happy*).

### #2: Tense and Voice Together

In "By the time the manager reviewed it, the report had already been submitted by the intern," identify the tense and voice of *had already been submitted*.

**Solution:**

1. Find the verb group: *had ... been submitted*.
2. Check the first helper: *had* plus *been* shows **past perfect**.
3. Check the subject *the report*: it received the submission, so *been submitted* is **passive voice**.

**Check:** Active form "the intern had already submitted the report" keeps *had submitted* (past perfect) with *the intern* acting.

### #3: Formal Notice Without an Agent

A formal notice must report that a change to the access codes is complete and matters now, with an unknown doer. Choose between: "The access codes were changed," "The administrator has changed the access codes," and "The access codes have been changed."

**Solution:**

1. Rule out simple past *were changed* because the change matters now (present perfect).
2. Rule out *the administrator* because the doer is unknown.

:::try
Finish the last step: which sentence remains, and why does it fit?
:::answer
The answer is **"The access codes have been changed."** *Have been changed* is present perfect passive, linking the completed change to now without inventing an actor.

**Check:** Confirm all three rules: *have been* (present perfect), *been changed* + receiving subject (passive), and no invented *by* phrase or administrator.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

First find the subject and ask whether it acts or receives the action. Next check the full verb group for *be* (or *get*) plus a past participle rather than an adjective.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- An object after the verb or an actor after *by*: judge voice from the grammatical subject, not other nouns. {#check-subject-not-by-phrase}
- A form of *be* before a describing word like *happy*: test whether it is an adjective (linking), not a passive participle. {#be-plus-adjective}
- Unknown doer in a formal notice: keep passive voice in the right tense instead of inventing an actor. {#keep-unknown-agent-passive}
:::
