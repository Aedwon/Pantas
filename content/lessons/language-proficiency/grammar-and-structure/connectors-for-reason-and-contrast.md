## Core Concept

### Reason and Contrast Connectors, and Avoiding Double Connectors

A **connector** joins clauses and shows how their ideas relate:

- **Reason** (such as *because, since*): explains why something happened. *"We stayed inside because it was raining."*
- **Contrast** (such as *although, even though, though*): introduces a fact that goes against the expected result. *"Although she was tired, she finished her homework."*

**Use one connector for one link.** A subordinating connector (*although, because*) already joins one dependent clause to one main clause. A coordinating connector (*but, so*) joins two independent clauses. Using both for one link is an error, as in *"Although it rained, but we walked"* or *"Because the store was closed, so we went home."* Keep one connector for the link: *"Although it rained, we walked"* or *"It rained, but we walked."*

:::why
Why does *"Because it rained, so we stayed inside"* fail? *Because* makes *"it rained"* wait for a main clause, while *so* expects a main clause before it. Drop *so* so *"we stayed inside"* serves as the main clause.
:::

:::check
Fix *"Although the team practiced, but it lost"* in two ways: keep *although*, or keep *but*.
:::answer
Keep *although*: **"Although the team practiced, it lost."** Keep *but*: **"The team practiced, but it lost."** Each version has one connector for the one link.
:::

### Comma Placement by Clause Order

Compare comma placement by order:

1. **Dependent clause first:** Usually add a comma after it: *"Because it was raining, we stayed inside."*
2. **Essential reason second:** Skip the comma: *"We stayed inside because it was raining."*

:::pair #comma-by-clause-order
Right: *"Because it was raining, we stayed inside."* (comma after *raining*) and *"We stayed inside because it was raining."* (no comma before *because*).
Wrong: Claim *"No: the comma should be removed entirely"* for the opening clause, or *"Yes: a comma is always required regardless of clause order"* for the second.
Comma use depends on clause order. Usually put a comma after an opening dependent clause; skip it before a following essential *because* clause.
:::

:::check
Where does a comma belong in *"Although the shop was open we left without buying anything"*?
:::answer
The answer is **After *open***. *"Although the shop was open, we left without buying anything."* An opening dependent clause usually takes a comma before the main clause.
:::

### Completing a Dependent-Clause Fragment

In formal writing, a dependent clause standing alone with a period is a **fragment**, an incomplete sentence: *"Although the teacher explained the lesson twice."*

To fix a dependent-clause fragment, attach a true **independent clause** with a subject, finite verb, and no subordinating word at the front. Adding another dependent clause (*while, because*), a noun phrase, or a prepositional phrase still leaves a fragment.

:::pair #fragment-is-not-sentence
Right: In formal prose, *"Although the teacher explained the lesson twice"* is **a dependent-clause fragment**.
Wrong: Call *"Although the teacher explained the lesson twice"* **a complete independent sentence** or **a run-on with two main clauses**.
*The teacher explained* has a subject and verb, yet *Although* turns the single clause into a fragment waiting for a main clause. One clause cannot be a run-on.
:::

:::pair #two-dependent-clauses
Right: *"Because the store was closed, we went home."*
Wrong: Write *"Because the store was closed, while we walked back home."* or *"Although she reviewed it carefully, because the manager still had questions."*
Both clauses start with subordinating words (*because, while, although*). Joining two dependent clauses leaves the sentence with no main clause.
:::

:::check
Which completes *"If the records are accurate, ___"* as a full sentence: *"the totals will match"* or *"when the totals finally match"*?
:::answer
The answer is **the totals will match**: **"If the records are accurate, the totals will match."** *The totals* is the subject and *will match* is the finite verb with no subordinating word at the front.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Fixing a Fragment Split by a Period

Revise *"Although the manager reviewed the proposal carefully. She still had questions."* into one complete formal sentence.

**Solution:**

1. *Although* makes the first group a dependent fragment.
2. *"She still had questions"* is an independent clause.
3. Join them with a comma: *"Although the manager reviewed the proposal carefully, she still had questions."*

**Check:** Cover the *although* clause; *"she still had questions"* stands alone.

### #2: Checking Comma Order

Decide whether the comma placement is right in (a) *"Because it was raining, we stayed inside."* and (b) *"We stayed inside because it was raining."*

**Solution:**

1. In (a), the *because* clause comes first, so the comma after *raining* is right.
2. In (b), the main clause comes first and the reason clause is essential, so no comma before *because* is right.

**Check:** Move the *because* clause in (b) to the front; a comma follows *raining*, as in (a).

### #3: Completing an Opening Clause

Complete *"Even though the bus was late, ___"* with one option: (a) *"after waiting an hour"*, (b) *"because the driver was lost"*, or (c) *"the students arrived on time."*

**Solution:**

1. Choice (a) cannot stand alone as a main clause.

:::try
Finish the last step: test (b) and (c), then write the full sentence.
:::answer
**Even though the bus was late, the students arrived on time.** (b) starts with *because*, adding a second dependent clause. In (c), *students* is the subject and *arrived* is the finite verb with no subordinating word.

**Check:** Drop *"Even though the bus was late,"* and read *"The students arrived on time."* It stands alone as a main clause.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Count subordinating words (*because, although, if*). Every sentence needs at least one clause with no subordinating word, and do not use two connectors for one link (*although* with *but*, *because* with *so*).

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Reversing clause order with *because*: usually put a comma after an opening *because* clause, and skip it before a closing essential *because* clause. {#comma-by-clause-order}
- Dependent clause ending with a period in formal prose: treat it as a fragment and attach it to a main clause. {#fragment-is-not-sentence}
- Two clauses both starting with subordinating words: drop the second connector so one clause is independent. {#two-dependent-clauses}
:::
