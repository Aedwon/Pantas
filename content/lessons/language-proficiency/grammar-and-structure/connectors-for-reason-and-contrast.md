## Core Concept

### Reason and Contrast Connectors, and Avoiding Double Connectors

A **connector** joins clauses and shows how their ideas relate:

- **Reason** (such as *because, since*): explains why something happened. *"We stayed inside because it was raining."*
- **Contrast** (such as *although, even though, though*): introduces a fact that goes against the expected result. *"Although she was tired, she finished her homework."*

**Use one connector for one link.** A subordinating connector (*although, because*) already joins one dependent clause to one main clause. A coordinating connector (*but, so*) joins two independent clauses. Using both for one link is an error, as in *"Although it rained, but we walked"* or *"Because the store was closed, so we went home."*

:::why
Why does *"Because it rained, so we stayed inside"* fail? *Because* makes *"it rained"* wait for a main clause, while *so* expects a main clause before it. Drop *so* so *"we stayed inside"* serves as the main clause.
:::

:::check
Fix *"Though the road flooded, so we kept driving."* Keep the connector that fits the meaning.
:::answer
The answer is **"Though the road flooded, we kept driving."** Flooding goes against driving on, so *though* stays. *So* shows a reason, so it goes, which leaves one connector for the one link.
:::

### Comma Placement by Clause Order

Compare comma placement by order:

1. **Dependent clause first:** Usually add a comma after it: *"Because it was raining, we stayed inside."*
2. **Essential reason second:** Skip the comma: *"We stayed inside because it was raining."*

:::pair #comma-by-clause-order
Right: *"Because it was raining, we stayed inside."* (comma after *raining*) and *"We stayed inside because it was raining."* (no comma before *because*).
Wrong: Claim *"No: the comma should be removed entirely"* for the opening clause, or *"Yes: a comma is always required regardless of clause order"* for the second.
Usually put a comma after an opening dependent clause; skip it before a following essential *because* clause.
:::

:::check
Where does a comma belong in *"Although the shop was open we left without buying anything"*?
:::answer
The answer is **after "open"**. *"Although the shop was open, we left without buying anything."* An opening dependent clause usually takes a comma before the main clause.
:::

### Completing a Dependent-Clause Fragment

In formal writing, a dependent clause standing alone with a period is a **fragment**, an incomplete sentence: *"Although the teacher explained the lesson twice."*

To fix a dependent-clause fragment, add a **main clause** that can stand alone, with no opening word like *because* or *although*. Adding another dependent clause (*while, because*), a noun phrase, or a prepositional phrase still leaves a fragment.

:::pair #fragment-is-not-sentence
Right: In formal prose, *"Although the teacher explained the lesson twice"* is **a dependent-clause fragment**.
Wrong: Call *"Although the teacher explained the lesson twice"* **a complete independent sentence** or **a run-on with two main clauses**.
*The teacher explained* has a subject and verb, yet *Although* turns the single clause into a fragment waiting for a main clause.
:::

:::pair #two-dependent-clauses
Right: *"Because the store was closed, we went home."*
Wrong: Write *"Because the store was closed, while we walked back home."* or *"Although she reviewed it carefully, because the manager still had questions."*
Both clauses start with words that make them dependent (*because, while, although*). Joining two dependent clauses leaves the sentence with no main clause.
:::

:::check
Which completes *"Since the office was closed, ___"* as a full sentence: *"we called the next day"* or *"because the clerk was ill"*?
:::answer
The answer is **we called the next day**: **"Since the office was closed, we called the next day."** *We called the next day* stands on its own as a full statement.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Fixing a Fragment Split by a Period

Revise *"Although the nurse checked the chart twice. She found a mistake."* into one complete formal sentence.

**Solution:**

1. *Although* makes the first group a dependent fragment.
2. *"She found a mistake"* is an independent clause.
3. Join them with a comma: *"Although the nurse checked the chart twice, she found a mistake."*

**Check:** Cover the *although* clause; *"she found a mistake"* stands alone.

### #2: Checking Comma Order

Decide whether the comma placement is right in (a) *"Because the road flooded, the bus stopped."* and (b) *"The bus stopped because the road flooded."*

**Solution:**

1. In (a), the *because* clause comes first, so the comma after *flooded* is right: "Because the road flooded, the bus stopped."
2. In (b), the main clause comes first and the reason clause is essential, so no comma before *because* is right: "The bus stopped because the road flooded."

**Check:** Bracket the clause in (b): "The bus stopped [because the road flooded]." No comma sits at the bracket.

### #3: Completing an Opening Clause

Complete *"Even though the bus was late, ___"* with the option that keeps the contrast: (a) *"the students arrived on time."* or (b) *"the students arrived late."*

**Solution:**

1. Both options have a subject and a finite verb, so either can stand alone as the main clause.

:::try
Finish the last step: choose the option that expresses the contrast, then write the full sentence.
:::answer
**Even though the bus was late, the students arrived on time.** A late bus leads us to expect late students, so (a) goes against that expectation. (b) matches it, so *even though* would not fit.

**Check:** Join them with *but*: "The bus was late, but the students arrived on time" makes sense.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Spot opening words like *because, although, if*. For the formal statements here, add a main clause that can stand alone, and do not use two connectors for one link (*although* with *but*, *because* with *so*).

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Reversing clause order with *because*: usually put a comma after an opening *because* clause, and skip it before a closing essential *because* clause. {#comma-by-clause-order}
- Dependent clause ending with a period in formal prose: treat it as a fragment and attach it to a main clause. {#fragment-is-not-sentence}
- Two clauses both starting with words like *because* or *although*: provide a main clause that keeps the intended relation. {#two-dependent-clauses}
:::
