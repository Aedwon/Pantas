## Core Concept

### Collective Nouns: One Unit or Separate Members

A **collective noun** names a group, like *company*, *class*, *team*, or *committee*. Your pronoun depends on whether the group acts as one unit or as separate people.

1. Acting as **one unit** (standard American one-unit reading): use *it* or *its*. "The company announced **its** new policy."
2. Members acting **individually**: plural *they* or *their* fits, as in "The committee returned to **their** homes." This is more common in British English.
:::pair #group-unit-vs-members
Right: Treating the company as one unit, "The company announced its new policy yesterday."
Wrong: "The company announced their new policy yesterday" under a one-unit reading.
A company has many people, but as one unit it takes *its*.
:::

:::check
Treat *crew* as one unit. Choose the pronoun: "The crew finished *(its / their / his)* shift early."
:::answer
The answer is **Its**. The crew is treated as one unit, so it takes the singular non-person possessive *its*.
:::

### Antecedents Joined by And

Antecedents joined by *and* normally form a plural pair and take *they*, *them*, *their*, or *theirs*: "Liza and Carlo finished **their** project early."
Use *their* before a noun (*their project*), and *theirs* alone ("The project is **theirs**"). Exception: if *and* joins two titles for one person ("my friend and mentor"), match that single person.

:::pair #joined-by-and-one-person
Right: In "Sofia and her sister compared their schedules," *their* refers to both Sofia and her sister.
Wrong: Say *their* refers only to Sofia (first named) or only to her sister (named closest).
Two different people joined by *and* form a plural pair.
:::

:::check
Mara and Jun share one room. Choose the form: "Mara and Jun cleaned *(his / their / theirs)* room today."
:::answer
The answer is **Their**. Both Mara and Jun share the room, and *their* sits before the noun *room*.
:::

### Mixed Antecedents with Or and Nor

When *or* or *nor* joins a singular noun and a plural noun ("Neither the coach nor the players..."), a formal task may follow the convention that the pronoun agrees with the **nearer** noun.
If the pronoun sounds awkward, name the people directly, use *their own*, or rewrite with an article (*the* or *a*) when one shared set of items is meant.

:::why
Why is *his* wrong in "Neither the coach nor the players brought his own required equipment"? *His* fits only the coach, not the players, and it guesses the coach's gender. *Their own* covers everyone and keeps each person's own equipment.
:::

:::pair #mixed-nor-forced-gender
Right: "Neither the coach nor the players brought their own required equipment."
Wrong: "Neither the coach nor the players brought his own required equipment."
*His* fails to cover both the coach and the players, and it forces male gender. *Their* covers both. It also matches the nearer noun, *players*, a convention a formal task may specify.
:::

:::check
Each person named had a badge. In "Neither the guard nor the drivers wore their badges," which people can *their* refer to: only the drivers, or both the guard and the drivers?
:::answer
**Both the guard and the drivers.** *Their* keeps each person's own badge, so no one named is left out. A singular *his* or *her* would leave out the drivers.
:::

## Examples

### #1: One Unit Versus Individual Members

Choose what fits each rule: (a) Treating *class* as one unit: "The class finished *(its / their)* project early." (b) In a variety allowing plural collective reference, stressing separate members: "The staff *(published its schedule / picked up their coats)*."

**Solution:**

1. In (a), one class with one shared project takes **its**: "The class finished its project early."
2. In (b), separate coats belong to individual members: "The staff **picked up their coats**."

**Check:** Swap in *the class's project* and *the members' coats*: each reads naturally in place of *its project* and *their coats*.

### #2: Joined Antecedents

In "Tess and Raul, who share one garden, watered ______ garden at dawn," choose *their*, *her*, *his*, or *theirs*.

**Solution:**

1. *Tess and Raul* are joined by *and* and share the garden, so rule out *her* and *his*.
2. The noun *garden* follows the blank, so rule out standalone *theirs*.
3. Choose **their**: "Tess and Raul, who share one garden, watered **their** garden at dawn."

**Check:** With *they*: "They watered their garden at dawn."

### #3: A Group Inside a Longer Sentence

Treating any group as one unit, fill each blank with *its*, *her*, or *their*: "The clinic opened on Monday. Dr. Sol said the clinic had met ______ goal, and she kept ______ notes in a folder." The clinic set the goal, and the notes are Dr. Sol's own.

**Solution:**

1. Mark the two owners without choosing forms: [the clinic] set the goal, and [Dr. Sol] keeps the notes.

:::try
Finish the last step: which pronoun fills each blank, and why?
:::answer
The answer is **its** and **her**: "The clinic opened on Monday. Dr. Sol said the clinic had met **its** goal, and she kept **her** notes in a folder." The clinic is one unit, so it takes *its*; Dr. Sol is one person, so she takes *her*. *Their* would suit separate members.

**Check:** Read the whole passage with nouns: "Dr. Sol said the clinic had met the clinic's goal, and she kept Dr. Sol's notes in a folder." Each owner is unchanged.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Check if a group acts as one unit (*its*) or separate members (*their*). Treat *and*-joined names of two different people as plural (*their*).

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Collective noun acting as one organization unit: use *its*, reserving *their* for contexts pointing to separate members. {#group-unit-vs-members}
- Two people joined by *and*: include both with *their*, rather than matching only the first or nearest name. {#joined-by-and-one-person}
- Mixed singular and plural antecedents with *or/nor*: use a pronoun that covers everyone named (*their own*) or rewrite with *the* for one shared set; a formal task may specify the nearer noun. {#mixed-nor-forced-gender}
:::
