## Core Concept

### Collective Nouns: One Unit or Separate Members

A **collective noun** names a group, like *company*, *class*, *team*, or *committee*. Your pronoun depends on whether the group acts as one unit or as separate people.

1. Acting as **one unit** (standard American one-unit reading): use *it* or *its*. "The company announced **its** new policy."
2. Members acting **individually**: plural *they* or *their* fits, as in "The committee returned to **their** homes." This is more common in British English. Naming the people ("The committee members returned to **their** homes") is clearer.

:::pair #group-unit-vs-members
Right: Treating the company as one unit, "The company announced its new policy yesterday."
Wrong: "The company announced their new policy yesterday" under a one-unit reading.
A company has many people, but as one unit it takes *its*.
:::

:::check
Treat *crew* as one unit. Choose the pronoun: "The crew finished *(its / their / his)* shift early."
:::answer
The answer is **Its**. The crew is treated as one unit, so it takes the singular non-person possessive *its*.
:::

### Antecedents Joined by And

Antecedents joined by *and* normally form a plural pair and take *they*, *them*, *their*, or *theirs*: "Liza and Carlo finished **their** project early." "Sofia and her sister compared **their** schedules" refers to both people, not just the first or nearest name.

Use *their* before a noun (*their project*), and *theirs* alone ("The project is **theirs**"). Exception: if *and* joins two titles for one person ("my friend and mentor"), match that single person.

:::pair #joined-by-and-one-person
Right: In "Sofia and her sister compared their schedules," *their* refers to both Sofia and her sister.
Wrong: Say *their* refers only to Sofia (first named) or only to her sister (named closest).
Antecedents joined by *and* form a plural pair. Do not drop either person based on word order.
:::

:::check
Mara and Jun share one room. Choose the form: "Mara and Jun cleaned *(his / their / theirs)* room today."
:::answer
The answer is **Their**. Both Mara and Jun share the room, and *their* sits before the noun *room*.
:::

### Mixed Antecedents with Or and Nor

When *or* or *nor* joins a singular noun and a plural noun ("Neither the coach nor the players..."), formal style usually makes the pronoun agree with the **nearer** noun. A pronoun for a singular person can also force an unstated gender (*his*, *her*).

If the pronoun sounds awkward, name the people directly, use *their own*, or rewrite with an article (*the* or *a*) when one shared set of items is meant.

:::why
Why is *his* wrong in "Neither the coach nor the players brought his own required equipment"? The nearer noun, *players*, is plural, so *his* does not agree, and it guesses the coach's gender. *Their own* keeps each person's own equipment.
:::

:::pair #mixed-nor-forced-gender
Right: "Neither the coach nor the players brought their own required equipment."
Wrong: "Neither the coach nor the players brought his own required equipment."
Formal style matches the nearer noun, *players*, which is plural, so *his* does not agree and forces male gender. *Their* agrees.
:::

:::check
Which fits: (A) "Neither the guard nor the drivers wore its badges." or (B) "Neither the guard nor the drivers wore their badges."?
:::answer
The answer is **(B)**. The nearer noun, *drivers*, is plural, so *their* agrees. *Its* treats people as an object.
:::

## Examples

### #1: One Unit Versus Individual Members

Choose what fits each rule: (a) Treating *class* as one unit: "The class finished *(its / their)* project early." (b) In a variety allowing plural collective reference, stressing separate members: "The committee *(published its report / returned to their homes)*."

**Solution:**

1. In (a), one class with one shared project takes **its**: "The class finished its project early."
2. In (b), separate homes belong to individual members: "The committee **returned to their homes**."

### #2: Joined Antecedents

In "Tess and Raul, who share one garden, watered ______ garden at dawn," choose *their*, *her*, *his*, or *theirs*.

**Solution:**

1. *Tess and Raul* are joined by *and* and share the garden, so rule out *her* and *his*.
2. The noun *garden* follows the blank, so rule out standalone *theirs*.
3. Choose **their**: "Tess and Raul, who share one garden, watered **their** garden at dawn."

**Check:** With *they*: "They watered their garden at dawn."

### #3: A Group Inside a Longer Sentence

Treating *choir* as one unit, fill the blank with *its*, *their*, or *his*: "The choir performed on Friday. The director said the choir was proud of ______ performance."

**Solution:**

1. Find the word the blank points back to: *the choir*.

:::try
Finish the last step: which pronoun fills the blank, and why?
:::answer
The answer is **Its**: "The choir performed on Friday. The director said the choir was proud of its performance." One unit takes *its*, not *their* (separate members) or *his* (one male person).

**Check:** *Its* stands for *the choir's*.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Check if a group acts as one unit (*its*) or separate members (*their*). Treat *and*-joined names as plural (*their*). In mixed *or/nor* sentences, formal style usually matches the nearer noun; use *their own* or rewrite with *the* when one shared set is meant.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Collective noun acting as one organization unit: use *its*, reserving *their* for contexts pointing to separate members. {#group-unit-vs-members}
- Two people joined by *and*: include both with *their*, rather than matching only the first or nearest name. {#joined-by-and-one-person}
- Mixed singular and plural antecedents with *or/nor*: formal style usually matches the nearer noun; use *their own* or rewrite with *the* for one shared set. {#mixed-nor-forced-gender}
:::
