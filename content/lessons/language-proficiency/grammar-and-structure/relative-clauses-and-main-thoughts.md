## Core Concept

### Relative Clauses and Relative Words

A **relative clause** is a dependent clause that often describes a noun or pronoun right before it (this lesson covers that use). Words such as *who, whom, whose, which,* and *that* introduce one.

In *"The volunteer who repaired the bench returned today,"* *who repaired the bench* describes *the volunteer*. Inside it, *who* is the subject and *repaired* is the finite verb.

Pick the relative word by job and commas:

- **Who vs. whom:** Use *who* as the clause subject (*the volunteer who repaired the bench*). In formal prose, use *whom* as the object of a verb or preposition (*the volunteer whom the mayor thanked*).
- **That vs. which (formal American style):** *That* is a common choice, without commas, for an essential clause telling which thing is meant (*"The bench that sits by the gate is dry."*). *Which* can do either job, so the commas show extra side detail about an already clear noun (*"The front bench, which was painted yesterday, is dry."*). *That* never follows a comma in a non-defining clause.

:::pair #relative-is-dependent
Right: In *"The volunteer who repaired the bench returned today,"* *"who repaired the bench"* is a **Dependent clause**.
Wrong: Classify *"who repaired the bench"* as an **Independent clause**, a **Prepositional phrase**, or a **Noun phrase**.
*Who* links the clause to *the volunteer*, so it cannot stand alone. With the finite verb *repaired*, it is a clause, not a phrase.
:::

:::check
Which fits better in formal prose: *"The report, (that / which) arrived this morning, is on your desk"*?
:::answer
The answer is **which**. The commas mark extra detail, and *that* is not used after a comma in a non-defining clause.
:::

### Finding the Main Thought Around a Relative Clause

A relative clause often sits in the middle of a sentence, right between the main subject and main verb.

In *"The driver who found the road closed delayed our delivery,"* *who found the road closed* sits inside the subject to tell which driver is meant. The main verb for *the driver* is *delayed*. Because the relative clause sits inside the main frame, **the whole sentence** is the independent clause.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: the sentence "The driver [who found the road closed] delayed our delivery." with [who found the road closed] bracketed and shaded as "relative clause (dependent)" with an arrow back to "The driver", and a teal arc connecting "The driver" (main subject) over the bracket to "delayed" (main verb) -->

:::why
Why is the whole sentence the independent clause? Drop the embedded relative clause and *"The driver delayed our delivery"* remains. Keep it in, and the whole sentence still forms one complete main statement.
:::

:::pair #stop-before-main-verb
Right: In *"The driver who found the road closed delayed our delivery,"* **The whole sentence** is the independent clause.
Wrong: Select *"who found the road closed"*, *"The driver who found the road closed"*, or *"Delayed our delivery"* as the independent clause.
*"The driver who found the road closed"* lacks its main verb, *"Delayed our delivery"* lacks its subject, and *"who found the road closed"* is the dependent relative clause.
:::

:::check
In *"The clerk who checked my form found an error,"* what is the main verb of the independent clause?
:::answer
The answer is **found**. *Checked* belongs to *who checked my form*, while *found* tells what *the clerk* did.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Describing a Volunteer

In *"The volunteer who repaired the bench returned today,"* classify *"who repaired the bench,"* name the noun it describes, and explain why there are no commas.

**Solution:**

1. *Who repaired the bench* has subject *who* and finite verb *repaired* linked to *volunteer*, so it is a **dependent relative clause**.
2. It describes *the volunteer*.
3. It identifies which volunteer returned, so it is essential and takes no commas.

**Check:** Read the outer frame: *"The volunteer returned today."* The verb *returned* matches *the volunteer*.

### #2: Choosing Who or Whom

Choose the formal form in *"The applicant (who / whom) the committee selected starts on Monday."*

**Solution:**

1. Isolate the relative clause: *"who/whom the committee selected."*
2. *The committee* is the subject doing the selecting, so the missing word is the object of *selected*.
3. Use **whom** for an object in formal prose: "The applicant **whom** the committee selected starts on Monday."

**Check:** Test with *he* or *him*: *"The committee selected him."* Since *him* fits, *whom* is right.

### #3: Separating the Relative Clause From the Main Thought

In *"The pilot who flew the storm route landed safely,"* find the relative clause and state which group of words is the independent clause.

**Solution:**

1. *Who flew the storm route* starts with *who* and describes *the pilot*, so it is a dependent relative clause.

:::try
Finish the last step: name the independent clause and its main verb, and say why.
:::answer
The answer is **The whole sentence**, with main verb **landed**. *The pilot* is the main subject, and *flew* belongs to the relative clause inside the subject.

**Check:** *"The pilot landed safely"* keeps the main subject and verb.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Bracket the relative clause after a noun. Read outside the brackets to pair the main subject with its main verb.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Relative clause starting with *who, which,* or *that* after a noun: classify it as a dependent clause, never as independent or a phrase. {#relative-is-dependent}
- Long subject containing a relative clause: read past the relative clause to connect the main subject to its main verb. {#stop-before-main-verb}
- Comma vs. no comma on a relative clause: use commas (with *which* or *who*) for extra detail, and no commas for essential identification. {#relative-clauses-and-relative-words}
:::
