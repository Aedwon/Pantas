## Core Concept

### Correlative Conjunctions: Matching Both Sides

**Correlative conjunctions** work in pairs: *either...or*, *neither...nor*, *not only...but also*, and *both...and*. Look right after the first word and right after the second word. Both sides must share the same grammatical form.

1. Two adjectives: *"The policy is not only practical but also affordable."*
2. Two *to + verb* phrases: *"She wanted both to travel and to save money."*
3. Two base verbs after *will*: *"The company will neither raise prices nor cut jobs."*

In *"The festival was not only fun but also it was educational,"* *fun* (an adjective) follows *not only*, while *it was educational* (a full clause) follows *but also*. Drop *it was* to leave two adjectives: *"not only fun but also educational."*

Also check connector placement. In *"Applicants may either submit online or by mail,"* *either* sits before *submit* while *or* sits before *by mail*. Move *either* after *submit*: *"Applicants may submit either online or by mail."*

:::pair #mismatched-correlative-pair
Right: Change *"it was educational"* to *"educational"* in *"The festival was not only fun but also it was educational."*
Wrong: Change *"it was educational"* to *"to educate people,"* or change *"fun"* to *"enjoyably."*
*Fun* is an adjective. A *to + verb* phrase (*to educate people*) or an adverb (*enjoyably*) still mismatches the adjective form.
:::

:::pair #misplaced-correlative-connector
Right: *"Applicants may submit either online or by mail."*
Wrong: *"Applicants may either submit online or a mailed application."*
Leaving *either* before *submit* forces a verb phrase (*submit online*) to pair with a noun phrase (*a mailed application*). Put *either* right before the two ways to submit.
:::

:::check
Choose the matching verb in *"The company will neither raise prices nor *(cut / cutting / cuts)* jobs."*
:::answer
The answer is **Cut**. The shared helping verb *will* governs both base verbs: *will raise* and *will cut*.
:::

### Comparisons: Comparing Like to Like

Comparisons with *than* or *as* must compare the same kind of thing or activity on both sides.

In *"Reading a book is more relaxing than to watch television,"* the left side uses *-ing* (*reading*) while the right switches to *to watch*. Change *to watch* to *watching*: *"Reading a book is more relaxing than watching television."*

Watch for missing nouns too. *"The cost of a bus trip is lower than a taxi"* compares a *cost* with a *vehicle*. State the cost on both sides: *"The cost of a bus trip is lower than the cost of a taxi trip."*

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: two balanced comparison cards joined by "is lower than": left card "The cost of a bus trip" tagged "cost"; right card "the cost of a taxi trip" tagged "cost", with "a taxi driver" and "taking a taxi" crossed out below -->

:::why
Why can you not say *"Her report is longer than him"*? A report is writing, while *him* is a person. Comparing *her report* to *his report* keeps the comparison fair.
:::

:::pair #comparing-unlike-things
Right: *"The cost of a bus trip is lower than the cost of a taxi trip."*
Wrong: *"The cost of a bus trip is lower than taking a taxi to the same destination."*
The left side names a cost, while the right switches to an activity (*taking a taxi*). Compare a cost with another cost.
:::

:::check
Which ending keeps *"Reading a book is more relaxing than ___"* parallel: *"to watch television"*, *"watching television"*, or *"a television"*?
:::answer
The answer is **Watching television**. *Reading a book* and *watching television* are both *-ing* activities.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Fixing a Correlative Pair

Identify and fix the error: *"He is both intelligent and he works hard."*

**Solution:**

1. Find the pair: *both...and*.
2. After *both* sits the adjective *intelligent*; after *and* sits the clause *he works hard*.
3. Replace the clause with a matching adjective: *hardworking*.

Corrected sentence: **"He is both intelligent and hardworking."**

**Check:** Read *He is* before each word: *"He is intelligent"* and *"He is hardworking."* Both are adjectives.

### #2: Moving a Misplaced Connector

Revise *"He wanted either to win the race or finishing in the top three"* without changing its meaning.

**Solution:**

1. After *either* sits *to win*; after *or* sits *finishing*.
2. Factor out *wanted to* before both base verbs: *"He wanted to win the race or finish in the top three."*

**Check:** Read *"He wanted to"* before each choice: *"win the race"* and *"finish in the top three."* Both goals remain.

### #3: Fixing Two Errors in One Sentence

Which replacements fix both errors in: *"The seminar teaches not only how to negotiate but also improving communication, with topics such as budgeting, marketing, and how to manage a team"*?

**Solution:**

1. Fix the *not only...but also* pair: match *how to negotiate* with *"how to improve communication."*
2. Check the topic list: *budgeting*, *marketing*, and *how to manage a team*.

:::try
Finish the last step: replace *how to manage a team* so all three topics are noun phrases.
:::answer
The answer is **Use "how to improve" and "team management."** Both skills start with *how to*, and all three topics (*budgeting*, *marketing*, *team management*) are noun phrases.

**Check:** Check the pair (*how to negotiate* / *how to improve*) and the list (*budgeting* / *marketing* / *team management*) separately. Both match.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Cover everything before the first word of a paired connector, then compare what follows each half. For *than* and *as*, name the thing on each side (*cost* vs. *cost*, *-ing* vs. *-ing*).

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Correlative pairs (*not only...but also*, *both...and*): match the exact grammatical form after each connector. {#mismatched-correlative-pair}
- Shared verb before *either* or *neither*: move the connector after the shared verb so both choices match. {#misplaced-correlative-connector}
- Comparisons with *than* or *as*: compare two costs, two people, or two *-ing* activities. {#comparing-unlike-things}
:::
