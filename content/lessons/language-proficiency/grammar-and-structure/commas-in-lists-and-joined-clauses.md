## Core Concept

### Commas in a List

**Punctuation** uses marks such as commas (,) and periods (.) to show sentence boundaries clearly. Use commas to separate three or more items in a list: *"I bought apples, bananas, and oranges."*

The comma right before *and* or *or* is the **serial comma** (Oxford comma). Some style rules require it and some leave it out, so follow the rule given in the question. Always keep it if leaving it out makes the items run together.

Never place a comma between a verb and its object (*"I bought, apples..."*), and never place the serial comma after *and* (*"bananas and, oranges"*).

:::pair #comma-between-verb-and-list
Right: "I bought apples, bananas, and oranges."
Wrong: "I bought, apples, bananas, and oranges." or "I bought apples, bananas and, oranges."
A comma after *bought* splits the verb from what was bought. A comma after *and* sits on the wrong side of the joining word.
:::

:::check
Choose the correctly punctuated list: *"Bring a pencil, an eraser and, a ruler."* or *"Bring a pencil, an eraser, and a ruler."*
:::answer
The answer is **"Bring a pencil, an eraser, and a ruler."** The serial comma belongs right before *and*, not after it.
:::

### Joining Two Independent Clauses

An **independent clause** is a group of words with a subject and a finite verb that can stand alone as a sentence. A **coordinating conjunction** is one of seven joining words: *for*, *and*, *nor*, *but*, *or*, *yet*, and *so*.

When one of these seven words joins **two independent clauses**, place a comma right **before** the joining word. Join *"She studied hard"* and *"she passed the exam"* with *and*:

1. First independent clause: *"She studied hard."*
2. Comma plus joining word: *", and"*
3. Second independent clause: *"she passed the exam."*

Result: *"She studied hard, and she passed the exam."*

Condition: do **not** add a comma when two verbs share one subject (a **compound predicate**). In *"She studied hard and passed the exam,"* *"passed the exam"* has no new subject and cannot stand alone, so omit the comma before *and*.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: two stacked sentence diagrams: top "She studied hard, and she passed the exam." with two complete boxes connected by a teal ", and"; bottom "She studied hard and passed the exam." with one subject "She" branching to "studied hard" and "passed the exam" and no comma before "and" -->

:::why
Why does a comma go before *and* only when a second subject follows? A comma warns the reader that a brand-new complete clause is starting. When one subject simply does two actions, the sentence stays one clause.
:::

:::pair #comma-after-joining-word
Right: "She studied hard, and she passed the exam."
Wrong: "She studied hard and, she passed the exam." or "She studied hard and she, passed the exam."
Put the boundary comma before *and*, not after it. Never place a comma between the second subject (*she*) and its verb (*passed*).
:::

:::check
Should you add a comma before *but* in *"The clerk checked the form but forgot to sign it"*?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. *Forgot to sign it* shares the subject *the clerk*, so *and* or *but* joins two verbs in one clause, not two independent clauses.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Punctuating a Simple List

Add commas to this list, using the serial comma: *"Bring a pencil an eraser and a ruler."*

**Solution:**

1. Name the three separate items: *a pencil*, *an eraser*, and *a ruler*.
2. Put a comma after the first item and a serial comma before *and*.

Correct sentence: **"Bring a pencil, an eraser, and a ruler."**

**Check:** Count the items: (1) a pencil, (2) an eraser, (3) a ruler. Two commas separate the three items cleanly.

### #2: Joining Two Independent Clauses

Add a comma where needed: *"The clerk checked the form and the applicant signed it."*

**Solution:**

1. Test the words before *and*: *"The clerk checked the form"* has subject *the clerk* and verb *checked*. It stands alone.
2. Test the words after *and*: *"the applicant signed it"* has subject *the applicant* and verb *signed*. It stands alone.
3. Place a comma before *and*.

Correct sentence: **"The clerk checked the form, and the applicant signed it."**

**Check:** Read both sides separately as full sentences: *"The clerk checked the form."* *"The applicant signed it."* Both work, so the comma before *and* is required.

### #3: Compound Predicate vs. Two Clauses

Choose the correctly punctuated sentence:

(A) "The clerk checked and filed the form."
(B) "The clerk checked, and filed the form."
(C) "The clerk checked the form, and he filed it."
(D) Both (A) and (C).

**Solution:**

1. Look at (A) and (B): *checked* and *filed* share one subject (*the clerk*), so no comma belongs before *and*. That makes (A) right and (B) wrong.
2. Now test (C) by splitting at *and*.

:::try
Finish the last step: check both halves of (C) around *and*, and name the correct choice.
:::answer
The answer is **(D) Both (A) and (C).** In (C), *"The clerk checked the form"* and *"he filed it"* are two independent clauses, so the comma before *and* is right; in (A), one subject shares two verbs with no comma.

**Check:** Check for a subject after *and*: (A) has no subject after *and* (no comma), while (C) has *he* after *and* (comma before *and*).
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

When you see *for, and, nor, but, or, yet,* or *so*, cover the joining word and read the right side alone. If it has its own subject and verb, put a comma before the joining word. If it is only a second verb for the first subject, leave the comma out.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- List of three or more items: put commas between items and before *and*, never after the verb or after *and*. {#comma-between-verb-and-list}
- Joining two independent clauses with *and* or *but*: put the comma before the joining word, never after it or after the subject. {#comma-after-joining-word}
- Two verbs sharing one subject: leave out the comma when the second part has no separate subject. {#joining-two-independent-clauses}
:::
