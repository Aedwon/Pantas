## Core Concept

### Matching Number and Ownership Form

A **pronoun** replaces a noun or noun phrase, like *she*, *it*, or *they*. The **antecedent** is the word or group of words it refers to. In "The girl lost **her** book," *the girl* is the antecedent of *her*.

Match number and kind: use *her*, *his*, or *its* for one (or singular *their*, explained next), and *their* for more than one ("The girls lost **their** books"). Before a noun, use *her* or *their* (*her book*). Alone without a noun after it, use *hers* or *theirs* ("That book is **hers**").

:::check
Choose the right form: "The blue bag is *(her / hers)*."
:::answer
The answer is **Hers**. No noun follows the blank, so use standalone *hers*.
:::

### Singular They

**Singular they** (*they, them, their, theirs*) refers to one person whose gender is unknown or irrelevant, or to someone who uses *they*. Modern formal English accepts singular *their* after words like *each*, *somebody*, and *everybody*. Never use *its* for a person. Follow any specific rule if a question asks for *his or her*, and choose *they/their* for gender-neutral wording.

:::pair #reject-singular-they
Right: "Each of the students must submit their essay by Friday."
Wrong: Reject *their* because every use of *their* requires a plural antecedent.
Modern formal English uses *their* for one unspecified person too. Do not guess *his* or *her*, or use *its* for a person.
:::

:::check
Choose the gender-neutral pronoun: "Somebody dropped *(his / their / its)* pen in the hall."
:::answer
The answer is **Their**. *Somebody* is one unspecified person, so *their* fits without guessing gender or using *its*.
:::

### Verbs with Everybody and Singular They

Singular *they* changes the pronoun, not the verb attached to *everybody*. Match each verb to its own subject:

1. *Everybody*, *everyone*, *each*, and *somebody* take a **singular** verb (*is, has*).
2. *They* always takes plural verb forms (such as *are*, *have*, and plain verbs without *-s*), even when referring to one person.

:::why
Why write "Everybody **has** said they **are** ready"? Each verb agrees with its own subject. *Everybody* is grammatically singular (*has*), while *they* pairs with *are* in the present.
:::

:::pair #everybody-and-they-verbs
Right: "Everybody has said they are ready."
Wrong: "Everybody have said they are ready," or "Everybody has said they is ready."
Check each subject-verb pair separately. *Everybody* takes *has*, and *they* takes *are*.
:::

:::check
Choose the verbs: "If somebody *(is / are)* late, they *(has / have)* to sign in."
:::answer
The answer is **Is, have**. *Somebody* is singular (*is*), and *they* takes *have*.
:::

### Repeating a Name for Clear Reference

A pronoun is often unclear when two names in the sentence could fit it. Neither the first name nor the nearest name wins by default. Repeat the intended name to make the meaning clear.

:::pair #ambiguous-antecedent
Right: In "Sarah told Michelle that her painting won," *her* is unclear, so name the owner.
Wrong: Say the painting must be Michelle's because *Michelle* is the nearest name.
Proximity does not fix an unclear pronoun. When either person could own the painting, repeat the owner's name.
:::

:::check
Mia alone was late. Which sentence says so clearly: "Lea told Mia that she was late" or "Lea told Mia that Mia was late"?
:::answer
The answer is **"Lea told Mia that Mia was late."** Repeating *Mia* names who was late. In the first sentence, *she* could mean Lea or Mia.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Plural and Gender-Neutral Reference

Choose the pronouns: (a) "The boys left *(his / their)* bags at the gate." (b) Gender-neutral: "Each of the players must bring *(his / their / its)* shoes."

**Solution:**

1. In (a), *the boys* is plural: "The boys left **their** bags at the gate."
2. In (b), *each* points to one unspecified player: "Each of the players must bring **their** shoes."

### #2: Combining Everybody and Singular They

Choose the correct sentence: (A) "Somebody have said they are ready." (B) "Somebody has said they is ready." (C) "Somebody has said they are ready."

**Solution:**

1. *Somebody* is singular, so it needs *has* (not *have*).
2. *They* takes *are* (not *is*).
3. Only **(C) "Somebody has said they are ready"** gets both verbs right.

**Check:** Test each clause: "Somebody has said it" and "They are ready."

### #3: Fixing an Ambiguous Reference

Explain why "When Mr. Lim called Ben, he sounded tired" is unclear, and fix it so Ben sounded tired.

**Solution:**

1. Both *Mr. Lim* and *Ben* fit *he*, so either man could be the one who sounded tired.

:::try
Finish the last step: which sentence clearly states that Ben sounded tired?

(A) "When Mr. Lim called Ben, the man sounded tired."

(B) "When Mr. Lim called Ben, Ben sounded tired."
:::answer
The answer is **(B) "When Mr. Lim called Ben, Ben sounded tired."** Replacing *he* with *Ben* names the exact person meant. In (A), *the man* still fits either person.

**Check:** Ask "Who sounded tired?" Only *Ben* answers the question, with no guessing by order or proximity.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Find the antecedent first. Keep *everybody + singular verb* separate from *they + are*, and repeat a name when two people fit one pronoun.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Singular indefinite words like *each* or *somebody*: use singular *their* for gender-neutral reference instead of guessing *his* or *its*. {#reject-singular-they}
- *Everybody* paired with *they*: keep *everybody* with a singular verb (*has*) and *they* with *are*. {#everybody-and-they-verbs}
- Two people who fit *she* or *her*: repeat the intended name instead of picking the first or nearest noun. {#ambiguous-antecedent}
:::
