## Core Concept

### Tense, Aspect, and Simple Present

English verbs show **tense** (when an action happens) and **aspect** (whether it is a habit, ongoing, or completed).

The **base form** is the plain verb with no ending (*write, sort*). **Simple present** uses the base form (adding *-s* or *-es* for *he, she, it*, or a singular noun; *am, is, are* for *be*). Common uses:

1. Regular habits or duties: "On every working day, the records officer **sorts** the mail."
2. General truths: "Water **boils** at its boiling point."
3. Fixed schedules: "Tomorrow's briefing **starts** at noon."

:::pair #habit-wrong-tense
Right: "On every working day, the records officer sorts the incoming mail."
Wrong: Choose *sorted*, *is sort*, or *sorting* for a regular duty.
Here the duty continues now, and *every working day* marks it as repeated, so a past act (*sorted*) does not fit.
:::

:::check
Choose the verb for a habit that continues now: "The clerk usually *(checks / is checking / checked)* passes at the door."
:::answer
The answer is **Checks**. The stem says the habit continues now, so *checked* is past. Singular *the clerk* takes *-s*.
:::

### Now or Around Now

**Present progressive** uses *am*, *is*, or *are* plus *-ing* (*is writing, are checking*). Common uses:

1. An action happening right now: "She **is writing** a letter right now."
2. A wider project ongoing around now (*this week, this month*), even during a break: "I **am writing** a novel this month."

:::pair #wider-present-project
Right: "I am writing a novel this month" shows a project ongoing around the present.
Wrong: Claim *am writing* means I must be writing at this exact second.
Time clues like *this month* widen the present window.
:::

:::check
Does "My brother is repairing his bike this week" mean the repair is underway this week or was finished last week?
:::answer
The answer is **Underway this week**. *Is* plus *-ing* (*repairing*) shows an action in progress around now, and *this week* names the period.
:::

### State Verbs Usually Take Simple Forms

Verbs like *know, own, believe, belong, understand,* and *seem* describe a **state**, not a step-by-step action. They usually take a simple form ("I **know** how to use the filing system"), not present progressive ("I am knowing").

Condition: a few verbs shift between state and activity. *Have* is a state in "She **has** a manual" (ownership), yet an activity in "She **is having** lunch" (eating).

:::why
Why do state verbs like *know* avoid *-ing*? Progressive *-ing* shows an activity unfolding with starts and stops, whereas knowing or owning something holds steady at once.
:::

:::pair #stative-verb-progressive
Right: "I know how to use the filing system."
Wrong: "I am knowing how to use the filing system."
*Know* describes a current state of knowledge, not an action in progress.
:::

:::check
Choose the usual form: "My sister *(prefers / is preferring)* tea to coffee."
:::answer
The answer is **Prefers**. *Prefer* states a steady liking, so it takes simple present rather than progressive.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Habit, Truth, and Fixed Schedule

Choose the forms: (a) "On every school day, the guard *(opens / opened / open)* the gate," a duty that continues now. (b) "Ice *(melts / melted)* when it gets warm." (c) "The last bus *(leaves / left)* at nine tomorrow."

**Solution:**

1. In (a), *every school day* marks the duty as repeated and the stem says it continues now, so choose **opens**: "On every school day, the guard **opens** the gate."
2. In (b), the sentence states a general truth, so choose **melts**: "Ice **melts** when it gets warm."
3. In (c), a fixed timetable uses simple present for tomorrow, so choose **leaves**: "The last bus **leaves** at nine tomorrow."

**Check:** In (a), *opened* would end the duty in the past, but the stem says it continues now.

### #2: Action Now Versus Project This Month

A student eating lunch says "I am studying for the exam this week." Name the tense and say why it fits.

**Solution:**

1. *This week* marks a longer project around the present, not this second.
2. So *am* + *-ing* is **present progressive**: "I **am studying** for the exam this week."

**Check:** He is eating lunch as he speaks, yet the sentence is true because the studying continues around now.

### #3: Choosing the Right Verb Form

A manager describes the office's situation right now. Choose the correct sentence: (A) "The office is needing two printers." (B) "The office needed two printers." (C) "The office will need two printers." (D) "The office needs two printers."

**Solution:**

1. The need is **right now**, so rule out past *needed* (B) and future *will need* (C).
2. Choose between simple present *needs* and present progressive *is needing*.

:::try
Finish the last step: which of those two is correct, and why?
:::answer
The answer is **(D) "The office needs two printers."** *Need* describes a state rather than a step-by-step action, so simple present is standard.

**Check:** "The office is needing two printers" sounds as wrong as "I am knowing," because *need* is a state here.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Use simple present for habits, truths, and timetables, and usually for a verb in its state sense; use *am/is/are + -ing* for actions going on now or around now.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Habit clues like *every working day* for a duty that continues now: choose simple present (*sorts*), not past *-ed*, *is + base*, or bare *-ing*. {#habit-wrong-tense}
- Present progressive with *this week* or *this month*: it covers projects around the present, even outside this exact second. {#wider-present-project}
- State verbs like *know* or *belong* in their state sense: usually use simple present, not *-ing*. {#stative-verb-progressive}
:::
