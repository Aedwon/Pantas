## Core Concept

An **antecedent** is the noun a pronoun REPLACES or refers back to. "Maria forgot *her* umbrella" — "Maria" is the antecedent, and "her" is the pronoun replacing it.

**Pronoun-antecedent agreement** means the pronoun must MATCH its antecedent in NUMBER (singular or plural) and GENDER, when applicable.

"The girl lost *her* book." (singular antecedent, singular pronoun) versus "The girls lost *their* books." (plural antecedent, plural pronoun).

The same tricky situations from subject-verb agreement apply here too, since pronouns must agree the same way verbs do.

**Indefinite pronoun antecedents** ending in *-body*, *-one*, or *-thing* (like *everybody*, *someone*, *anything*) are SINGULAR, so the pronoun replacing them should also be singular: "Everyone must bring *his or her* (not *their*, in strict formal grammar) own lunch." In everyday and even much modern formal usage, "their" is now widely accepted as a gender-neutral singular pronoun, but exams that test strict traditional grammar rules may still expect "his or her."

**Collective noun antecedents** (like *team*, *class*, *family*) are usually SINGULAR when acting as one unit: "The team celebrated *its* victory," not "their victory."

**Compound antecedents** joined by *and* are PLURAL: "Ana and Ben brought *their* own snacks." Compound antecedents joined by *or* or *nor* match the CLOSER antecedent: "Neither the coach nor the players brought *their* equipment."

## Examples

### #1: Singular Indefinite Pronoun Antecedent

Choose the correct pronoun: Each of the students must submit *(his or her / their)* essay by Friday, according to strict formal grammar rules.

**Solution:**

1. "Each" is a singular indefinite pronoun, even though it refers to a group of students.
2. Under strict, traditional grammar rules tested on formal exams, a singular antecedent needs a singular pronoun.
3. The correct answer is "his or her": "Each of the students must submit *his or her* essay by Friday."

### #2: Collective Noun Antecedent

Choose the correct pronoun: The company announced *(its / their)* new policy yesterday.

**Solution:**

1. "Company" is a collective noun, referring to ONE organization acting as a single unit.
2. The correct pronoun is "its" (singular): "The company announced *its* new policy yesterday."

### #3: Compound Antecedent with "And"

Choose the correct pronoun: Liza and Carlo finished *(her / their)* project early.

**Solution:**

1. "Liza and Carlo" is a compound antecedent joined by "and," making it PLURAL.
2. The correct pronoun is "their": "Liza and Carlo finished *their* project early."

### #4: Ambiguous Antecedent

Identify the problem with this sentence: "When Mrs. Santos spoke to Anna, she seemed upset."

**Solution:**

1. The pronoun "she" could refer to EITHER Mrs. Santos OR Anna — it is unclear which one seemed upset.
2. This is called an AMBIGUOUS (unclear) antecedent, a common sentence-correction error on exams.
3. A clearer version names the person directly: "When Mrs. Santos spoke to Anna, Anna seemed upset."

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

**Check number first, then gender.** Confirm whether the antecedent is singular or plural before choosing the pronoun; this catches most errors quickly.

**Indefinite pronouns ending in -body, -one, -thing are always singular antecedents**, the same rule used for subject-verb agreement.

**Watch for two possible antecedents.** If a sentence has two nouns of the same gender before a pronoun, check whether it is clear which one the pronoun refers to; if not, the sentence has an ambiguous antecedent error.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Using a Plural Pronoun for a Singular Indefinite Antecedent

> *Everybody*, *someone*, *anything*, and *each* are SINGULAR antecedents under strict formal grammar rules.

Everyday speech often uses "they/their" for these words, but grammar-focused exam questions typically expect the traditionally "correct" singular form ("his or her") when testing this specific rule.

### #2: Assuming Collective Nouns Need Plural Pronouns

> Collective nouns like *team*, *family*, and *company* usually take a SINGULAR pronoun ("its"), since they act as one unit.

Writing "The team celebrated *their* victory" breaks strict agreement rules; the grammatically consistent choice is "*its* victory."

### #3: Leaving the Antecedent Ambiguous

> If a pronoun could refer to MORE THAN ONE noun in the sentence, the antecedent is unclear — this is a common sentence-correction error.

Exam questions often ask you to identify or fix a sentence where "she," "he," "it," or "they" could logically point to two different nouns. The fix is usually to replace the confusing pronoun with the specific noun it should refer to.
