## Core Concept

**Parallelism**, or **parallel structure**, means that parts doing the same job in a sentence use matching forms. Check this in lists, paired ideas, and comparisons.

“She likes *swimming, hiking,* and *reading*.” Each item uses the *-ing* form. The list is parallel.

“She likes *swimming, to hike,* and *reading*.” The list mixes an *-ing* form with a *to + verb* form. Each form can follow *likes* by itself, but the list does not match.

**Correlative conjunctions** are joining words used in pairs. Examples are *either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also,* and *both...and*. Compare the parts joined by each pair.

“She wanted *not only to travel but also to save money*.” Both joined parts use *to + verb*. In “The trip was *both short and pleasant*,” both joined words describe the trip.

A **base verb form** is the plain form of a verb, such as *install, update,* or *troubleshoot*. In “how to install, update, and troubleshoot,” one *to* can apply to all the base verbs. Repeating *to* is also possible: “to install, to update, and to troubleshoot.”

Matching form does not always mean matching visible endings. Find the parts that do the same job. Then check whether their grammar matches and whether a shared word applies to each part.

Comparisons need matching forms too. “Reading is more relaxing than watching television” compares two activities in the *-ing* form. The same check applies to comparisons with *as*, such as “Writing is as useful as reading.”

## Examples

### #1: Fixing a Simple List

Find the part that breaks parallel structure and rewrite the sentence correctly: “The coach told the team to run faster, jumping higher, and to try harder.”

**Solution:**

1. The list mixes *to run*, *jumping*, and *to try*.
2. Change *jumping* to the matching *to + verb* form.
3. Corrected sentence: “The coach told the team *to run faster, to jump higher,* and *to try harder*.”

### #2: Fixing a Correlative Pair

Find the part that breaks parallel structure and rewrite the sentence correctly: “The festival was not only fun but also it was educational.”

**Solution:**

1. After *not only*, *fun* describes the festival. After *but also*, *it was educational* repeats who or what is being described.
2. Keep the describing words in both joined parts.
3. Corrected sentence: “The festival was not only *fun* but also *educational*.”

### #3: Choosing the Parallel Sentence

Choose the sentence with correct parallel structure.

(A) “The manual explains how to install the software, updating it, and troubleshoot common errors.”

(B) “The manual explains how to install the software, update it, and troubleshoot common errors.”

**Solution:**

1. In (A), *updating* does not match the base verbs *install* and *troubleshoot*.
2. In (B), *install, update,* and *troubleshoot* are all base verbs.
3. The single *to* in *how to install* applies to the whole list. It means how to install, how to update, and how to troubleshoot.
4. The correct answer is (B).

### #4: Parallelism in Comparisons

Rewrite this sentence with parallel structure: “Reading a book is more relaxing than to watch television.”

**Solution:**

1. The comparison matches *reading* with *to watch*. These use different forms.
2. Change the second part to an *-ing* form.
3. Corrected sentence: “Reading a book is more relaxing than *watching* television.”

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Line up the list items. Check whether they use the same kind of word or phrase.

Do not check endings alone. A shared word can apply to several items. In “to read, write, and revise,” the single *to* applies to all the base verbs.

With a correlative pair, compare the joined parts. Check what follows *either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also,* or *both...and*.

In comparisons with *than* or *as*, check both sides. Matching ideas should also use matching forms.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Missing an Error in a Long Sentence

> Break a long list into separate items and check each one.

A mismatch can hide in the middle of a sentence. Do not check only the first and last items.

### #2: Checking Each Item Only by Itself

> Items that work by themselves may still fail to match each other.

“She likes hiking and to swim” mixes two forms that can each follow *likes*. Make the pair parallel: “She likes hiking and swimming.”

### #3: Repeating Every Shared Word

> Parallel items do not always need the same word repeated before each one.

“She wants to read, write, and revise” is parallel. The word *to* applies to each base verb in the list.

### #4: Forgetting the Joined Parts

> Check the form on each side of a correlative pair.

“He wanted either to win or winning” mixes forms. A parallel version is “He wanted either to win or to finish with pride.”
