## Core Concept

### Subjects Joined by And

A **compound subject** joins subjects with *and*, *or*, or *nor*. Subjects joined by *and* usually take a plural verb because they name more than one person or thing: "Ana and Ben **are** here."

One exception: when joined words name **one** person with two roles, or **one** combined unit, use a singular verb: "The owner and manager **is** signing the form." A repeated *the* is a clue, not proof: ask who the nouns name. After *each* or *every*, use a singular verb: "Every driver and passenger **is** checked."

:::pair #joined-by-and-plural
Right: "Ana and Ben are already here, waiting by the entrance."
Wrong: "Ana and Ben is already here, waiting by the entrance."
Two people joined by *and* take plural *are*.
:::

:::check
The cook is also the driver. Choose the verb: The cook and driver *(is / are)* ready to leave.
:::answer
The answer is **Is**. One person holds both roles, so singular *is* agrees.
:::

### Agreement with Or and Nor

With *or*, *either...or*, and *neither...nor*, a common formal convention (the **proximity convention**) is to match the verb to the nearer subject:

1. "Neither the manager nor the employees **were** aware." (Nearer head: *employees*, plural -> *were*.)
2. "Neither the students nor the teacher **was** late." (Nearer head: *teacher*, singular -> *was*.)

Set aside detail phrases. In "Neither the coach nor the players on the visiting team **were** satisfied," cover *on the visiting team* so plural *players* controls *were*.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: the sentence "Neither the coach nor the players on the visiting team were satisfied." with "the coach" grayed out, "on the visiting team" bracketed as "added detail", and a teal arrow from "players" (labeled "nearer head, plural") to "were" -->

:::pair #proximity-first-subject
Right: "Neither the manager nor the employees were aware of the change."
Wrong: "Neither the manager nor the employees was aware of the change."
*The manager* comes first, yet *employees* sits closer to the verb. By the convention, match the nearer subject.
:::

When *or* or *nor* joins mismatched subjects like *I* and *she*, one verb feels awkward. Rewrite into two clauses so each subject gets its own verb: "Either she is responsible, or I am."

:::why
Why rewrite awkward *or/nor* sentences into two clauses? One verb cannot match both *she* (*is*) and *I* (*am*) at once. Two clauses let each subject take its natural verb.
:::

:::pair #split-clause-agreement
Right: "The students were not late, and the teacher was not late."
Wrong: "The students were not late, and the teacher were not late."
Plural *students* takes *were*, and singular *teacher* takes *was*.
:::

:::check
Under the formal proximity convention, choose the verb: Neither the drivers nor the passenger *(was / were)* ready.
:::answer
The answer is **Was**. Singular *passenger* is closer to the verb, so choose *was*.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Separate People Versus One Person

Choose each verb: (a) The supervisor and the clerk *(is / are)* checking the forms. (b) Our treasurer and secretary, who handles both jobs alone, *(is / are)* absent.

**Solution:**

1. In (a), two people are joined by *and*: "The supervisor and the clerk **are** checking the forms."
2. In (b), one person handles both jobs: "Our treasurer and secretary, who handles both jobs alone, **is** absent."

**Check:** Test pronouns: "They are checking" fits (a), and "She is absent" fits (b).

### #2: Rewriting Neither...Nor into Two Clauses

Revise "Neither the cooks nor the waiter arrived early" into two negative clauses with agreeing *be*-verbs.

**Solution:**

1. First clause: *the cooks* (plural) -> *The cooks were not early*.
2. Second clause: *the waiter* (singular) -> *and the waiter was not early*.
3. Combined: "The cooks were not early, and the waiter was not early."

**Check:** *Cooks were*, *waiter was*; both keep the negative meaning.

### #3: Two Clauses with Or and Nor

Using formal proximity, choose the verbs in one sentence: Neither the nurses nor the doctor on the night shift *(was / were)* ready, and either the guard or the visitors *(was / were)* waiting outside.

**Solution:**

1. In the first clause, look after *nor*: *the doctor on the night shift*.
2. Cover *on the night shift*. Nearer head *doctor* is singular, so choose **was**.
3. Then look after *or*.

:::try
Finish the last step: is the second verb *was* or *were*?
:::answer
The answer is **Were**. The nearer head, *visitors*, is plural. The finished sentence is "Neither the nurses nor the doctor on the night shift **was** ready, and either the guard or the visitors **were** waiting outside."

**Check:** Pair each head with its verb: "The doctor was ready" and "The visitors were waiting."
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

With *and*, ask whether the nouns name two things or one unit; after *each* or *every*, use singular. With *or* or *nor*, cover added phrases and, by the formal convention, match the nearer head.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Two names joined by *and* doing one shared activity: keep the verb plural. {#joined-by-and-plural}
- *Neither...nor* or *either...or*: by the formal convention, match the nearer subject's head, not the first subject. {#proximity-first-subject}
- Rewriting *neither...nor* into two clauses: check each clause's verb separately instead of repeating one form. {#split-clause-agreement}
:::
