## Core Concept

### Marking Earlier Completion in the Past

**Past perfect** uses *had* plus a past participle (*had cooked*, *had written*, *had finished*). Use it to show an action was already complete by a past event or time:

- "By the time the guests arrived, she **had cooked** the entire meal."
- "She **had written** the letter by noon yesterday."

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: horizontal timeline showing Event 1 marked "had cooked (earlier past, finished first)" before Event 2 marked "guests arrived (later past)", both to the left of "now" -->

*Had* stays the same for every subject (*I had, she had, they had*). Put *had* on the action that was already finished at the later past point.

:::pair #mark-earlier-past
Right: By the time the guests arrived, she had cooked the entire meal.
Wrong: Use simple past (*cooked*) when the prompt asks for past perfect, or present (*cooks*).
*Arrived* puts the sequence in the past, so present *cooks* breaks the timeline. The requested form, *had cooked*, shows cooking finished before the guests arrived.
:::

:::check
Choose the past-perfect form for an action finished before a past time: "Ana *(had called / has called)* the client before the meeting started yesterday."
:::answer
The answer is **Had called**. *Before the meeting started yesterday* is a past reference point, so earlier completion uses *had* rather than *has*.
:::

### When Time Words Already Show Order

Past perfect is optional when *before* or *after* already shows order. Compare simple past and past perfect with *before*:

- "She **finished** the report before her boss **asked** for it." (Simple past, clear order.)
- "She **had finished** the report before her boss **asked** for it." (Past perfect, stresses earlier completion.)

:::why
Why are both sentences right with *before*? The word *before* already tells the reader which action came first. Past perfect is still helpful when you want to stress that the first action was completely done, or when the sentence has no clear order word like *before*.
:::

:::pair #before-already-orders
Right: "She finished the report before her boss asked for it" is grammatical because *before* clearly orders the two past events.
Wrong: Claim that any action that happened first must always use past perfect instead, or that the later event must always use past perfect instead of simple past.
With *before*, simple past is grammatical because the order is already clear. Past perfect marks an action already finished at a later past point, but it is not required.
:::

:::check
Which event happened first in "The train had left when we reached the station"?
:::answer
The answer is **The train leaving**. *Had left* shows the earlier past event, while *reached* shows the later past event.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Showing Earlier Completion Before a Past Point

Choose the past-perfect form to make earlier completion explicit: "By the time the guests arrived, she ___ the entire meal."

**Solution:**

1. Find the later past point: *the guests arrived* (simple past).
2. Check when cooking happened: it was already complete by the time they arrived.
3. Build past perfect with *had* plus the past participle *cooked*: **had cooked**.

Correct: "By the time the guests arrived, she had cooked the entire meal."

**Check:** *The guests arrived* sets the past reference point, and *had cooked* directly marks cooking as finished before that arrival.

### #2: Two Past Actions in One Sentence

Why is "He locked the gate before the guard arrived" grammatical in standard English?

**Solution:**

1. Identify the two past actions: *locked* (Action 1) and *arrived* (Action 2).
2. Check the word that joins them.

:::try
Finish the last step: why does simple past *locked* work here without needing *had locked*?
:::answer
The answer is **"*Before*" clearly orders the two past events.** Both verbs stay in the past, and *before* already shows that locking the gate happened earlier than the guard's arrival.

**Check:** Point directly to *before* and the two past verbs *locked* and *arrived*: *before* fixes *locked* as the earlier event, so simple past is unambiguous.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

When two past events appear together, put them on a mental timeline. Use *had* + past participle on an action completed before a later past reference point. If *before* or *after* already makes the order unmistakable, simple past is also valid unless the question asks specifically for past perfect.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- A prompt that asks for the past-perfect form: put *had* + past participle on the action finished first. {#mark-earlier-past}
- A sentence with *before* or *after* joining two simple past verbs: accept simple past when the connector already makes event order clear. {#before-already-orders}
:::
