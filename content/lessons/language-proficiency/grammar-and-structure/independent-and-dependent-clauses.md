## Core Concept

### What Makes a Clause Independent

In an ordinary statement, a **clause** has a stated subject and a **finite verb** (a verb form that often shows tense or agreement, such as *slept* or *arrives*). A command like *Close the door* leaves its subject understood. This lesson compares finite clauses; *after leaving* has no finite verb.

An **independent clause**, also called a **main clause**, gives a complete thought and can stand alone as a sentence. Even a short group is independent when it has a subject, a finite verb, and no word leaving the reader waiting: *"The cat slept."* *"She finished her homework."*

:::pair #short-clause-is-complete
Right: Classify *"The cat slept"* as an **Independent clause**.
Wrong: Classify *"The cat slept"* as a **Prepositional phrase** or a **Dependent clause** because it is short.
Length does not decide clause type. *The cat* is the subject and *slept* is the finite verb, with no word making it lean on another clause.
:::

:::check
Which can stand alone: *"Our neighbor waved to us"* or *"Our neighbor waving to us"*?
:::answer
**Our neighbor waved to us.** *Our neighbor* is the subject and *waved* is the finite verb. *Waving* shows no tense by itself.
:::

### Dependent Clauses and Subordinating Words

A **dependent clause** (subordinate clause) in an ordinary statement has a subject and finite verb, yet cannot stand alone in its sentence. A **subordinating conjunction** (such as *although, because, since, when, while, if, until, after, before*) usually introduces it and makes it lean on a main clause. Some have no connector: in *"I thought you knew,"* *you knew* is dependent.

In *"Although she was tired,"* *although* leaves you waiting for what happened next. Pair it with a main clause: *"Although she was tired, she finished her homework."*

:::why
Why does one opening word change a whole clause? Drop *although* from *"Although she was tired"* and *"She was tired"* stands alone. Adding *although* signals that a main clause must go with it, before or after.
:::

:::pair #subordinator-makes-dependent
Right: Classify *"Although she was tired"* and *"When the bell rang"* as **Dependent clauses**.
Wrong: Classify *"Although she was tired"* or *"When the bell rang"* as an **Independent clause** because *"she was tired"* and *"the bell rang"* can stand alone without the first word.
Include the opening word in your stand-alone test. *Although* and *when* make those groups depend on a main clause.
:::

:::check
Which group leaves you waiting for a main clause: *"The crowd left early"* or *"When the crowd left early"*?
:::answer
**When the crowd left early.** *When* makes it lean on a main clause: *"When the crowd left early, the street was quiet."*
:::

### Checking After Words Like After, Before, and Since

*After, before, since,* and *until* can start a **prepositional phrase** or a **dependent clause**. Compare the two groups shown, then ask whether a finite verb is present:

1. Noun group only, no finite verb: *"After the bus trip"* is a prepositional phrase.
2. Subject paired with a finite verb: *"After the bus arrived"* has subject *the bus* and finite verb *arrived*, so it contains a dependent clause.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: two stacked groups starting with "After": top "After [the bus trip]" with "the bus trip" bracketed and labeled "noun group, no finite verb -> prepositional phrase"; bottom "After [the bus] [arrived]" with "the bus" labeled "subject", "arrived" labeled "finite verb", and the whole group labeled "dependent clause" -->

:::pair #event-noun-vs-verb
Right: *"After the bus arrived"* contains a finite dependent clause.
Wrong: Pick *"After the bus trip"* or *"During the bus journey"* as a finite clause because *trip* and *journey* name actions.
Here *trip* and *journey* act as nouns after *the bus*, not finite verbs. Look for a subject paired with a finite verb.
:::

:::check
Which group contains a finite clause: *"Before the sale"* or *"Before the sale ended"*?
:::answer
**Before the sale ended.** *The sale* is the subject and *ended* is the finite verb; *"Before the sale"* has no verb.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Testing a Short Statement

How is the whole group *"the dog barked"* classified?

**Solution:**

1. Find the subject: *the dog*.
2. Find the finite verb: *barked*.
3. Check for any opening subordinating word: none appears.

The group is an **independent clause**: "The **dog** (subject) **barked** (finite verb)."

**Check:** Read it alone with a period: nothing leaves you waiting for more.

### #2: Finding the Signal Word

In *"Because the road flooded, the bus was late,"* which word signals that the first clause is dependent?

**Solution:**

1. Isolate the first clause: *"Because the road flooded."*
2. Remove *Because*: *"the road flooded"* has subject *the road* and verb *flooded*, and stands alone.
3. So **Because** is the signal word making the clause dependent: "**Because** the road flooded, the bus was late."

**Check:** Stop after *Because the road flooded*: you still wait for a main clause, which is *the bus was late*.

### #3: Phrase or Clause After a Time Word

Which group contains a finite clause: *"Until the doors opened"* or *"Until the opening ceremony"*?

**Solution:**

1. Mark the words after *Until* in each group: *the doors opened* and *the opening ceremony*.

:::try
Finish the last step: which group has a verb that changes when the time changes?
:::answer
**Until the doors opened** contains the finite clause. *The doors* is the subject, *opened* is the finite verb, and *Until* makes the clause dependent. In *"Until the opening ceremony,"* *ceremony* is a noun, so the group is a prepositional phrase.

**Check:** Change the time: *"Until the doors open"* changes the verb, while *"Until the opening ceremony"* has no verb to change.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For finite clauses, run two checks: find a subject paired with a finite verb, not an event noun like *trip* or *journey*, and keep any opening connector (*although, when, since*) in the stand-alone test.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Short statement with a subject and finite verb: do not call it a phrase or dependent just because it is brief. {#short-clause-is-complete}
- Clause starting with *although, since,* or *when*: include the opening word in the stand-alone test. {#subordinator-makes-dependent}
- Time group with an event noun like *trip* or *journey*: check whether a finite verb is present. {#event-noun-vs-verb}
:::
