## Core Concept

### Phrases Versus Clauses

A **subject** tells who or what a group of words is about. A **verb** tells the action or state of that subject.

In this lesson, a **clause** is a full group with both a subject and a verb working together: "The cat **slept**." (*The cat* is the subject, and *slept* is the verb.) An **independent clause** can stand alone as a complete sentence. A **dependent clause** has a subject and a verb too, but cannot stand alone: "because the cat slept."

A **phrase** is a group of words working as one part of a sentence without a subject paired with a verb: "under the old bridge." It tells where, yet has no verb at all. It is a **prepositional phrase** because it begins with the preposition *under* followed by the noun group *the old bridge*.

Later lessons cover verb forms such as *to finish the project* and clauses with words left out.

:::pair #phrase-vs-dependent-clause
Right: "Under the old bridge" is a prepositional phrase.
Wrong: Call "under the old bridge" a dependent clause (because it cannot stand alone) or an independent clause (because it gives clear location details).
In this lesson, a clause needs a subject and a verb. "Under the old bridge" is a location phrase: the preposition *under* and its noun group.
:::

:::check
Is "after the morning meeting" a phrase or a clause?
:::answer
The answer is **A phrase**. *After* introduces the noun group *the morning meeting*, with no subject-verb pair.
:::

### Commands Have an Understood Subject

In a command such as "Close the door" or "Sit down," the subject is usually not written out, yet it is still there. The **understood subject** (implied subject) is *you*, the person spoken to: "(You) close the door."

Because a command pairs the understood subject *you* with a real verb (*close*, *sit*) and gives a complete instruction, it is an **independent clause**.

:::why
Why do commands leave out *you*? When you speak directly to someone to give an order or polite request, the listener already knows *you* must do the action, so English drops the word unless extra stress is needed.
:::

:::pair #command-has-understood-you
Right: "Close the door" is an independent clause with an implied subject (*you*).
Wrong: Call "Close the door" a dependent clause with a missing main clause, or a noun phrase because it names an action.
Even without a written subject, *close* is paired with understood *you*, so the command stands on its own as an independent clause.
:::

:::check
Which group has an understood subject: "Pass the salt, please" or "Mina passed the salt"?
:::answer
The answer is **"Pass the salt, please."** It has no written subject, so the listener (*you*) is the understood subject. "Mina passed the salt" writes its subject, *Mina*.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Two Word Groups

Classify these word groups as a prepositional phrase or an independent clause: (a) "near the red gate"; (b) "the bus stopped."

**Solution:**

1. (a) is a **prepositional phrase**: *near* is a preposition followed by *the red gate*, with no verb and no subject-verb pair: "**near the red gate**."
2. (b) is an **independent clause**: *the bus* is the subject and *stopped* is its verb, expressing a complete thought: "**the bus stopped**."

**Check:** Try each group alone with a period: "Near the red gate." leaves the reader asking what happened there, whereas "The bus stopped." states a complete event.

### #2: One Word Group on Its Own

Is "beside the quiet lake" a phrase or a clause: (A) Prepositional phrase, (B) Independent clause, (C) Dependent clause, or (D) Main verb phrase?

**Solution:**

1. Scan the words for a verb: *beside* (preposition), *the* (article), *quiet* (adjective), *lake* (noun). There is no verb.
2. Rule out (B) Independent clause and (C) Dependent clause, because the full clauses compared here need a subject-verb pair.
3. Rule out (D) Main verb phrase, because there is no verb.
4. Choose **(A) Prepositional phrase**: "We camped **beside the quiet lake**."

**Check:** Ask *where did we camp?* The whole group *beside the quiet lake* answers it as one location unit.

### #3: Open the Window

How do we classify the group of words "Open the window"?

**Solution:**

1. Find the verb: *open*.
2. Ask who is expected to open the window.

:::try
Finish the last step: is "Open the window" a phrase, a dependent clause, or an independent clause, and why?
:::answer
The answer is **Independent clause; implied subject.** In "**Open** the window," the verb *open* pairs with the understood subject (*you*, not written) and gives a complete instruction, so it stands alone without another clause.

**Check:** Read "Open the window." alone with a period. It is a complete instruction, so it needs no other clause.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Look for the verb first. In these examples, a group with no verb is a phrase, not a clause. If the group is an order or polite request, supply *you* in front to reveal the full independent clause.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- A location group like "under the old bridge": check for a verb before calling any incomplete group a dependent clause. {#phrase-vs-dependent-clause}
- A command with no written subject like "Close the door": supply understood *you* and treat it as an independent clause. {#command-has-understood-you}
:::
