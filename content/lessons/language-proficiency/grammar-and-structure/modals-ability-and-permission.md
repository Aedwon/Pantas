## Core Concept

### Always Use the Base Form After a Modal

A **modal verb** is a helper such as *can, could, may, might, must, should,* or *will*. True modals never add *-s* for singular subjects, and the next verb always stays in its **base form** (no *-s*, *-ed*, *-ing*, and no *to*):

- "The assistant **can prepare** the minutes without help."
- "The new clerk **can answer** routine inquiries."

Semi-modals like *have to* and *ought to* include *to*, while true modals take the bare base verb directly.

:::pair #base-form-after-modal
Right: The assistant can prepare the minutes without help.
Wrong: The assistant can prepares (or *can prepared*, or *can preparing*) the minutes without help.
Even with a singular subject like *the assistant*, never add *-s*, *-ed*, or *-ing* after *can*.
:::

:::pair #no-to-after-modal
Right: The new clerk can answer routine inquiries without assistance.
Wrong: The new clerk can to answer (or *answers*, or *answering*) routine inquiries without assistance.
True modals like *can* take the bare base form *answer* directly, never *to* plus a verb (*to answer*), an *-s* ending (*answers*), or an *-ing* form (*answering*).
:::

:::check
Choose the right form: Omar *(can fix / can fixes / can to fix)* the bike.
:::answer
The answer is **Can fix**. After *can*, use the bare base verb *fix* with no *-s* and no *to*.
:::

### Can and Could for Ability

Use **can** for present or general ability: "She **can type** sixty words a minute." Use **could** for general ability in the past: "I **could type** before I entered college."

For a single past success rather than general skill, writers usually prefer *was able to* or *managed to* over *could*.

:::check
Choose the form: Before school started, Lina *(can / could)* already read short books.
:::answer
The answer is **Could**. *Before school started* describes general past ability, so choose *could*.
:::

### Requesting Permission

Three common modals ask for permission, from everyday to formal:

1. **Can** (everyday): "**Can** I borrow your pen?"
2. **Could** (polite request): "**Could** I use this desk?"
3. **May** (formal): "**May** I enter the room?"

Because *could* also expresses past ability or possibility, read the whole sentence to see which job it does.

:::why
Why do polite requests often use *could*? In requests, a past-looking modal (*could*) softens the ask without placing the request in past time.
:::

:::pair #could-permission-vs-ability
Right: "Could I use this desk?" uses *could* to request permission.
Wrong: Choose "I could type before I entered college" or "The records could arrive later this afternoon" as a permission request.
In a statement, *could* often shows past ability (*could type*) or possibility (*could arrive*). Look for a question asking someone to allow an action.
:::

:::check
Which asks permission: "Could it rain tonight?" or "Could I borrow your charger?"
:::answer
The answer is **"Could I borrow your charger?"** The speaker is asking to be allowed to borrow it, whereas *Could it rain tonight?* asks whether something is possible.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Checking Verb Form After a Modal

Complete "The pilot can ___ the plane in strong wind" with *lands*, *landing*, *to land*, or *land*.

**Solution:**

1. Spot the modal before the blank: *can*.
2. Apply the modal rule: *can* takes the bare base form.
3. Rule out *lands*, *landing*, and *to land*, leaving **land**: "The pilot can land the plane in strong wind."

**Check:** Test the same verb after another modal: "The pilot will **land** the plane" and "The pilot must **land** the plane" both use *land*.

### #2: Ability and Formal Permission

Choose each modal: (a) Present ability: "Maria *(can / must)* solve the problem now." (b) Past ability: "Before college, she *(could / may)* speak three languages." (c) Formal permission: "*(May / Must)* I enter the room?"

**Solution:**

1. Present ability pairs with **can**.
2. General past ability pairs with **could**.
3. Formal permission pairs with **May**.

**Check:** Read each with its meaning: *can solve* (able now), *could speak* (able in the past), and *May I enter* (formal permission).

### #3: Spotting Permission Among Other Uses of Could

Choose the sentence using *could* to request permission: "She could dance well as a child," "It could rain by noon," or "Could I take this seat?"

**Solution:**

1. Check "She could dance well as a child": it states general past ability.

:::try
Finish the job: say what *could* does in the other two sentences, then choose the permission request.
:::answer
"It could rain by noon" states a possibility. The permission request is **"Could I take this seat?"** The speaker is politely asking someone to allow the use of the seat.

**Check:** Swap *Could* for *May* ("May I take this seat?"): it still asks permission cleanly, whereas swapping *may* into "She could dance" breaks the past-ability meaning.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Check modals in two passes. First check form: after a modal such as *can, could, may, might, must, should,* or *will*, allow only the bare base verb (no *-s, -ed, -ing,* or *to*). Next read the full sentence for meaning: ability, permission, or possibility.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Verb after a true modal like *can*: keep the bare base form, never adding *-s*, *-ed*, or *-ing*. {#base-form-after-modal}
- Putting *to* after a true modal: drop *to* after *can*, *could*, *may*, or *must* (*can answer*, not *can to answer*). {#no-to-after-modal}
- Spotting *could* for permission: pick a request asking to be allowed to act, not past ability or possibility. {#could-permission-vs-ability}
:::
