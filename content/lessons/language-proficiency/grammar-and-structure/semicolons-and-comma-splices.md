## Core Concept

### Joining Independent Clauses with a Semicolon

A **semicolon (;)** joins two closely related independent clauses without a joining word: *"The train was late; the passengers remained calm."* Each side has a subject and finite verb and can stand alone.

Joining two independent clauses with only a comma is a **comma splice**: *"The concert was amazing, everyone stayed until the end."* Fix a comma splice in ways such as these:

1. Replace the comma with a semicolon (;).
2. Replace the comma with a period (.).
3. Add a coordinating conjunction (*and, but, so, yet, or, for, nor*) after the comma.

Condition: a semicolon that joins clauses needs an **independent clause on both sides**. Do not put one before a dependent clause (*"; because she visits..."*), before a phrase (*"; after regular servicing"*), or right after *and* (*"and;"*).

:::pair #comma-splice-same-topic
Right: Fix *"The concert was amazing, everyone stayed until the end"* by replacing the comma with a semicolon (;).
Wrong: Keep the comma because both clauses concern the same concert.
Sharing a topic does not let a comma alone join two complete clauses in formal writing. Test both halves as standalone sentences.
:::

:::pair #semicolon-before-dependent-part
Right: "She loves reading; she visits the library every weekend."
Wrong: "She loves reading; because she visits the library every weekend."
*Because she visits the library every weekend* has a subject and verb, yet *because* makes it dependent. A semicolon cannot join a main clause to a dependent clause.
:::

:::check
Can a semicolon join *"The bus left on time"* and *"with every seat taken"*?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. *"With every seat taken"* is a phrase with no subject or finite verb, so it cannot follow a semicolon here.
:::

### Separating Complex List Items

When list items already contain commas, commas alone blur the items together. Keep commas inside each item and place semicolons between the full items.

In *"We visited Manila, Philippines; Tokyo, Japan; and Seoul, Korea,"* each pair keeps its inside comma, and semicolons divide the pairs.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: marked-up list "We visited [Manila, Philippines] ; [Tokyo, Japan] ; and [Seoul, Korea]." with each city-country pair bracketed, the internal commas shown in gray, and the boundary semicolons highlighted in teal -->

:::why
Why switch to semicolons for a list with inside commas? Semicolons act as heavier dividers than commas, so the reader sees three two-part items.
:::

:::pair #semicolons-in-complex-list
Right: "We visited Manila, Philippines; Tokyo, Japan; and Seoul, Korea."
Wrong: "We visited Manila, Philippines, Tokyo, Japan, and Seoul, Korea." or "We visited Manila; Philippines, Tokyo; Japan, and Seoul; Korea."
All commas blur the pairs into six places, while semicolons inside each pair split each city from its country. Keep commas inside pairs and put semicolons between pairs.
:::

:::check
Each city in *"The delegates came from Calamba, Laguna, Vigan, Ilocos Sur, and Malolos, Bulacan"* comes with its province. Where do the semicolons go?
:::answer
The answer is **After *Laguna* and after *Ilocos Sur***. Each city-province pair keeps its internal comma, and semicolons divide the three pairs.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Fixing a Comma Splice

Identify and fix the error: *"The store closed early, the clerks went home."*

**Solution:**

1. Test the first half: *"The store closed early"* stands alone.
2. Test the second half: *"the clerks went home"* stands alone.
3. Replace the comma splice with a semicolon (;).

Corrected sentence: **"The store closed early; the clerks went home."**

**Check:** Put a period at the semicolon: *"The store closed early."* *"The clerks went home."* Both are complete sentences.

### #2: Why a Semicolon Fails Before a Phrase

Why is the semicolon faulty in *"The boat sank; during the storm"*?

**Solution:**

1. *"The boat sank"* has subject *the boat* and finite verb *sank*, so it is independent.
2. *"During the storm"* has no subject and no finite verb, so it is a phrase.
3. Because the second half is not an independent clause, the semicolon is faulty.

**Check:** Read *"During the storm."* alone: it is not a full sentence.

### #3: Choosing the Punctuation for a List

Which sentence punctuates the list of three guests correctly?

(A) "The panel included Dr. Cruz, a surgeon, Ms. Lim, a teacher, and Mr. Ong, a pilot."
(B) "The panel included Dr. Cruz; a surgeon, Ms. Lim; a teacher, and Mr. Ong; a pilot."
(C) "The panel included Dr. Cruz, a surgeon; Ms. Lim, a teacher; and Mr. Ong, a pilot."

**Solution:**

1. Group each guest with the job: (1) *Dr. Cruz, a surgeon*, (2) *Ms. Lim, a teacher*, (3) *Mr. Ong, a pilot*.

:::try
Finish the last step: select the correct choice, and say why the others fail.
:::answer
The answer is **(C) "The panel included Dr. Cruz, a surgeon; Ms. Lim, a teacher; and Mr. Ong, a pilot."** Choice (A) uses only commas, so the three guests blur into six items. Choice (B) splits each name from its job with a semicolon.

**Check:** Bracket each guest in (C): *[Dr. Cruz, a surgeon]*, *[Ms. Lim, a teacher]*, *[Mr. Ong, a pilot]*. Commas stay inside; semicolons sit between the brackets.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For a semicolon between clauses, cover it and test both sides as standalone sentences; if either side needs the other, drop the semicolon. For complex lists, bracket each item, then put semicolons between brackets.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Two independent clauses joined by only a comma: fix the comma splice with a semicolon, period, or comma plus joining word. {#comma-splice-same-topic}
- Semicolon before a *because*-clause or phrase: check that both sides can stand alone as full sentences. {#semicolon-before-dependent-part}
- List of items with internal commas: keep commas inside each item and put semicolons between items. {#semicolons-in-complex-list}
:::
