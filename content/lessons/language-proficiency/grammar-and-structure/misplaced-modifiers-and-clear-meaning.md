## Core Concept

### Placing a Modifier Beside Its True Target

A **modifier** is a word, phrase, or clause that adds detail about another part of a sentence.

A **misplaced modifier** sits where it seems to describe the wrong noun or action. In *"The waiter served pasta to the customer covered in cheese,"* *covered in cheese* sits right after *the customer*, making the customer sound covered in cheese.

Move the modifier right next to the noun it describes: *"The waiter served the customer pasta covered in cheese."* Now *covered in cheese* follows *pasta* directly.

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: two stacked sentences: top "The waiter served pasta to the [customer] [covered in cheese]." with an arrow from "covered in cheese" pointing mistakenly to "customer"; bottom "The waiter served the customer [pasta] [covered in cheese]." with a teal arrow from "covered in cheese" pointing to "pasta" -->

:::pair #wrong-noun-attachment
Right: *"The waiter served the customer pasta covered in cheese."*
Wrong: Write *"The waiter served pasta to the customer covered in cheese."* or *"Covered in cheese, the waiter served pasta to the customer."*
After *customer*, *covered in cheese* attaches to the customer; at the front before *the waiter*, it attaches to the waiter. Put the phrase right beside *pasta*.
:::

:::check
The car, not the owner, has a dented door. Revise *"The mechanic showed a car to the owner with a dented door."*
:::answer
The answer is **"The mechanic showed the owner a car with a dented door."** Putting *with a dented door* right after *a car* connects the dented door to the car.
:::

### Keeping the Intended Meaning When Revising

A clear revision is still wrong if it moves the modifier onto the **wrong person or object** and changes the intended meaning.

If the student wore the jacket, compare *"The teacher, who wore a blue jacket, spoke to the student after class"* with *"The teacher spoke to the student wearing a blue jacket."* The first sentence is smooth, yet it puts the jacket on the teacher instead of the student.

The same rule holds for single-word modifiers like *broken*. When the window is damaged, *"A worker on a ladder repaired the broken window"* keeps *broken* on *window*, while *"A worker repaired the window on a broken ladder"* shifts the damage to the ladder.

:::why
Why can a smooth sentence still fail? Modifier questions test both clarity and faithful meaning. Attaching a clear phrase to the wrong noun tells a different story.
:::

:::pair #smooth-but-wrong-target
Right: *"The teacher spoke to the student wearing a blue jacket."*
Wrong: Choose *"The teacher wearing a blue jacket spoke to the student after class."* or *"The teacher, who wore a blue jacket, spoke to the student after class."* when the student wore the jacket.
Both wrong options are clear, yet they put the jacket on *the teacher*.
:::

:::check
Which clearly states that the mug, not the shelf, is cracked: *"Tom put the mug on the cracked shelf"* or *"Tom put the cracked mug on the shelf"*?
:::answer
The answer is **"Tom put the cracked mug on the shelf."** Putting *cracked* before *mug* keeps the damage on the mug.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Moving a Phrase Next to Its Noun

The book has a torn cover. Revise *"The librarian handed a book to the student with a torn cover"* clearly.

**Solution:**

1. Find the modifier: *with a torn cover*.
2. Notice it sits after *the student*, making the student seem to have a torn cover.
3. Move it right after *a book*: **"The librarian handed the student a book with a torn cover."**

**Check:** Read backward from *with a torn cover*: it lands directly on *a book*, matching the prompt.

### #2: Preserving Which Object Is Damaged

Which sentence identifies a broken window, not a broken ladder: *"On a broken ladder, a worker repaired the window outside the hall"* or *"A worker on a ladder repaired the broken window"*?

**Solution:**

1. In *"On a broken ladder, a worker repaired the window outside the hall,"* *broken* describes *ladder*.
2. In *"A worker on a ladder repaired the broken window,"* *broken* sits right before *window*.
3. The second sentence keeps the intended meaning.

**Check:** Drop *"on a ladder"* and read *"A worker repaired the broken window."* The damage stays on *window*.

### #3: Fixing Two Modifier Targets in One Passage

The visitors walk into the room, and the bread is wrapped in a napkin. Check this draft: *"Entering the room, we smelled fresh bread. We received bread from the baker wrapped in a napkin."* What works, what is misplaced, and how do you fix it?

**Solution:**

1. In sentence 1, *we* sits right after *"Entering the room,"* so the visitors are clearly named.
2. In sentence 2, *"wrapped in a napkin"* sits after *the baker*, attaching the napkin to the baker.

:::try
Finish the last step: rewrite sentence 2 so *"wrapped in a napkin"* clearly describes *bread*.
:::answer
**"Entering the room, we smelled fresh bread. We received bread wrapped in a napkin from the baker."** Putting *wrapped in a napkin* right after *bread* removes *the baker* from between them.

**Check:** Match each modifier to its noun: *"Entering the room, we..."* (people enter) and *"...bread wrapped in a napkin"* (bread is wrapped). Both fit the prompt.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Check two things for every modifier: what noun sits right beside it, and whether the prompt says that noun owns the detail.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Modifier beside the wrong noun: move it directly next to the noun it actually describes. {#wrong-noun-attachment}
- Smooth revision that shifts a detail onto another person or object: check the target against the prompt before picking it. {#smooth-but-wrong-target}
:::
