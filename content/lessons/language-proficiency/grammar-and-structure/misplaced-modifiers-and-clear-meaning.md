## Core Concept

### Placing a Modifier Beside Its True Target

A **modifier** is a word, phrase, or clause that adds detail about another part of a sentence.

A **misplaced modifier** sits where it seems to describe the wrong noun or action. In *"The waiter served pasta to the customer covered in cheese,"* *covered in cheese* sits right after *the customer*, making the customer sound covered in cheese.

Move the modifier right next to the noun it describes: *"The waiter served the customer pasta covered in cheese."*

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: two stacked sentences: top "The waiter served pasta to the [customer] [covered in cheese]." with an arrow from "covered in cheese" pointing mistakenly to "customer"; bottom "The waiter served the customer [pasta] [covered in cheese]." with a teal arrow from "covered in cheese" pointing to "pasta" -->

:::pair #wrong-noun-attachment
Right: *"The waiter served the customer pasta covered in cheese."*
Wrong: Write *"The waiter served pasta to the customer covered in cheese."* or *"Covered in cheese, the waiter served pasta to the customer."*
After *customer*, *covered in cheese* attaches to the customer; at the front before *the waiter*, it attaches to the waiter.
:::

:::check
The car, not the owner, has a dented door. Which sentence says so: *"The mechanic showed a car to the owner with a dented door"* or *"The mechanic showed the owner a car with a dented door"*?
:::answer
**"The mechanic showed the owner a car with a dented door."** *With a dented door* follows *a car*, so the dent belongs to the car.
:::

### Keeping the Intended Meaning When Revising

A clear revision is still wrong if it moves the modifier onto the **wrong person or object**. If the student wore the jacket, compare *"The teacher, who wore a blue jacket, spoke to the student after class"* with *"After class, the teacher spoke to the student wearing a blue jacket."*
The same rule holds for single-word modifiers like *broken*. When the window is damaged, *"A worker on a ladder repaired the broken window"* keeps *broken* on *window*, while *"A worker repaired the window on a broken ladder"* shifts the damage to the ladder.

:::why
Why can a smooth sentence still fail? Modifier questions test both clarity and faithful meaning. Attaching a clear phrase to the wrong noun tells a different story.
:::

:::pair #smooth-but-wrong-target
Right: *"After class, the teacher spoke to the student wearing a blue jacket."*
Wrong: Choose *"The teacher wearing a blue jacket spoke to the student after class."* or *"The teacher, who wore a blue jacket, spoke to the student after class."*
Both wrong options are clear, yet they put the jacket on *the teacher*.
:::

:::check
Which clearly states that the mug, not the shelf, is cracked: *"Tom put the mug on the cracked shelf"* or *"Tom put the cracked mug on the shelf"*?
:::answer
The answer is **"Tom put the cracked mug on the shelf."** Putting *cracked* before *mug* keeps the damage on the mug.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Moving a Phrase Next to Its Noun

The toy has a missing wheel. Revise *"The clerk gave a toy to the girl with a missing wheel"* clearly.

**Solution:**

1. Find the modifier: *with a missing wheel*.
2. Notice it sits after *the girl*, making the girl seem to have a missing wheel.
3. Keep the word order and move only the modifier right after *a toy*: **"The clerk gave a toy with a missing wheel to the girl."**

**Check:** Ask which noun now gets the detail. *A missing wheel* belongs to the toy, and the girl has none.

### #2: Preserving Which Object Is Damaged

Which sentence identifies a chipped glass, not a chipped tray: *"A waiter carried the glass on a chipped tray"* or *"A waiter carried the chipped glass on a tray"*?

**Solution:**

1. In *"A waiter carried the glass on a chipped tray,"* *chipped* describes *tray*.
2. The second sentence, with *chipped* right before *glass*, keeps the intended meaning: **"A waiter carried the chipped glass on a tray."**

**Check:** Ask which noun now gets the detail. *Chipped* belongs to the glass, and the tray is plain.

### #3: Fixing Two Modifier Targets in One Passage

The travelers wait at the station, and the snack is wrapped in foil. Check this draft: *"Waiting at the station, we heard a whistle. We bought a snack from the vendor wrapped in foil."* What works, what is misplaced, and how do you fix it?

**Solution:**

1. In sentence 1, *we* sits right after *"Waiting at the station,"* so the travelers are clearly named.
2. In sentence 2, *"wrapped in foil"* sits after *the vendor*, attaching the foil to the vendor.

:::try
Finish the last step: which rewrite of sentence 2 makes *"wrapped in foil"* describe *snack*: (A) *"Wrapped in foil, we bought a snack from the vendor."* or (B) *"We bought a snack wrapped in foil from the vendor."*?
:::answer
**B: "Waiting at the station, we heard a whistle. We bought a snack wrapped in foil from the vendor."** *Wrapped in foil* follows *snack*, with *the vendor* no longer between them. In A, the opener would describe *we*.

**Check:** Ask what is wrapped in foil: the snack, not the vendor. Ask what stayed the same: we still bought it from the vendor.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Ask what each modifier should describe.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Modifier beside the wrong noun: move it directly next to the noun it actually describes. {#wrong-noun-attachment}
- Smooth revision that shifts a detail onto another person or object: check the target against the prompt before picking it. {#smooth-but-wrong-target}
:::
