## Core Concept

### Commas After Opening Clauses and Phrases

A **dependent clause** cannot stand alone. Usually put a comma after an opening one that has its own subject and verb: *"Because she was tired, she rested."*

An **opening phrase** usually takes a comma after the whole phrase: *"After the meeting, we went home."* A short, clear opening (*"Yesterday we met"*) may skip it.

Put the opening comma at the **end** of the opening group, never inside it (*"After, the meeting"*) or between subject and verb (*"we, went home"*).

:::pair #splitting-opening-or-subject
Right: "After the meeting, we went home."
Wrong: "After, the meeting we went home." or "After the meeting we, went home."
*After, the meeting* splits the preposition from its noun, and *we, went* splits the subject from its verb. The opening comma belongs after the whole opening group.
:::

:::check
Where does the comma go in *"Before the proctor collected the sheets everyone stopped writing"*?
:::answer
The answer is **after "sheets"**. *"Before the proctor collected the sheets"* is an opening dependent clause, so the comma follows it.
:::

### Extra Information vs. Identifying Information

**Nonrestrictive (extra):** adds a side detail about a person or thing already identified. Use a word such as *who* or *which*, never *that*, with commas (on **both** sides mid-sentence).

**Restrictive (identifying):** tells **which** ones out of a group (words such as *who*, *which*, or *that*, **no commas**).

Compare extra detail with a clause that picks out some students:

1. *"My brother, who lives in Cebu, is visiting this week."*
2. *"The students who missed the test must report today."*

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: two stacked sentences comparing comma pairs: top "My brother, who lives in Cebu, is visiting." with "who lives in Cebu" shaded between two teal commas and labeled "only one brother -> extra info"; bottom "The students who missed the test must report." with no commas and a tight bracket from "who missed the test" to "The students" labeled "picks out some students -> identifying" -->

:::why
Why do commas change who is meant? With paired commas, you can remove the middle clause and still know who is meant. Without commas, the clause stays locked to the noun to narrow the group.
:::

:::pair #missing-commas-on-extra-info
Right: A writer has only one brother: "My brother, who lives in Cebu, is visiting this week."
Wrong: "My brother who lives in Cebu is visiting this week."
*Who lives in Cebu* is extra detail about an only brother, so it needs commas on both sides.
:::

:::pair #commas-on-identifying-clause
Right: Some students missed the test, and only they must report: "The students who missed the test must report today."
Wrong: "The students, who missed the test, must report today."
Commas around *who missed the test* make it extra detail about all the students. Leave them out when the clause narrows the group.
:::

:::check
The office owns four printers, and only one is broken. Should *"that is broken"* take commas in *"The printer that is broken sits near the door"*?
:::answer
The answer is **No**. *That is broken* tells which of the four printers is meant, so it takes no commas.
:::

## Examples

### #1: An Opening Dependent Clause

Add the needed punctuation: *"Because the office was closed we returned the next day."*

**Solution:**

1. Find the opening clause: *"Because the office was closed."*
2. Put one comma before the main clause *"we returned the next day."*

Correct sentence: **"Because the office was closed, we returned the next day."**

**Check:** Cover the opening clause; *"We returned the next day"* still stands alone.

### #2: Opening and Extra Information

The speaker has only one brother. Add commas: *"After the long meeting with the principal my brother who lives in Cebu drove me home."*

**Solution:**

1. Put a comma after the opening phrase: *"After the long meeting with the principal,"*
2. The speaker has only one brother, so *who lives in Cebu* is extra and takes commas on both sides.

Correct sentence: **"After the long meeting with the principal, my brother, who lives in Cebu, drove me home."**

**Check:** Lift out *"who lives in Cebu"*: *"After the long meeting with the principal, my brother drove me home."* The single brother is still clear.

### #3: Applicants and Folders

Only some applicants submitted incomplete folders, and only they must return on Monday. Choose the right sentence:

(A) "The applicants, who submitted incomplete folders, must return on Monday."

(B) "The applicants who submitted incomplete folders must return on Monday."

**Solution:**

1. Decide whether *who submitted incomplete folders* adds extra detail about all applicants or identifies which subset must return.

:::try
Finish the last step: choose (A) or (B), and explain how the comma choice matches the intended meaning.
:::answer
The answer is **(B) "The applicants who submitted incomplete folders must return on Monday."** Leaving out the commas locks the clause to *The applicants* so only that subset must return; the commas in (A) would claim every applicant submitted an incomplete folder.

**Check:** Remove *who submitted incomplete folders*: "The applicants must return on Monday" would mean all of them, so the clause is needed to identify the group.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Usually put one comma after an opening dependent clause, and after an opening phrase unless it is short and clear. For *who* or *which*, ask: narrows which one (no commas) or adds a side detail (commas)?

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- If an opener takes a comma: place it after the whole opening, never inside it or between the main subject and verb. {#splitting-opening-or-subject}
- Extra detail about a person or thing already identified: set it off with commas (both sides mid-sentence). {#missing-commas-on-extra-info}
- Identifying clause narrowing a group: omit commas so it does not claim all members. {#commas-on-identifying-clause}
:::
