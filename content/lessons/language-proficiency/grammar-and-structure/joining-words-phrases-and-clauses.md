## Core Concept

### Prepositions, Conjunctions, and Interjections

The remaining three classes in traditional school grammar connect words or express reactions.

- **Preposition**: introduces a noun or pronoun phrase to show a relation such as place, time, or direction (*under the table, before dinner, toward home*). In "The cat hid **under** the table," *under* links *the table* to *hid*.
- **Conjunction**: joins words, phrases, or clauses (*and, but, or, because, before*). A **coordinating conjunction** (*for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*) joins equal parts, such as two full clauses.
- **Interjection**: expresses a sudden feeling or reaction and stands apart from the sentence grammar (*Ouch!, Wow!, Hey!*). In "**Ouch!** That really hurt," *Ouch* shows pain on its own.

:::pair #location-phrase-adverb
Right: In "The cat hid under the table," *under* is a preposition.
Wrong: Label *under* an adverb because "under the table" tells where the cat hid.
The full phrase tells where, so test *under* by itself. It takes the noun phrase *the table*, so it is a preposition.
:::

:::pair #but-linking-clauses
Right: In "She wanted to attend the party, but she had too much homework," *but* is a conjunction.
Wrong: Label *but* a preposition or an adverb because it shows contrast.
Both groups around *but* have a subject and verb, so *but* joins two clauses as a conjunction.
:::

:::check
What part of speech is **Oops** in "**Oops!** I dropped my pen on the floor"?
:::answer
The answer is **Interjection**. *Oops* expresses a sudden reaction and stands independent of the clause that follows.
:::

### A Phrase or a Clause After a Joining Word

A **subject** tells who or what a clause is about. A **clause** has a subject and a verb (commands leave *you* understood). A **phrase** works as one part without a subject-verb pair.

A **subordinate clause** usually has its own subject and verb, yet cannot stand alone: *before we finished dinner* leaves you waiting. Some grammars also count *before leaving* (subject understood) as one; this lesson does not. A **subordinating conjunction** (such as *before, after, because, when, until*) introduces a subordinate clause.

Words like *before*, *after*, and *until* can do two jobs. Compare a noun phrase with a clause after *before*:

1. Preposition, noun phrase *dinner* with no verb: "We met **before** dinner."
2. Subordinating conjunction, clause with subject *we* and verb *finished*: "We left **before** we finished dinner."

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: two stacked sentences comparing "before": top "We met before [dinner]" with [dinner] bracketed and labeled "noun phrase -> preposition"; bottom "We left before [we finished dinner]" with "we" labeled "subject", "finished" labeled "verb", and [we finished dinner] labeled "clause -> subordinating conjunction" -->

:::why
Why does traditional school grammar place *before* in two classes? A preposition links a noun phrase to a sentence, while a conjunction links a whole clause. Reading past the first noun to check for a verb reveals which job it does.
:::

:::pair #phrase-or-clause
Right: In "We left before we finished dinner," *before* is a subordinating conjunction.
Wrong: Label *before* a preposition because the noun *dinner* appears after it.
Because *we* (subject) and *finished* (verb) follow *before*, it introduces a subordinate clause.
:::

:::check
In school grammar, what part of speech is **after** in "She called **after** the meeting ended"?
:::answer
The answer is **Subordinating conjunction**. *The meeting* (subject) and *ended* (verb) form a subordinate clause after *after*.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Interjection and Conjunction

Identify the parts of speech of **Wow** and **but**: "**Wow!** Ana solved the puzzle." "She wanted to attend, **but** she had homework."

**Solution:**

1. *Wow* stands apart and shows sudden surprise, so it is an **interjection**.
2. *She wanted to attend* and *she had homework* are both complete clauses.
3. *But* joins two equal clauses, so it is a **conjunction**.

**Check:** Drop *Wow!* and the sentence still works.

### #2: Preposition or Subordinating Conjunction

In traditional school grammar, identify **before** in each sentence: "We met **before** dinner." "We left **before** it rained."

**Solution:**

1. First, *dinner* follows *before* with no verb, so *before* is a **preposition**.
2. Second, *it* (subject) and *rained* (verb) follow *before*, forming a subordinate clause.
3. So *before* is a **subordinating conjunction**.

**Check:** Only "it rained" has a subject and verb.

### #3: Four Words in One Sentence

In school grammar, identify **(1) extremely**, **(2) diligent**, **(3) quietly**, and **(4) before** in: "The **extremely** **diligent** students **quietly** finished their difficult exam **before** the bell rang."

**Solution:**

1. *Extremely* modifies the adjective *diligent*, so **(1) extremely** is an **adverb**.
2. *Diligent* describes the noun *students*, so **(2) diligent** is an **adjective**.
3. *Quietly* modifies the verb *finished*, so **(3) quietly** is an **adverb**.

:::try
Finish the last step: is **(4) before** a preposition or a conjunction?
:::answer
The answer is **Conjunction** (a subordinating conjunction). *The bell* is a subject and *rang* is a verb, so *before the bell rang* is the subordinate clause.

**Check:** Stopping at *the bell* would miss the verb *rang*; reading the full group "before the bell rang" confirms a subject-verb clause.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Look past *before*, *after*, *since*, and *until*. In these sentences, a noun phrase alone signals a preposition; a subject and verb signal a subordinating conjunction. Alone (*seen it before*), *before* is an adverb.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- Place or time phrase like "under the table": classify the opening word as a preposition when a noun phrase follows it. {#location-phrase-adverb}
- Joining word like *but* between two clauses: classify it as a conjunction rather than a preposition or adverb. {#but-linking-clauses}
- *Before*, *after*, or *until* followed by a noun: keep reading for a verb before calling it a preposition. {#phrase-or-clause}
:::
