## Core Concept

### Alliteration

**Alliteration** repeats the same starting sound, usually a consonant, in nearby words, as in *busy bees buzzed*. Sound matters in alliteration, not just spelling.

Let's mark the repeated starting sound: **P**eter **P**iper **p**icked a **p**eck of **p**ickled **p**eppers.
:::pair #repeated-sound
Right: "Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers" is alliteration.
Wrong: "Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers" is onomatopoeia.
No word here imitates a noise. A repeated starting sound is alliteration.
:::

:::check
Which words in "Cold winds crept closer" repeat a starting sound?
:::answer
The answer is *cold*, *crept*, and *closer*. All three start with the *k* sound, so the line uses alliteration.
:::

### Onomatopoeia

**Onomatopoeia** uses a word that sounds like the sound it names, such as *buzz* or *clang*.

:::check
Which word in "Frogs croaked in the garden" imitates its noise?
:::answer
The answer is *croaked*. It sounds like the noise it names, so it is onomatopoeia.
:::

### Oxymoron

An **oxymoron** puts words with opposing meanings together, such as *jumbo shrimp*.

Which device comes from adjacent words that contradict each other in "deafening silence"?

:::pair #opposing-words
Right: "Deafening silence" is an oxymoron.
Wrong: "Deafening silence" is hyperbole.
The phrase joins two opposing words. It does not simply stretch the truth.
:::

:::check
Which two words in "Her cruel kindness surprised everyone" oppose each other?
:::answer
The answer is *cruel* and *kindness*. Joining opposing words makes an oxymoron.
:::

## Examples

### #1: The Snakes

“Silver snakes silently slid across the sand.” Which sound device is used most clearly?

A) Alliteration

B) Onomatopoeia

C) Metaphor

D) Oxymoron

**Solution:**

1. Mark the starting sound: **S**ilver **s**nakes **s**ilently **s**lid across the **s**and.
2. Repeating a starting consonant sound is alliteration. Answer: **A**.

**Check:** Five of the seven words start with the *s* sound.

### #2: The Firecracker

“The firecracker went **bang** beside the gate.” Which device is shown by the word in bold?

A) Onomatopoeia

B) Simile

C) Metaphor

D) Oxymoron

**Solution:**

1. Say **bang** aloud. It imitates the sudden noise it names.
2. A word that imitates a sound is onomatopoeia, so "The firecracker went bang beside the gate" uses it. Answer: **A**.

**Check:** Replace *bang* with *happened*: no noise is imitated, so the device is gone.

### #3: The Meeting

“The meeting ended in **organized chaos**.” Which literary device is created by the phrase in bold?

A) Personification

B) Simile

C) Oxymoron

D) Onomatopoeia

**Solution:**

1. Name the meaning of each word in the phrase.

:::try
Finish the last step: compare the two meanings and choose the device.
:::answer
*Organized* means arranged in order, and *chaos* means complete disorder. These meanings oppose each other, so “organized chaos” is an oxymoron. The answer is **C**.

**Check:** Nothing can be organized and in complete disorder at once, so the two words clash.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

**Check the effect.** Ask whether the words compare things, give a human quality, stretch the truth, repeat a sound, or join opposing meanings.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- A repeated starting sound: alliteration, not onomatopoeia. {#repeated-sound}
- Two opposing words together: oxymoron, not hyperbole. {#opposing-words}
- A sound word such as *clang* or *bang*: check if the word imitates a noise, which is onomatopoeia. {#onomatopoeia}
:::
