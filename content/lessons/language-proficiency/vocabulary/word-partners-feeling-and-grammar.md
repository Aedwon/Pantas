## Core Concept

When two choices still seem possible, check smaller clues:

### Word Partners and Feeling

- **Word partners:** some words often appear together. In *file a complaint*, *file* means submit, not smooth a surface.
- **Connotation:** a word can carry a feeling beyond its basic meaning. *Slim* and *scrawny* both suggest being thin, but *scrawny* suggests being too thin.

Here is *charged* with its partner.

- *Charged the guest for an extra night*: the partner is a fee.
- A fee points to *billed*.

:::pair #charged-partner
Right: The hotel charged the guest for an extra night. Here *charged* means billed.
Wrong: The hotel charged the guest for an extra night. Here *charged* means accused.
The partner words *extra night* and *fee* point to billing. *Accused* needs a crime.
:::

:::check
In "file a complaint," does *file* mean submit or smooth?
:::answer
The answer is submit. The partner *complaint* is something you hand in.
:::

### Register and Grammar

- **Register:** wording can be formal or casual. *Request* sounds more formal than *ask for*. An official letter may help you recognize a formal meaning, but formality alone cannot prove it.
- **Grammar:** check the word's job. Does the sentence need a thing, an action, or a describing word?

First guess the meaning from the sentence. Then put a possible answer in place of the target word. It must keep the meaning and fit the grammar.

:::pair #furnish-grammar
Right: Please provide proof of residence.
Wrong: Please furniture proof of residence.
After *please*, the sentence needs a verb. *Furniture* is a noun.
:::

Here is a feeling that is not the whole meaning: *qualified support* in "they favored it only if drainage repairs were completed first."

:::pair #qualified-meaning
Right: *Qualified support* means limited support.
Wrong: *Qualified support* means certified support.
The word *only if* shows a limit. *Certified* is a different meaning of *qualified*.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Formal Wording

The official letter asked the applicant to **furnish** proof of residence before the office could process the request. What does the word in bold most likely mean?

A) decorate  
B) inspect  
C) provide  
D) withhold

**Solution:**

1. *Furnish* can mean put furniture in a room or provide something.
2. Here, its partner is *proof of residence*.
3. The office needs the applicant to provide a document. This use fits the formal letter.
4. Put C back: “The official letter asked the applicant to provide proof of residence.”
5. *Provide* fits after *to* and keeps the meaning. Answer: **C**.

**Check:** A, B, and D fail. *Decorate* needs a room, *inspect* changes the action, and *withhold* means the opposite.

:::try
In "The notice asked residents to **furnish** a valid ID," which word can replace *furnish*: *decorate* or *provide*?
:::answer
The answer is *provide*. The partner *a valid ID* is a document that someone hands over.

**Check:** "Asked residents to provide a valid ID" keeps the meaning and the grammar.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

**Put the choice back.** Check the full sentence for meaning and grammar. For a noun or describing word, keep that same job when replacing it.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- A word with a partner: let the partner pick the meaning, as *extra night* picks *billed*. {#charged-partner}
- A replacement word: keep the same job, such as a verb for a verb. {#furnish-grammar}
- A word with more than one meaning: use the detail around it, as *only if* picks *limited*. {#qualified-meaning}
- Formality alone: it helps, but the meaning must fit what the sentence says.
:::
