## Core Concept

### Comparisons With and Without Like or As

A **figure of speech** uses words to create a comparison, image, sound, or strong effect. A line can show more than one figure. Look for the clearest effect supported by the wording.

- **Simile:** compares different things, often with *like* or *as*: “Her voice was like velvet.”
- **Metaphor:** presents one thing as another to make a comparison: “Time is a thief.”

A simile needs a comparison. *Like* or *as* alone is not enough.

We test this on a line that has no *like* or *as*.

- “The classroom was a zoo.”
- The classroom is called a zoo directly, with no *like* or *as*.
- So it is a metaphor.

:::pair #metaphor-or-simile
Right: “The classroom was a zoo” is a metaphor, because it says one thing is another.
Wrong: “The classroom was a zoo” is a simile.
A simile usually compares with a marker such as *like* or *as*. This line calls the classroom a zoo directly.
:::

:::check
“The sky was a blue blanket” and “Her hands were like ice.” Which one is a simile?
:::answer
The answer is “Her hands were like ice.” It compares two different things with *like*. “The sky was a blue blanket” calls the sky a blanket directly, so it is a metaphor.
:::

### Two More Figures

- **Personification:** gives a human action or quality to something nonhuman: “Opportunity knocked.”
- **Hyperbole:** makes something seem much greater or smaller than it really is: “I've told you a million times.”

Now we test a line that has a human action.

- “The floorboards groaned, as if complaining.”
- The boards are given a human action, complaining.
- So it is personification.

:::pair #as-if-personification
Right: “The boards groaned, as if complaining” gives the boards the ability to complain, so it is personification.
Wrong: “The boards groaned, as if complaining” gives the boards the ability to complain through an explicit comparison.
Ask what the boards are made to do.
:::

:::check
“I could eat a horse.” Which figure is this?
:::answer
The answer is hyperbole. Eating a horse greatly overstates how hungry the speaker is.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Personification

“The wind whispered through the dark forest.” Which literary device is used most clearly?

A) Metaphor

B) Hyperbole

C) Personification

D) Simile

**Solution:**

1. Whispering is a human action, and the wind cannot speak.
2. Giving it to the wind creates personification. Answer: **C**: “The wind whispered through the dark forest.”

**Check:** Swap *whispered* for *blew softly*: no human action is left, so no personification.

### #2: Metaphor

“Hope is a bird that carries us beyond our fear.” Which literary device is used most clearly?

A) Metaphor

B) Simile

C) Alliteration

D) Onomatopoeia

**Solution:**

1. The line presents hope as a bird, directly.
2. So it is a metaphor. Answer: **A**: “Hope is a bird that carries us beyond our fear.”

**Check:** Add *like*: "Hope is like a bird" would be a simile.

### #3: The Moonlit Lake

“The calm lake shone like a mirror under the moon.” Which literary device is used most clearly?

A) Simile

B) Metaphor

C) Oxymoron

D) Personification

**Solution:**

1. The sentence sets a calm lake beside a mirror.

:::try
Finish the last step: choose the device.
:::answer
The answer is **Simile**, choice **A**. The marker *like* joins the lake and the mirror.

**Check:** Read it back: "The calm lake shone like a mirror under the moon." Rewriting it as "The calm lake was a mirror under the moon" would make it a metaphor.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

**Use signal words carefully.** *Like* and *as* may introduce comparisons, but they also have ordinary uses.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- A line that calls one thing another in a nonliteral way: it is a metaphor, even with no *like* or *as*. {#metaphor-or-simile}
- A line with *as if* that gives a nonhuman thing a human quality or action: it is personification. {#as-if-personification}
- *Like* or *as* alone: check for a comparison of different things before you say simile. {#comparisons-with-and-without-like-or-as}
:::
