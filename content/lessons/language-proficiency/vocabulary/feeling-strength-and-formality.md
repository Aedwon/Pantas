## Core Concept

### Connotation

**Connotation** is the feeling a word carries beyond its basic meaning. *Slim* and *skinny* both describe thinness, but *slim* often sounds more positive.

We choose between two words for careful spending.

- Sentence: “Her careful spending left enough money for essentials and for helping neighbors.”
- Helping neighbors is good, so the sentence approves.
- *Frugal* means careful with money, and it sounds approving.
- *Stingy* means unwilling to spend or give, and it sounds critical.
- So *frugal* keeps the tone: “Her frugal spending left enough money for essentials and for helping neighbors.”

:::pair #approving-tone
Right: She was frugal.
Wrong: She was stingy.
*Stingy* sounds critical, but the sentence approves.
:::

:::check
Her teacher admired her *(determined / stubborn)* effort. Which word keeps the positive tone?
:::answer
The answer is *determined*. *Admired* shows approval, *determined* sounds positive, and *stubborn* sounds critical.
:::

### Intensity

**Intensity** is the strength of a meaning. *Annoyed* means bothered or a little angry.

We replace *annoyed* in this sentence: “The clerk was annoyed by a minor interruption but resumed work calmly.”

- The clerk resumed work calmly, so the anger is mild.
- *Bothered* is mild, and *enraged* is extreme anger.
- So *bothered* fits: “The clerk was bothered by a minor interruption but resumed work calmly.”

:::pair #mild-intensity
Right: The clerk was bothered by a minor interruption.
Wrong: The clerk was enraged by a minor interruption.
*Enraged* is too strong for a clerk who resumed work calmly.
:::

:::check
The room felt slightly cool. Which fits better: *chilly* or *freezing*?
:::answer
The answer is *chilly*. It matches a mild feeling, while *freezing* means extreme cold.
:::

### Register

**Register** is the level of formality that fits a situation. *Commence* is formal, *start* is neutral, and *kick off* is casual.

Ask who will read the sentence. Then choose the word whose meaning and setting both fit.

We replace *kick off* in a public notice: “Processing will kick off after the briefing.”

- A public notice is formal.
- *Commence* is formal, and *get going* is casual.
- So *commence* fits: “Processing will commence after the briefing.”

:::pair #formal-register
Right: Processing will commence after the briefing.
Wrong: Processing will get going after the briefing.
A public notice calls for formal wording. *Get going* is too casual.
:::

:::check
“Staff may ___ supplies.” Both words mean the same. Which fits a formal notice: *purchase* or *buy*?
:::answer
The answer is *purchase*. “Staff may purchase supplies” is formal. *Buy* is neutral.
:::

## Examples

### #1: Matching Formality

Which word or phrase best completes this formal notice?

“The examination will _____ at 8:00 a.m.”

A) commence

B) kick off

C) get going

D) fire up

**Solution:**

1. A formal notice needs official wording.
2. **Commence** means begin and is formal. The answer is **A**: “The examination will commence at 8:00 a.m.”

**Check:** “The examination will kick off at 8:00 a.m.” is too casual for the notice.

### #2: Keeping a Positive Tone

The article praised the athlete's **slim** build. Which replacement best keeps the positive tone of the word in bold?

A) skinny

B) slender

C) frail

D) bony

**Solution:**

1. *Praised* shows a positive tone.
2. **Slender** describes a thin build with a positive tone. The answer is **B**: “The article praised the athlete's slender build.”

**Check:** “The article praised the athlete's skinny build” sounds critical, so *skinny* fails.

### #3: Before the Quiz

Marco was **nervous** before the quiz, but he still smiled and joked with friends. Which replacement best keeps the strength of the word in bold?

A) terrified

B) uneasy

C) cheerful

D) alarmed

**Solution:**

1. Read the whole sentence, not only the bold word.

:::try
Finish the last step: choose the replacement that keeps the strength of **nervous**.
:::answer
The answer is **B**, **uneasy**. Marco smiled and joked in the same moment, so the fear was mild. *Uneasy* is mild, *terrified* and *alarmed* are stronger, and *cheerful* is not fear.

**Check:** Put *uneasy* back: “Marco was uneasy before the quiz, but he still smiled and joked with friends.” Someone terrified would hardly joke, so mild fear fits.
:::

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

Put the choice in place of the target word. Check meaning, grammar, feeling, strength, and formality.

## Traps to Watch For

:::checklist
- An approving sentence: pick the approving word, such as *frugal*, not *stingy*. {#approving-tone}
- A mild feeling: pick a mild word such as *bothered*, not *enraged*. {#mild-intensity}
- A formal notice: pick a formal word such as *commence*, not *get going*. {#formal-register}
:::
