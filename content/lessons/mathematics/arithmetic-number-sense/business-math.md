## Core Concept

Business math uses percentages and rates to describe changes in money. Two common applications are discounts and markups, and simple and compound interest.

### Discounts and Markups

A **discount** is an amount taken off a listed price. To find the discount amount, multiply the original price by the discount rate.

A **markup** is an amount added to an item's cost to set or help set its selling price. A markup is not automatically the same as profit because a business may have other costs and expenses.

The **Original Price** is the starting listed price before a discount. The **Selling Price** is the price after the stated discount or markup calculation.

### Simple and Compound Interest

**Interest** is money paid for the use of money. A borrower may pay interest, while a saver or investor may earn interest.

The **Principal** is the starting amount. The **Rate** is the interest rate for a stated time period.

**Simple Interest** is calculated only on the original principal. When the rate is annual, use:

$$I = PRT$$

Here $I$ is the interest, $P$ is the principal, $R$ is the annual interest rate written as a decimal, and $T$ is the time in years.

**Compound Interest** adds interest to the balance so later interest can be earned or charged on earlier interest. If $r$ is the rate per compounding period and $t$ is the number of compounding periods, the total amount $A$ is:

$$A = P(1 + r)^t$$

When a problem gives an annual rate but compounds more than once a year, convert the annual rate to a per-period rate and count the total number of compounding periods.

## Mnemonics & Memory Aids

For simple interest, remember:

$$I = PRT$$

Interest equals Principal times Rate times Time.

For sale prices, it can be faster to find the percentage that remains. If a shirt has a $20\%$ discount, then $80\%$ of the original price remains to be paid.

## Examples

### #1: Basic Discount

A pair of shoes originally priced at Php $1,500$ is on sale for $20\%$ off. What is the final selling price of the shoes?

**Solution:**

1. Find the percentage you actually have to pay. The whole price is $100\%$.
2. Subtract the discount:

$$100\% - 20\% = 80\%$$

3. Convert $80\%$ to $0.80$ and multiply by the original price.

$$1500 \times 0.80 = 1200$$

4. The final selling price is Php $1,200$.

### #2: Successive Discounts

A laptop listed at Php $30,000$ is given successive trade discounts of $20\%$ and $10\%$. What is the net price of the laptop?

**Solution:**

1. Apply the discounts one at a time.
2. After the first $20\%$ discount, the price is:

$$30000 \times 0.80 = 24000$$

3. Apply the $10\%$ discount to the new price:

$$24000 \times 0.90 = 21600$$

4. The net price is Php $21,600$.

### #3: Markup Based on Cost

A retailer buys a gadget for Php $1,200$ and applies a markup of $25\%$ based on cost. At what price is the gadget listed for sale?

**Solution:**

1. Find the markup amount.

$$1200 \times 0.25 = 300$$

2. Add the markup to the cost.

$$1200 + 300 = 1500$$

3. The listed selling price is Php $1,500$.

### #4: Finding Simple Interest Time

How many years will it take for an investment of Php $15,000$ to earn Php $4,500$ in simple interest at $6\%$ per annum?

**Solution:**

1. Use the simple-interest formula.

$$I = PRT$$

2. Substitute the given values.

$$4500 = 15000 \times 0.06 \times T$$

3. Simplify.

$$4500 = 900T$$

4. Divide by $900$.

$$T = 5$$

5. It will take $5$ years.

### #5: Compound Interest

An amount of Php $10,000$ is deposited into a savings account that yields an annual interest rate of $4\%$ compounded semi-annually. What is the total amount in the account after $2$ years? (Assume $1.02^4 \approx 1.0824$.)

**Solution:**

1. Semi-annual compounding means two compounding periods per year.
2. The rate per period is:

$$4\% \div 2 = 2\%$$

3. There are four periods in two years:

$$2 \times 2 = 4$$

4. Substitute into the compound-interest formula.

$$A = 10000(1 + 0.02)^4$$

5. Use the approximation given in the question.

$$A = 10000 \times 1.0824$$

6. The total amount is approximately Php $10,824$.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For **compound growth**, the **Rule of 72** gives a rough doubling-time estimate: divide $72$ by the annual percentage rate. For example, at an annual compound growth rate of about $8\%$, the estimated doubling time is $72 \div 8 = 9$ years. This is an approximation, not the simple-interest formula.

For successive discounts of $a\%$ and $b\%$, the equivalent single discount is:

$$\text{Total discount} = a + b - \dfrac{ab}{100}$$

For $20\%$ and $10\%$, the equivalent discount is $20 + 10 - 2 = 28\%$.

## Traps to Watch For

### #1: Adding Successive Discounts

> Do not add successive discount percentages directly.

A $20\%$ discount followed by another $20\%$ discount is not a $40\%$ discount because the second discount is applied to the already-reduced price.

### #2: Mismatching the Time Unit and Rate

> The time unit must match the period used by the interest rate.

If the simple-interest rate is given per annum and the time is $6$ months, use $0.5$ year for $T$. If a rate were stated for some other period, convert the time consistently.

### #3: Missing the Compounding Frequency

> Check how often compound interest is applied.

If interest is compounded quarterly, there are four compounding periods per year. Convert the annual rate to a quarterly rate and count four periods for each year.
