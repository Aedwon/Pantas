## Core Concept

A **conditional statement** is an if-then sentence: "If $p$, then $q$." The letters stand for statements that can be true or false.

The **hypothesis** ($p$) is the "if" part. The **conclusion** ($q$) is the "then" part. A conditional rules out a true hypothesis with a false conclusion.

The words **only if** also form a conditional. "$p$ only if $q$" means "If $p$, then $q$." The part after "only if" is the conclusion.

For example, "A figure is a square only if it has $4$ sides" means "If a figure is a square, then it has $4$ sides." It does not say that every figure with $4$ sides is a square.

## Examples

### #1: Naming the Parts

Find the hypothesis and conclusion: "If a number is divisible by $6$, then it is divisible by $3$."

**Solution:**

1. The hypothesis follows "if": a number is divisible by $6$.
2. The conclusion follows "then": it is divisible by $3$.
3. The conditional is true. A multiple of $6$ is also a multiple of $3$ because $6=3\times2$.

### #2: Reading Only If

An office follows this rule: "An applicant may enter the records room only if the applicant has a visitor pass." Rewrite the rule as an if-then statement. Does having a visitor pass alone prove that an applicant may enter?

<!-- ILLUSTRATION: One-way arrow from applicant may enter the records room to applicant has a visitor pass; label the first statement hypothesis and the second conclusion. -->

**Solution:**

1. The part after "only if" is the conclusion: the applicant has a visitor pass.
2. The rule means "If an applicant may enter the records room, then the applicant has a visitor pass."
3. Having a visitor pass does not prove that the applicant may enter. That would use the converse, which the rule does not establish.
4. The rule does show that an applicant without a visitor pass may not enter. This is the contrapositive.

## Speed Tricks & Shortcuts

For "only if," put the part after those words after "then." Do not reverse the direction.
